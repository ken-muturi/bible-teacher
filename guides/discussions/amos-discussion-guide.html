AMOS

The Prophets  ·  Book 30 of 66
Justice that rolls like waters — the prophet of social conscience
A shepherd from the backwater town of Tekoa walks into the prosperous northern kingdom of Israel and delivers God's verdict: religious observance without justice is not worship — it is an offence.
Before You Engage
Have you ever been in a community or organisation that looked successful on the outside — good numbers, confident leadership, the right rituals — but something underneath felt wrong? What gave it away?
When someone with no institutional power speaks an uncomfortable truth to a powerful group, what usually happens to them? What makes the rare exceptions different?
Think of a time you benefited from a system that worked well for you but poorly for someone else. How easy or hard was it to see it at the time?
Key Terms
Day of the Lord Israel expected a coming day when God would vindicate them and defeat their enemies. Amos reverses it: that day will be darkness, not light — judgment begins with God's own people.
Covenant The binding agreement between God and Israel — not just a religious arrangement but a social contract. Oppressing the poor is not a political failure; it is a covenant violation.
Oracles against the Nations Speeches condemning surrounding peoples for their crimes. Amos opens with six of them — and then, in a rhetorical trap, turns the same formula on Israel itself.
Social Justice In Amos, this means fair courts, honest commerce, and protection of the poor — not an abstract ideal but a measurable test of whether the covenant is real.
Remnant A surviving portion — in Amos, a small, chastened group that passes through judgment. The idea holds open a future without softening the present verdict.
Discussion Questions
Observation
Amos opens with oracles against Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, and Moab — nations Israel would have heard condemned with satisfaction. Then he turns to Judah, and then to Israel itself, using the exact same formula. What effect does this rhetorical sequence create, and what does it reveal about how Amos expects his audience to listen?
Observation
In chapter 7, Amaziah the priest tells Amos to go home and prophesy in Judah where he belongs. Amos replies: "I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son — I was a shepherd and a dresser of sycamore figs." What does Amos's insistence on his outsider credentials tell us about the authority he believes he has, and where it comes from?
Context
Amos 4:1 addresses the "cows of Bashan" — wealthy women of Samaria who are pressing their husbands for more wine while the poor are crushed. In the ancient Near East, Bashan cattle were a symbol of pampered prosperity. How does Amos use economic imagery to make a theological point, and is that kind of language effective or does it just alienate the people it targets?
Context
Amos 5:21–24 records God saying "I hate, I despise your feasts" and rejecting Israel's offerings, songs, and assemblies — despite these being practices God himself commanded. What does Amos say has made legitimate worship unacceptable? And what would it take, in his view, to make it acceptable again?
Application
The plumb line in chapter 7 is a builder's tool — it shows whether a wall is truly vertical, not whether it looks fine from a distance. If justice is Amos's plumb line for measuring a community's health, what would you put the plumb line against in your own context — your church, your workplace, your city? What might go out of true?
Application
Amos condemns Israel at its most prosperous — a time of military success, expanding borders, and thriving commerce (the reign of Jeroboam II). Prosperity, in Amos, doesn't signal divine favour; it can mask systemic injustice. How do we tell the difference between genuine flourishing and prosperity that has simply pushed its costs somewhere less visible?
Application
The book ends in 9:11–15 with an unexpected promise of restoration — vineyards replanted, cities rebuilt, the poor lifted up. It arrives without any resolution of the preceding judgment. Does a hope-filled ending make the hard words easier to receive, or does it risk undercutting the urgency of the critique? What do you do with a book that holds both without resolving the tension?
Going Deeper
Book
Gary V. Smith, Amos: A Commentary (Mentor) — covers historical background, the Hebrew text, and the social ethics driving each oracle
Resource
The Bible Project's Amos video and study notes (thebibleproject.com) — a visual introduction to the book's structure, themes, and rhetorical design
Read Alongside
Isaiah 1:10–17 — the same indictment of hollow worship from a different prophet; compare how each frames the relationship between sacrifice and justice
Closing Thought
Amos establishes once for all that Israel's God cannot be kept in the sanctuary while the poor are sold for a pair of sandals in the street — justice is not the application of faith, it is the test of whether faith is real.
