EZEKIEL

The Prophets  ·  Book 26 of 66
The glory that leaves — and comes back: God's presence in a ruined world
A priest deported to Babylon becomes a watchman to the exiles. Through bizarre visions and stranger street-theatre, he announces that Jerusalem will fall because God's glory has left it — and then promises that the same glory will return, a valley of dead bones will live, and a river will flow from a new temple to heal the world.
Before You Engage
Have you ever stayed loyal to an institution or relationship long after the thing that made it worth belonging to had quietly drained away? What finally made the absence undeniable?
When you need people to grasp something they're determined not to hear, words often fail. Have you ever seen someone get a hard truth across by doing something strange rather than saying it?
Is there any situation in your life right now that feels like a "valley of dry bones" — so far gone that recovery seems not just unlikely but physically impossible? What would it take for you to believe it could live again?
Key Terms
Glory of the LORD (kavod) God's visible, weighty presence. Ezekiel's spine: the glory enters in ch.1, leaves the temple in ch.10, and returns to a new temple in ch.43. The whole book tracks where God's presence is.
Son of Man (ben adam) God's name for Ezekiel — "mortal," "son of dust" — used ~93 times. It keeps the prophet small before the overwhelming glory. Jesus later takes the title and fills it with new meaning.
Sign-Act Prophecy performed, not just spoken. Ezekiel lies bound for months, eats rationed food, shaves his head, packs an exile's bag — his own body becomes the message Israel won't hear in words.
"You will know that I am the LORD" The recognition formula — repeated ~70 times. The goal of both judgment and rescue is the same: that people would truly know who God is. Knowing God, not mere survival, is the point.
New Heart / New Spirit God's promise (36:26) to remove the "heart of stone" and give a "heart of flesh," putting his own Spirit within. The internal transformation the New Testament ties to being born again.
Watchman Ezekiel's job description (ch.3, 33): a lookout responsible to sound the alarm. If he stays silent, the people's blood is on his hands — a sober model of accountable truth-telling.
Discussion Questions
Observation
In chapters 8–11 Ezekiel is given a vision-tour of the corrupt worship happening inside the temple, and then watches the glory of God rise from the holy place, pause at the threshold, move to the east gate, and finally leave the city entirely. Why do you think the departure is shown as slow and reluctant — in stages — rather than all at once? What might that pacing be communicating about God?
Observation
In the valley of dry bones (ch.37), God asks Ezekiel, "Can these bones live?" and Ezekiel answers, "Sovereign LORD, you alone know." Then the bones come together, get flesh, but remain dead until the breath (ruach) enters them. Why is the vision staged in two distinct steps — assembly first, then breath — and what does that two-stage process suggest about what real restoration requires?
Context
Ezekiel's first audience were exiles who believed Jerusalem and its temple were untouchable because God lived there. For chapters on end Ezekiel insists the city will fall — and then, the moment news arrives that it has (ch.33), his message flips almost entirely to hope. Why would God withhold the comfort until after the disaster rather than before? What does that timing reveal about the difference between false hope and real hope?
Context
In chapter 18 Ezekiel confronts a proverb the exiles were repeating — "The parents eat sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" — their way of saying they were being punished for their ancestors' sins. God's reply insists each person is accountable for their own choices. What was attractive about the proverb to people in exile, and where do we hear modern versions of the same move today?
Application
God tells Ezekiel that even when no one listens, "they will know that a prophet has been among them" (2:5). His faithfulness is measured by his obedience, not his results. Where in your own life have you assumed that the value of doing the right thing depends on whether it "works" or whether people respond? How would it change things to be responsible only for the speaking, not the outcome?
Application
The promise of chapter 36 is not that God will help people try harder, but that he will replace the heart of stone with a heart of flesh and put his Spirit inside them. Is there an area where you've been relying on willpower to change something that may actually require a change of heart you can't manufacture? What's the difference, practically, between behaviour modification and a new heart?
Application
Ezekiel ends with a river flowing from the temple that gets deeper the farther it goes and turns even the Dead Sea fresh and full of life (ch.47), and the city's final name is "The LORD Is There" (48:35). After 47 chapters that include some of the most disturbing imagery in the Bible, why do you think the book lands on presence as its last word — and what would it mean for a community today to be defined not by its programs or success but simply by "the LORD is there"?

Read Alongside
Revelation 4 and 21–22 — John's throne vision and the river of life draw directly on Ezekiel; compare how each pictures God's presence with his people
Closing Thought
Ezekiel watches the glory of God walk out of a compromised temple — and stakes everything on the promise that it will walk back into a place made new. The book's last word is not judgment but an address: "The LORD Is There."
