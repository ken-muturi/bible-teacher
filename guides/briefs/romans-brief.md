# ROMANS

> Series 8: Paul's Letters · Book 45 of 66 · The most systematic case for the gospel ever written

---

## The Thesis

> "For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith."
>
> — Romans 1:16–17 (ESV) — Everything else argues from here

---

## Movement 1 — The Problem: All Are Under Judgment (Chapters 1–3)

- Gentiles are without excuse — creation itself witnesses to God (1:18–32)
- Jews are also without excuse — knowing the law and breaking it is worse, not better (2:1–3:8)
- The hammer: "None is righteous, no, not one." (3:10) — Jew and Gentile equally condemned

Paul's rhetorical trap: he builds a case against Gentile sin (his Jewish reader nods along), then pivots without warning — "therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges." (2:1)

---

## Movement 2 — The Solution: Justification by Faith, Then Life in the Spirit (Chapters 3–8)

- God's righteousness is given, not earned — through faith in Christ (3:21–26)
- Abraham as the paradigm: credited as righteous before circumcision, before the law existed (ch. 4)
- Adam brought death to all; Christ brings life to all who receive it — the two humanities (ch. 5)
- Does grace mean sin doesn't matter? Emphatically not — we died to it (ch. 6)
- The law is good but cannot fix the problem — it shows sin without removing it (ch. 7)
- The Spirit accomplishes what the law could not — no condemnation, adoption, future glory (ch. 8)

Chapter 8 is the crescendo. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." (8:1) — and it ends with one of the most durable paragraphs in Scripture (8:38–39).

---

## Movement 3 — The Hard Question: What About Israel? (Chapters 9–11)

- If the gospel is true, has God abandoned his promises to Israel? Paul's answer: no — but the story isn't finished
- God's sovereignty in election: the potter and the clay (ch. 9) — does not eliminate human responsibility
- Israel stumbled but did not fall — a remnant remains; Gentile inclusion was always in the plan (chs. 10–11)
- "All Israel will be saved" (11:26) — one of the most debated lines in Paul; at minimum, the story of Israel is not over

Paul ends chs. 9–11 not with a tidy answer but with doxology: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments..." (11:33). He reaches mystery, not resolution.

---

## Movement 4 — The Practice: How the Justified Actually Live (Chapters 12–16)

- Living sacrifice — the whole of life as response to the gospel (12:1–2)
- The body with many members; gifts used for others, not self-display (12:3–8)
- Governing authorities, paying taxes — the justified live as responsible citizens (ch. 13)
- The strong and the weak — don't use your freedom to destroy someone else's conscience (chs. 14–15)
- Personal greetings: 26 people named — the letter lands in a real community with real tensions

Romans 12:1–2 is the hinge between theology and practice — "Therefore" connects everything Paul argued in chs. 1–11 to everything he asks in chs. 12–16. The ethics are the logic of the gospel, not an addition to it.

---

## Key Passage — Read Aloud

> "For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."
>
> — Romans 8:38–39 (ESV)

## Verdict

> The gospel is not primarily a self-improvement program. It is the announcement that God has done what no one could do for themselves — and the only appropriate response is a life given away in return.
>
> — The theological backbone of the New Testament

## Context

**Why Paul Wrote This**
Paul had not yet visited Rome. He was writing to introduce himself and his gospel before arriving — and to address real tensions between Jewish and Gentile believers in the Roman church.

**Author / Date**
Paul of Tarsus · ~57 AD · written from Corinth · authorship universally accepted

---

## Common Misread

Romans is not a timeless systematic theology dropped from heaven. It is a letter, addressed to a specific community with specific tensions. Reading it as abstract doctrine instead of pastoral argument flattens it.

---

## Key Words

**δικαιοσύνη / Dikaiosyne** *(Righteousness)*
Used for both God's righteous character and the status given to the believer. Same root as "justification" (dikaiōsis). ESV distinguishes them; NLT often uses "right relationship."

**πίστις / Pistis** *(Faith)*
"Trust / faithfulness / reliance." Not intellectual assent — active reliance on what God has done. Some scholars argue "faithfulness of Christ" (not "faith in Christ") in key passages. [VERIFY: genitive debate in 3:22]

**χάρις / Charis** *(Grace)*
"Unearned favor." In Roman culture, charis was the language of patron-client relationships — gift given freely, response expected. Paul's audience would have heard commercial overtones.

**νόμος / Nomos** *(Law)*
Refers variously to the Mosaic law, a general principle, and the Pentateuch. Context determines which — a distinction English translations often lose.

## Cross-Bible Links

- **← OT** Hab 2:4 — "the righteous shall live by faith" — Paul's thesis text (1:17)
- **← OT** Gen 15:6 — Abraham's faith credited as righteousness (Romans 4)
- **← OT** Isaiah 52–53 — Paul's mission logic draws on the servant passages
- **≈** Galatians — same core argument, shorter, angrier
- **≈** James 2 — "faith without works is dead" — appears to contradict Romans; the two are answering different questions

## Then → Now

**The Reversal**
Romans insists the human problem is deeper than behavior — it is a broken relationship with God that produces broken behavior. Self-improvement programs don't reach the root. That's not pessimism; it's what makes the solution as large as it is.

---

## Written

~57 AD · Corinth · to a church Paul had not yet visited · the longest of his letters

## Question to Sit With

Romans argues the human problem is not primarily behavioral but relational — a broken standing before God. If that's true, what changes about how you think about self-improvement, guilt, and forgiveness?
