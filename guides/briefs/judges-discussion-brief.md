*A spiral, not a cycle — and what happens when everyone does what is right in their own eyes*

## Before You Watch

- Think of a community — a family, team, organization — that started well and gradually fell apart. What was the first sign something had shifted?

- When someone in a position of power lets you down, what do you find harder: the failure itself, or the fact that they seemed not to notice?

- Is there a difference between doing what feels right and doing what is right? How would you tell them apart in practice?

## Key Terms

**Shofet (Judge)**
Not a courtroom judge — closer to a regional warlord or deliverer. Temporary, local authority raised up in a crisis.

**The Covenant**
The binding agreement between Israel and God established at Sinai. The book of Judges is the story of Israel repeatedly breaking it.

**Baal**
A Canaanite fertility deity — associated with rain and crops. Not worshipped out of stupidity but out of economic anxiety.

**Hesed**
Covenant loyalty — the quality God has toward Israel and expects in return. Notably absent from most of Judges.

## Discussion Questions

**Observation**
The book of Judges uses a repeating cycle: rest → abandonment → oppression → cry for help → deliverance → rest. But each iteration ends worse than the last. What does the text suggest is the reason the cycle keeps repeating?

**Context**
Baal worship in ancient Canaan was partly an economic decision — Baal was the deity associated with rain and harvest. Knowing that, how does it change the way you read Israel's "abandonment" of God? Does it make it more or less understandable?

**Context**
Hebrews 11:32 lists Gideon, Samson, and Jephthah as examples of faith — despite their significant failures. What does it suggest about what "faith" means in the book of Judges? Is it moral faithfulness, or something else?

**Application**
The book's diagnosis is that "everyone did what was right in their own eyes." There's a healthy version of personal conviction and an unhealthy version of self-referential morality. How would you distinguish between them in your own life?

**Application**
The drift in Judges isn't caused by people choosing evil — it's caused by people not choosing at all, keeping religious options open, adding rather than replacing. Where do you see that pattern today — in communities, or in yourself?

**Application**
Chapters 17–21 end the book with violence and chaos that goes unresolved — no judge, no deliverance, no verdict except the final line. Why do you think the author chose to end there rather than with a restoration?

## Closing Thought

Judges shows what a community looks like when it keeps the form of its commitments without the substance — not through dramatic rebellion, but through gradual drift, one generation at a time.
