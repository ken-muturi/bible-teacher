# Mark

> NT 2 of 27 · Gospels 1 of 4 · The Gospel of the servant who moves — urgency, power, and a cross that redefines both

---

## εὐθύς (euthys) — "immediately / straightway"

41 occurrences in Mark. Approximately 5× in Matthew and Luke combined. More than the rest of the NT.

- Act 1 (1:1–8:26): ~30 occurrences — Power in Galilee
- Act 2 (8:31–16:8): 11 occurrences — Suffering toward Jerusalem

More than a stylistic tic — a theological statement. The Son of Man came to act, not to be discussed. Mark's pace is deliberate urgency.

## Act 1 — Power in Galilee (1:1–8:26)

**1:1–20 — Prologue + first disciples**
John, baptism, temptation, "Follow me"

**1:21–3:6 — Authority demonstrated**
Teaching, exorcism, healing, Sabbath controversy — "Who is this?"

**3:7–6:6 — Kingdom breaks in**
Kingdom parables, calming the storm, Gerasene demoniac, Jairus/bleeding woman

**6:7–8:26 — Expanding mission**
Sending of Twelve, feeding 5000, walking on water, feeding 4000, blind man of Bethsaida

**1:34 · 3:12 · 5:43 · 7:36 — Messianic secret begins**
Jesus silences demons, healed people, and eventually his own disciples

## The Pivot — Mark 8:27–30, Caesarea Philippi

> "Who do you say that I am?"
> "You are the Christ."

Everything before asks the question. Everything after answers what *kind* of Christ. Immediately followed by the first passion prediction (8:31) and "Get behind me, Satan" (8:33).

## Identity

- **Author:** John Mark — Peter's interpreter (1 Pet 5:13, Acts 12:12). Later redeemed after desertion (Acts 13:13; 2 Tim 4:11).
- **Date:** ~65–70 AD · earliest Gospel · written as Rome burns and persecution rises
- **Audience:** Roman/Gentile — explains Jewish customs, translates Aramaic, Latinizes expressions
- **Length:** 16 chapters · 678 verses · shortest Gospel
- **Source:** Peter's eyewitness testimony — vivid, present-tense narration, specific details

---

## Three Passion Predictions and Disciple Failures

| # | Reference | Prediction | Disciple Failure |
|---|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| 1 | 8:31 | Son of Man must suffer, be rejected by elders, be killed, and rise after three days. | Peter rebukes Jesus (8:32) — "Get behind me, Satan" (8:33) |
| 2 | 9:31 | Son of Man delivered into human hands, killed, raised on the third day. | Disciples argue about who is greatest (9:34) — more specific, more tone-deaf |
| 3 | 10:33–34 | Most detailed: betrayed, condemned by priests, handed to Gentiles, mocked, flogged, killed, raised. | James and John request seats of honour at Jesus's right and left (10:35–40) |

Each prediction becomes more specific. Each failure becomes more tone-deaf. Peter's final failure: the denial (14:66–72).

## The Messianic Secret

Jesus commands silence after healings and miracles. Identity can only be rightly understood after the cross. Every "messiah" without the cross is the wrong messiah.

- **1:34 · 3:12** — Jesus forbids demons to speak — they know who he is, but their testimony is premature
- **1:44** — Healed leper — "See that you say nothing to anyone"
- **5:43** — Jairus's daughter raised — "Tell no one what had happened"
- **7:36** — Deaf-mute healed — Jesus charged them to tell no one; they proclaimed it more
- **8:30** — After Peter's confession — "He strictly charged them to tell no one about him"
- **9:9** — After transfiguration — tell no one until the Son of Man is raised from the dead
- **15:39 — Secret lifted** — at the cross, a Roman soldier confesses: *"Truly this man was the Son of God."* The one who had no stake in false hope gets it right.

## Markan Sandwiches (Intercalation)

Mark's signature literary device: story A begins — story B interrupts and completes — story A concludes. The interrupting story always interprets the outer frame.

**Sandwich 1 — Jairus's Daughter (outer) / Bleeding Woman (inner)**
Outer A: Jairus: My Daughter (5:21–24, 35–43) — Inner B: Bleeding Woman (5:25–34)
Meaning: Faith that falls at Jesus's feet and faith that reaches out and touches — both receive "your faith has made you well." Healing flows through vulnerability, not status. The interruption is part of the miracle.

**Sandwich 2 — Fig Tree Cursed (outer) / Temple Cleansed (inner)**
Outer A: Fig Tree Cursed (11:12–14, 20–25) — Inner B: Temple Cleansed (11:15–19)
Meaning: The cursed fig tree = fruitless religion that looks alive but produces nothing. The cleansed temple = what true worship should be. Israel's institutional religion is being judged by the same standard as the tree.

**Sandwich 3 — Twelve Sent Out (outer) / John Beheaded (inner)**
Outer A: Twelve Sent Out (6:7–13, 30–32) — Inner B: John Beheaded (6:14–29)
Meaning: Disciples are sent out in power; while they are gone, John is killed in powerlessness. The sandwich foreshadows the cost of the mission — those sent in power may return to hear of death.

## Act 2 — Key Moments

- **9:2–8 — Transfiguration** — Moses and Elijah; "This is my beloved Son, listen to him." The disciples see his glory; they still don't understand.
- **10:17–22 — Rich young man** — "One thing you lack." Jesus loved him. He went away sad. The kingdom costs everything.
- **11:1–10 — Triumphal entry** — "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!" The crowd wants a political king.
- **14:3–9 — Anointing at Bethany** — "She has done a beautiful thing to me." The only person who prepares for his death.
- **14:32–42 — Gethsemane** — "Abba, Father... not what I will, but what you will." The disciples sleep three times.
- **14:66–72 — Peter's denial** — the final and deepest disciple failure. "And he broke down and wept."
- **15:33–39 — The cross** — three hours of darkness, cry of dereliction (Ps 22:1), temple curtain torn top to bottom, centurion: "Truly this man was the Son of God."
- **16:1–8 — Empty tomb** — "He has risen, he is not here." Three women. Linen cloths. An angel. And then silence.

**Mark's Final Word (16:8)**
> "They went out and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had seized them, and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid."

The oldest manuscripts end here — ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ — ending on a Greek postpositive conjunction, grammatically jarring. The longer ending (vv. 9–20) is absent from the best witnesses. The abruptness is almost certainly intentional. The reader must complete the story.

## Key Words

**εὐθύς / euthys** — "immediately / straightway" — Mark's signature word of urgency (41×)
More than a stylistic tic — a theological statement. The Son of Man came to act, not to be discussed. Mark's pace is deliberate urgency. 41× in Mark vs ~5× in Matthew and Luke combined.

**μυστήριον / mysterion** — "mystery / secret" — The Messianic Secret (4:11)
Jesus repeatedly commands silence about his identity. Mark's way of showing that the disciples and readers must understand the cross before they can name him rightly. A messiah without the cross is the wrong messiah.

**διάκονος / diakonos** — "servant / deacon" — The servant-leader paradox (10:43–45)
"Whoever would be great among you must be your servant... the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve." Greatness is redefined at every passion prediction.

**εὐαγγέλιον / euangelion** — "gospel / good news" — First word of the book (1:1)
Romans used this word for the emperor's proclamation — a new king has come, peace is established. Mark uses it for a different king whose throne is a cross.

**ἐκπλήσσω / ekplessō** — "amazed / astonished" — The crowd's repeated reaction to Jesus
The crowd is repeatedly and deeply astonished by his authority over teaching, demons, disease, and death. Mark uses this more than any other Gospel. The question "Who is this?" is the engine of Act 1.

## Cross-Bible Links

**← Isaiah 40:3 + Malachi 3:1**
Mark 1:2–3 quotes both as a single composite "Isaiah" citation — the voice in the wilderness, the messenger preparing the way. Note: Mark attributes both to Isaiah; only Isa 40:3 is technically Isaiah. Mark opens with the OT's own announcement of what it has been pointing to.

**← Isaiah 53**
The Suffering Servant is the background theology of 10:45 — "ransom for many" echoes Isa 53:10–12. The servant who bears iniquity of "many" makes himself an offering. Mark's ransom is Isaiah's servant.

**← Psalm 22**
The cross narrative is saturated with Ps 22: garments divided by lot (15:24), "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (15:34), mockery matching vv. 7–8. The psalm was both a cry of desolation and a promise of vindication.

**NT → Acts 13:13**
John Mark abandons Paul on the first missionary journey. Later (2 Tim 4:11) Paul calls him "useful for ministry." Tradition holds Mark is Peter's interpreter in Rome — the Gospel is Peter's testimony filtered through Mark's pen.

**≈ Matthew 16:16 / Luke 9:20**
Parallel confessions at Caesarea Philippi in all three Synoptics. Only Mark includes the unvarnished "Get behind me, Satan" (8:33) — Matthew and Luke soften the aftermath. Mark's Peter fails harder and more specifically.

## The Two Confessions

> "You are the Christ."
> — Peter, 8:29 · Caesarea Philippi · private · without understanding the cross

> "Truly this man was the Son of God."
> — Roman soldier, 15:39 · Calvary · public · after watching Jesus die · by an enemy · with full knowledge of what the cross means

The second confession is the correct one. The messianic secret is only unlocked at the cross.

---

## Then → Now

**Then · ~65–70 AD**
Mark was written for a Roman audience facing Nero's persecution — believers who had watched leaders martyred and wondered whether following a crucified king was a mistake. The breathless pace, stripped-back birth narrative, and suffering-servant theology are not accidental choices. The abrupt ending (16:8) left the reader inside the story's open question.

**Now**
The question of Caesarea Philippi is still the only question that matters — not "Who do people say Jesus is?" but "Who do *you* say he is?" Mark's unfinished ending is an invitation: the story continues in whoever reads it. For contemporary readers impatient with religion, Mark's Jesus is harder to dismiss than any theological argument.
