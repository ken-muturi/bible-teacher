# HOSEA
## The Prophets · Minor Prophets 1 of 12

*Love that will not let go — covenant faithfulness against covenant betrayal*

God commands the prophet Hosea to marry an unfaithful woman, and through that marriage gives Israel — and us — a lived parable of what it costs to love someone who keeps leaving, and why love pursues anyway.

---

## Before You Watch

- Have you ever stayed loyal to someone — a friend, a family member, a colleague — who repeatedly let you down? What made you stay, and what was the cost of staying?

- Is there a difference between being loyal to someone and performing loyalty? How would you tell them apart from the outside — and from the inside?

- Does love have conditions? Is unconditional love actually possible, or is that just something people say?

---

## Key Terms

**Hesed**
Steadfast love or covenant loyalty — the word that defines what God offers Israel and what Israel fails to return. In Hosea, its absence (6:6) is the root diagnosis of everything else that goes wrong.

**Whoredom / Harlotry**
Hosea's sustained metaphor for Israel's religious infidelity — worshipping Baal and other gods while still formally attached to God. Not primarily a sexual term here, but a relational and political one.

**Da'at (Knowledge of God)**
Not intellectual knowledge but intimate, covenant knowledge — the kind shared between partners. Hosea's charge against Israel (4:1, 6) is a lack of this knowing: they have religion without relationship.

**Shub (Return / Repentance)**
Literally "to turn back." Hosea uses it more than any other prophet. It captures both Israel's recurring failure to return and God's persistent invitation to do so, culminating in chapter 14.

**Valley of Achor**
The site of Achan's sin and execution in Joshua 7 — a place of shame and judgment in Israel's memory. Hosea 2:15 reframes it as a "door of hope," turning a symbol of catastrophe into a starting point for restoration.

## Discussion Questions

**Observation**
Hosea's three children are named Jezreel ("God scatters"), Lo-Ruhamah ("No mercy"), and Lo-Ammi ("Not my people") — and then in chapter 2 each name is reversed. What does the reversal of these names tell you about how the book understands judgment and restoration: are they opposites, or something more complicated?

**Observation**
In chapter 3, Hosea buys Gomer back for fifteen pieces of silver and a measure of barley — roughly half the price of a slave. Then he tells her to stay with him and wait. What is this scene doing in the book? What does it add that the speeches and oracles cannot?

**Context**
Hosea 6:6 — "I desire hesed and not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings" — comes right after 6:1–3, a speech of repentance that sounds sincere. What does the context suggest about why God rejects it? Is the problem the words, the timing, or something else?

**Context**
In chapter 2, God says he will "allure" Israel into the wilderness and "speak tenderly to her" — the same language used of courtship. The wilderness is also the place Israel spent forty years in judgment. What does it mean for tenderness and discipline to use the same geography? How does that tension shape the book's portrait of God?

**Application**
Hosea 11:1–9 shifts the metaphor from husband to parent: God as a father who taught Israel to walk, carried them, and cannot bring himself to give them up. "My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender. I will not execute my burning anger." What does this portrait say about the relationship between justice and love — and where do you find yourself in it?

**Application**
Hosea's charge in chapter 4 is that there is "no faithfulness, no hesed, and no knowledge of God in the land" — and that the priests are partly responsible because they have withheld knowledge from the people. What does it look like when religious communities produce performance rather than genuine knowing? How would you recognize it from the inside?

**Application**
Hosea 14 ends with an open invitation: "Return, O Israel, to the Lord your God." No guarantee of outcome is given, just an invitation to bring words and return. If you were writing a response to that invitation — honest rather than polished — what would it say?

## Going Deeper

**Book**
Derek Kidner, *The Message of Hosea* (Bible Speaks Today series, IVP) — accessible and pastoral, with close attention to the Hebrew; the best starting point for non-specialists.

**Resource**
Tim Keller's sermon series on Hosea, available through Gospel in Life (gospelinlife.com — search "Hosea") — applies the book's themes of grace and return to contemporary life.

**Read Alongside**
Jeremiah 31:31–34 — the new covenant promise that answers what Hosea diagnoses: God writing his law inwardly rather than relying on external performance.

---

## Closing Thought

Hosea insists that the gospel begins not with human seeking but with divine pursuit — a love that absorbs betrayal, bears cost, and keeps calling from the wilderness.
