# Persistent Prayer — Theological Brief

> Topic: The biblical and historical roots of "praying until something happens"
> Key texts: Luke 18:1–8 · Luke 11:5–13 · Genesis 32:24–32 · 1 Kings 18:41–45 · Daniel 10:2–13

---

## The Question

Where did we get the concept of encouraging fellow Christians to pray until something happens?

The practice has genuine biblical roots — but also tradition layered on top of the text. Both need to be distinguished honestly.

---

## The Clearest Biblical Warrant: Jesus's Own Parables

### Luke 18:1–8 — The Persistent Widow

Jesus explicitly tells this parable "to show that they should always pray and not give up." A widow keeps coming before an unjust judge until he grants her request. The logic is *a fortiori* — if an unjust judge eventually responds to persistence, how much more will a good God respond to his chosen ones who cry to him day and night?

### Luke 11:5–13 — The Friend at Midnight

A man knocks at his neighbour's door at midnight, keeps knocking, and gets what he needs "because of his shameless audacity" (Greek: ἀναίδεια, *anaideia* — also translated "persistence" or "boldness"). Jesus follows it immediately with: "Ask and it will be given... seek and you will find... knock and the door will be opened."

The key is the Greek verb tense: the repeated present imperatives ("keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking") imply ongoing, sustained action — not a single request made once.

These two parables are the clearest NT source for persistent prayer. Jesus himself is teaching that persistence in prayer matters.

---

## Old Testament Models the Tradition Draws On

### Jacob Wrestling (Genesis 32:24–32)

"I will not let you go unless you bless me." Jacob wrestles through the night and receives both a wound and a blessing. This has been read for centuries as the paradigm of *prevailing prayer* — refusing to release God until he answers.

### Elijah and the Rain (1 Kings 18:41–45)

Elijah prays seven times before the cloud appears. James 5:17–18 specifically holds this up as the model of persistent prayer: "a human being like us" whose sustained intercession stopped and started rain. James uses it to encourage ordinary believers that their prayers have the same power.

### Daniel's 21-Day Prayer (Daniel 10:2–13)

Daniel prays and fasts for three weeks before an angel appears, explaining he was delayed by "the prince of the Persian kingdom." This passage is widely used — especially in charismatic circles — to argue that persistent prayer overcomes spiritual resistance and eventually breaks through.

### Moses Interceding for Israel (Exodus 32; Numbers 14)

Extended intercession that appears to change God's declared course of action. Moses doesn't pray once and stop — he stands in the gap repeatedly, and God relents.

## Where the Specific Practice Came From

### The Holiness and Pentecostal Movements (Late 1800s–Early 1900s)

The practice of *tarrying* was drawn directly from Acts 1:4: "Do not leave Jerusalem, but wait for the gift my Father promised." The disciples waited in the upper room for ten days before Pentecost. Pentecostal revivalists — particularly around the Azusa Street Revival (1906, William Seymour) — took this as a template: you pray and wait until you receive the Holy Spirit.

**The problem with this reading:** Acts 1:4 is a specific historical instruction for a once-for-all event that brought the Holy Spirit at Pentecost. It was not a method Jesus gave for all believers to repeat indefinitely. But the practice caught on and became deeply embedded in Pentecostal and charismatic culture.

### Charles Finney and 19th-Century Revivalism

Finney's "new measures" — sustained revival meetings, anxious benches, all-night prayer — embedded the idea that prayer is something you press through until a breakthrough comes, emotionally and experientially. The Holiness movement's emphasis on "crisis experiences" (entire sanctification, baptism of the Spirit) reinforced the expectation that sustained prayer produces a felt, datable breakthrough.

## The Honest Tension

**What the Bible clearly supports:**
- Persistent, ongoing prayer — don't give up (Luke 18:1)
- Faith that keeps asking (Luke 11:9–10, present imperatives)
- Models of extended intercession: Moses, Daniel, Elijah
- Wrestling with God in honest struggle (Jacob)

**What has been added by tradition:**
- Tarrying as a method to receive the Holy Spirit (misapplied from Acts 1)
- The idea that length or intensity of prayer increases God's willingness
- The expectation of an emotional or physical experience as the sign that "something happened"
- Mechanical repetition — which Jesus himself warned against

### The Counterweight Jesus Provides

Matthew 6:7: "Do not keep on babbling like pagans, for they think they will be heard because of their many words."

The same Jesus who commended persistence in Luke 18 warns against verbosity as a method in Matthew 6. The distinction is between **faith that won't quit** and **technique that thinks God needs to be worn down**.

## Summary and Application

The concept has solid biblical roots — Jesus taught it, the OT models it, James commends it. Persistent prayer is not a tradition invented by revivalists; it is in the grain of the text.

But the specific format — tarrying meetings, "pray until you feel it," treating Acts 1 as a repeatable template — is largely a Pentecostal/Holiness tradition. The biblical warrant for persistence is strong. The biblical warrant for a particular method or experience as the required endpoint is much weaker.

**The healthiest reading:** Pray with faith that doesn't quit — not with a technique that treats God as reluctant. The widow's persistence was relational and desperate, not mechanical. That is what Jesus commended.

**Question to sit with:** Is my persistence in prayer coming from faith that believes God is good and will act — or from a technique that assumes God needs to be worn down?
