John
Book 43 of 66 · The Fourth Gospel · ~AD 85–95
Author: John the apostle — "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (21:24)
Written from: Ephesus (traditional)
Audience: A wide church, Jew and Greek, a generation who did not see

The Seven Signs — Miracles That Reveal His Glory (ch. 2–11)
John selects seven "signs" (sēmeia) — not mere wonders, but pointers to who Jesus is
Each sign unveils a facet of his glory and calls for belief; John says he chose these from many (20:30).

Raising of Lazarus
The climactic sign — "I am the resurrection and the life." Power over death itself.
John never calls these "miracles" (dynameis) as the other Gospels do; he calls them signs — each one a signpost pointing beyond the wonder to the identity of Jesus. Some count the resurrection itself as the eighth and greatest sign, and the miraculous catch of fish (ch. 21) as a resurrection echo. The signs are not proofs that compel; they are revelations that invite belief — and in John, people divide over every one.

Alongside the absolute "I AM" (8:58, "before Abraham was, I am"), at which they took up stones.

The predicated "I AM" sayings each pair naturally with a sign (bread ↔ feeding the 5,000; light ↔ the blind man; resurrection ↔ Lazarus). Behind them stands the absolute egō eimi of 8:58 — a claim to the very Name of God (Exod 3:14), which his hearers understood exactly, and picked up stones.
The Prologue — The Hinge of the Whole Gospel (1:1–18)
"The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (1:14)
John opens not at Bethlehem but "in the beginning" (echoing Genesis 1), identifying Jesus as the eternal Logos — God's self-expression, through whom all things were made. Then the scandal at the centre of the faith: this eternal Word "became flesh and dwelt (literally 'tabernacled,' eskēnōsen) among us." The God who once filled the tent in the wilderness now pitches his tent in a human life, and "we have seen his glory." Everything that follows — every sign, every "I AM," the cross as enthronement — unpacks this one sentence: the invisible God has made himself known in a Man you could touch. Grace and truth did not arrive as ideas; they walked into the room.
Six Key Words (Greek)
λόγος
logos — "word"
1:1, 14
Word, reason, self-expression
Rich in both Hebrew ("the word of the LORD" by which he creates and reveals) and Greek thought (the rational principle ordering the cosmos). John claims this Logos is a person — and became flesh. The bridge between God and everything he has made.
σημεῖον
sēmeion — "sign"
~17× in John
A sign, a pointer
John's chosen word for Jesus' miracles. A sign is never about itself; it points beyond, to the identity and glory of the one who does it. The signs are structured to lead the reader to the confession of 20:31 — "that you may believe."
δόξα
doxa — "glory"
1:14; 12:23; 17
Glory, weight, radiance
In John, Jesus' "glory" is seen in the signs — but supremely on the cross. "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified" means the hour to die. John's paradox: the throne is a cross, the crown is thorns, and lifting-up is both.
πιστεύω
pisteuō — "believe"
~98× in John
To believe, trust, entrust oneself
The verb that carries the book's purpose (20:31). Strikingly, John almost never uses the noun "faith" (pistis) — only the verb. Belief is not a thing you possess but an act you do: to trust and keep trusting the person of Jesus.
ζωή
zōē — "life"
~36× in John
Life; esp. "eternal life"
John's word for the gift Jesus brings — "in him was life" (1:4); "I came that they may have life… abundantly" (10:10). Eternal life is not merely endless duration but a quality of life, begun now, in knowing the Father and the Son (17:3).
μένω
menō — "abide / remain"
~40× in John
To remain, stay, dwell
The heartbeat of John 15: "Abide in me, and I in you… apart from me you can do nothing." Not a burst of effort but a settled staying-connected to Christ, the vine — the secret of all lasting fruit and the mark of genuine discipleship.

The Hardest Parts — Where Readers Wrestle
1. John's repeated phrase "the Jews" — doesn't this fuel antisemitism?
This is a serious and painful question, given how the phrase has been misused across history. Several things must be held together. John himself is Jewish, as are Jesus, the disciples, and the earliest believers; "salvation is from the Jews" (4:22) is John's own line. The Greek hoi Ioudaioi often functions in John not as "the Jewish people" as a race but as a shorthand for the Judean religious leadership hostile to Jesus — sometimes it even means simply "the Judeans" or the temple authorities, and John records crowds of Jews who do believe. The conflict in John is an intra-Jewish dispute over the identity of Israel's Messiah, not a racial indictment. To read it as licence for anti-Jewish hatred is a grievous distortion of a book written by a Jew about the Jewish Messiah for the salvation of the world. Responsible teaching names both the history of misuse and the text's actual meaning.
2. Why is John so different from Matthew, Mark, and Luke? Is it historical?
The differences are real: John has no parables or exorcisms, long "I AM" discourses instead of pithy sayings, a ministry spanning several Passovers, a Judean rather than Galilean focus, and the temple cleansing near the start rather than the end. But difference is not contradiction. John writes later, selectively (21:25), and for a different purpose — supplementing the others and drawing out the theological meaning of events he witnessed. He shows detailed, verifiable knowledge of pre-70 AD Jerusalem geography and Jewish festivals. Most scholars now take John as an independent, theologically shaped, but historically rooted witness — the same Jesus seen from a different, reflective angle, "that you may believe."
3. Predestination vs "whoever believes" — John seems to teach both. Which is it?
John holds together two things we struggle to: robust divine sovereignty and genuine human responsibility. "No one can come to me unless the Father… draws him" (6:44); "all that the Father gives me will come to me" (6:37); yet also the sweeping "whoever believes in him should… have eternal life" (3:16) and "you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (5:40). John never resolves this into a formula. The drawing of the Father does not cancel the call to believe; the call to believe does not deny that faith is itself a gift. Both are pressed with full force — sovereign grace as the ground of salvation, and real, responsible faith as the way it is received. The pastoral effect is humility (I cannot save myself) and urgency (I am summoned to believe).
4. The woman caught in adultery (7:53–8:11) — is it really part of John?
This beloved story is almost certainly not an original part of John's Gospel: it is missing from the earliest and best manuscripts, some copies place it elsewhere (even in Luke), and its style differs from John's. Most modern Bibles keep it but mark it off with a note. That does not make the account untrue or unedifying — many scholars regard it as a genuine tradition about Jesus that circulated early and rings true to his character ("Neither do I condemn you; go, and… sin no more"). The honest approach is to teach it openly: to explain what the manuscripts show, not build doctrine uniquely on it, and still treasure it as consistent with the Jesus the rest of the Gospel reveals. Textual honesty and reverence for Scripture belong together.
Then → Now Bridge
Then — An Old Apostle's Testimony
Decades after the events, the last living apostle — the one who had leaned on Jesus at the table and stood at the cross — set down his witness for a church that would never see Jesus in the flesh. He chose seven signs from many, wove them with the "I AM" sayings and the long farewell discourses, and stated his aim plainly: not to record everything, but to lead the reader to believe and find life in the name of Jesus.
Now — The Gospel That Asks You to Believe
John remains the doorway Gospel — the one pressed into the hands of seekers, because it is built for exactly that. It confronts every reader with the same crisis it forced on Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, and Thomas: not merely to admire Jesus but to believe — to entrust yourself to the Word made flesh, crucified and risen. Blessed, Jesus tells Thomas, "are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (20:29). That is us. That is the book's reach across the centuries to you.
Question to sit with: John wrote "that you may believe… and have life." Have I moved past admiring Jesus to actually entrusting my life to him — the Word made flesh, lifted up, and risen?
