Ezekiel
Book 26 of 66 · The Exilic Prophet · ~593–571 BC
Author: Ezekiel son of Buzi — a priest, deported to Babylon
Where: Tel-Abib, by the Kebar (Chebar) canal, Babylonia
Audience: The exiles of 597 BC — and, at a distance, Jerusalem
Contemporaries: Jeremiah (in Judah), Daniel (in Babylon's court)
Genre: Visionary prophecy · sign-acts · apocalyptic
Length: 48 chapters, precisely dated oracles

The glory returns; a healing river flows out; the land is reapportioned. Final word: Yahweh-Shammah.
The structural key — chapter 33:21. The whole book pivots on one dated line: "a man who had escaped from Jerusalem came to me and said, 'The city has fallen!'" Before that verse, Ezekiel's message is almost relentlessly judgment; after it, almost entirely hope. God refuses to comfort with false hope while the city still stands — but the moment judgment is complete, the tone transforms. The fall is not the end of the story; it is the hinge of it. (Note: the nations-oracles of 25–32 are dated around this pivot but gathered together as a literary bridge.)
The Spine of the Book — Where Is the Glory? (kavod)
The kavod — God's visible presence — moves five times. Track it, and you have the whole book.
More than any other prophet, Ezekiel organises everything around the location of God's presence.
Ch 1–3
ARRIVES in Babylon
The glory appears to the exiles by a foreign canal — God is not trapped in Jerusalem.
Ch 8–10
RISES from the temple
Because of idolatry, the glory lifts from the cherubim to the threshold.
Ch 10–11
LEAVES the city
It pauses at the east gate, then departs over the Mount of Olives. The temple is now empty.
Ch 12–39
ABSENT
The long middle: judgment, exile, the nations — a world where the presence has withdrawn.
Ch 43:1–5
RETURNS
The glory comes back from the east, fills the new temple, and never leaves again.
It leaves by the east gate (10:19) and returns by the east gate (43:4). The same door it walked out of, it walks back through. Ezekiel 48:35 gives the city its new and final name: Yahweh-Shammah — "The LORD Is There."
The Refrain — "Then You Will Know That I Am the LORD"
The recognition formula appears around 70 times — the single most repeated phrase in the book.
The goal of both judgment and salvation is identical: that Israel and the nations would truly know Yahweh. God does not act merely to punish or to rescue — he acts so that he will be rightly known. Knowing God, not comfort or survival, is the point of everything that happens in the book. The same phrase seals an oracle of doom and an oracle of restoration.
The Four Great Visions — Ezekiel's Vision-Frame
Ezekiel 1 · The Chariot-Throne
The Merkabah — Glory on the Move
Four living creatures, wheels within wheels full of eyes, a crystal expanse, and above it a throne with a figure "like a man" wrapped in fire and rainbow. It is deliberately overwhelming — Ezekiel can only stack "the likeness of… the appearance of…" The point: God's mobile throne has come to Babylon. He is not confined to the temple.
The wheels mean the throne goes anywhere — even into exile. God is present with his people in the unclean land.
Ezekiel 8–11 · The Temple Tour
What Drove the Glory Out
Carried in vision to Jerusalem, Ezekiel sees idol-worship hidden in the temple's chambers — the image of jealousy, elders worshipping creatures in the dark, women weeping for Tammuz, men bowing to the sun with their backs to God. Only then does the glory leave. The departure is caused: the presence withdraws from a house that has filled itself with rival gods.
God does not abandon arbitrarily. The glory leaves a temple that has already, in effect, evicted him.
Ezekiel 37 · The Valley of Dry Bones
Can These Bones Live?
A valley of sun-bleached bones — the exiles' own verdict on themselves: "our hope is gone, we are cut off." Ezekiel prophesies; bones rattle together, gain sinew and flesh — but stay dead until the ruach (breath/Spirit) enters. Two stages: reassembly, then life. Israel's national resurrection — and a window onto the resurrection of the dead.
Restoration is not self-improvement. The corpse cannot revive itself; only the in-breathed Spirit makes the dead live.
Ezekiel 40–48 · The New Temple & River
The Glory Returns — and a River Flows Out
An immense, meticulously measured temple; the glory returns through the east gate to fill it; and from under the threshold a river trickles, then deepens — ankle, knee, waist, then "deep enough to swim in" — flowing east to heal the Dead Sea and line its banks with fruit trees. The book ends with the land re-shared and the city renamed.
Presence is not static. Where God dwells, life flows outward and deepens, healing even the deadest water.
The Sign-Acts — Prophecy Performed With His Body
4:1–3
The Brick & Iron Pan
Ezekiel draws Jerusalem on a clay brick and lays siege to it with a model army and an iron griddle — a toy city under real wrath.
4:4–8
Bound on His Side
He lies on his left side 390 days, then his right 40 — bearing the years of the nation's guilt in his own immobilised body.
4:9–17
Rationed Bread
Weighed food and measured water cooked over fuel — the starvation diet of a besieged city, enacted daily.
5:1–4
The Shaved Head
He shaves hair and beard, weighs it, burns a third, strikes a third, scatters a third — the fate of the population divided three ways.
12:1–7
The Exile's Baggage
He digs through a wall and carries out a refugee's bundle at dusk — packing for a deportation the people still deny is coming.
24:15–27
The Wife Not Mourned
His wife dies — "the delight of his eyes" — and God forbids him to grieve, a sign that the loss of the temple will be too deep for ordinary mourning.
Six Key Words — The Vocabulary of Ezekiel
כָּבוֹד
kavod — "glory"
~19× in book
Glory / weight / visible presence
From a root meaning "heavy, weighty." The kavod is the manifest, almost tangible presence of God. Its movement (arrive → leave → return) is the structural backbone of the book. To lose the glory is to lose everything; its return is the climax of all hope.
רוּחַ
ruach — "spirit/wind/breath"
~52× in book
Spirit / wind / breath
One word, three senses, all in play. The ruach lifts Ezekiel between visions, animates the wheels of the chariot, is breathed into the dry bones, and is the new Spirit God places within his people (36:27). Ezekiel is the great prophet of the Spirit's life-giving power.
בֶּן־אָדָם
ben-adam — "son of man"
~93× in book
Son of man / mortal / son of dust
God's near-constant address to the prophet. It stresses his frailty before the glory — "mere mortal." The phrase keeps the human small and the vision large. Centuries later Jesus adopts "the Son of Man" as his favourite self-title, drawing on both Ezekiel and Daniel 7.
לֵב
lev — "heart"
key in 11, 18, 36
Heart / inner self / will
The diagnosis and the cure. The problem is a "heart of stone" — hard, unresponsive, idolatrous. The promise is a "heart of flesh," responsive and alive, with God's own Spirit inside it (36:26). The new covenant is fundamentally a heart transplant, not a behaviour-management programme.
תּוֹעֵבָה
to'evah — "abomination"
~43× in book
Abomination / detestable thing
Ezekiel's word for the idolatry that defiled the temple and provoked the glory's departure. It names worship and conduct that are not merely wrong but repugnant to God's holiness. The sheer frequency shows why the judgment is so severe: the holy place had been saturated with the detestable.
נָשִׂיא
nasi — "prince"
key in 34, 37, 44–48
Prince / leader / one lifted up
Ezekiel mostly avoids "king" (melek) for the future ruler, preferring nasi — "prince." After the failure of Judah's kings, the coming Davidic ruler is cast more humbly, as shepherd and prince under God's kingship (34:24; 37:25). Royal hope, chastened and re-rooted in David.

Deep Notes — Eight Passages Worth Preaching
Ezekiel 2:8–3:3
Eat the Scroll
"Then I ate it, and it tasted as sweet as honey in my mouth."
Before he can speak God's word, Ezekiel must internalise it — even though it is written front and back with "lament and mourning and woe." The hard word, taken in, still tastes sweet.
Preach: you cannot deliver a word you have not first swallowed. The messenger is shaped by the message before the audience ever hears it. (Cf. Rev 10:9–10.)
Ezekiel 3:16–21
The Watchman's Accountability
"…I will hold you accountable for their blood."
The watchman is judged not by whether people heed the warning but by whether he sounds it. Silence in the face of danger makes him complicit; faithful warning clears him regardless of response.
Preach: faithfulness is measured by obedience, not outcomes. We are responsible to warn, not to convert.
Ezekiel 10:18–19
The Glory at the Threshold
"Then the glory of the LORD departed from over the threshold of the temple."
The most devastating sentence in the book — and it happens quietly, in stages. The greatest tragedy is not the building's destruction but the presence's departure. An empty temple is just architecture.
Preach: Ichabod — "the glory has departed." A church can keep its forms long after the presence has gone. The question is never the building; it is whether God is there.
Ezekiel 18:30–32
Each One Accountable
"Get a new heart and a new spirit… Why will you die?"
Against the fatalistic proverb ("our parents sinned, we suffer"), God insists each person stands or falls on their own response — and pleads, "I take no pleasure in the death of anyone… Repent and live!"
Preach: God's judgment is just and personal, and his heart is not eager to condemn but longing to give life. The command to "get a new heart" (18) becomes the promise to give one (36).
Ezekiel 34:11–16
"I Myself Will Search"
"I myself will search for my sheep and look after them."
After indicting the shepherds who fed themselves, God announces he will do the shepherding personally — seek the lost, bind the injured, strengthen the weak — and set "my servant David" over them.
Preach: when human leadership fails the flock, God does not abandon the sheep — he comes himself. Jesus reads this chapter as his own job description (John 10; Luke 15).
Ezekiel 36:25–27
The Heart Transplant
"I will give you a new heart… and put my Spirit in you."
Three movements: cleansing (sprinkled water), replacement (stone heart → heart of flesh), and indwelling (God's own Spirit). This is the gospel of regeneration, promised in the OT and fulfilled at Pentecost.
Preach: real change is not trying harder with a stone heart; it is receiving a new one. The initiative is entirely God's — "I will… I will… I will."
Ezekiel 37:1–14
Can These Bones Live?
"Our bones are dried up and our hope is gone; we are cut off."
God meets the exiles' deepest despair — their own self-diagnosis as the walking dead — and answers it with two-stage resurrection: reassembly by the word, then life by the breath. National restoration that opens toward bodily resurrection.
Preach: no situation is too dead for the Spirit. But preach honestly — the bones were truly dead; this is resurrection, not first aid.
Ezekiel 47:1–12
The River From the Temple
"Where the river flows everything will live."
A river that deepens as it goes and grows fresher the farther it flows — healing the Dead Sea, lining its banks with ever-fruiting trees whose leaves are for healing. Presence overflows into a world made alive.
Preach: God's presence is never for hoarding. Where he dwells, life flows outward and deepens. The trajectory of grace is always from the sanctuary to the dead sea.
The Hardest Passages — Where Honest Readers Wrestle
1. The graphic, violent imagery of chapters 16 and 23
Ezekiel portrays Jerusalem and Samaria as unfaithful wives in language so explicit it has shocked readers for millennia. The imagery is deliberately offensive — it forces a complacent people to feel the obscenity of idolatry as betrayal, not as a tidy theological error. These chapters demand pastoral care in teaching: name the metaphor's purpose (covenant treachery felt as marital betrayal), acknowledge how the violent-marriage imagery can wound abuse survivors, and refuse to use it to excuse any real-world cruelty. The target is Israel's sin, never a licence for human violence.
2. The departing glory — does God abandon his people?
The glory's departure (ch.10) is not capricious. Chapters 8–9 show why: the temple had been filled with idols and "abominations." God withdraws from a house that has already evicted him. And even the departure is mercy in disguise — the same chapters reveal the glory has appeared to the exiles in Babylon (ch.1). He leaves the building but not the people. The withdrawal is judgment; the relocation is grace.
3. Gog and Magog (38–39) — when and who?
Interpreters divide sharply. Some read Gog as a symbolic figure for the archetypal end-time enemy (Revelation 20 takes it this way); others attempt historical or future-political identifications. The text itself is more interested in the outcome than the identity: a final, overwhelming assault on God's restored people that God defeats so completely the weapons become firewood and the point is, once more, "they shall know that I am the LORD." Caution against confident geopolitical date-setting; the certainty is God's victory, not the headlines.
4. The temple vision (40–48) — literal blueprint or symbol?
A long-running debate. (a) Literalist/dispensational: a future physical temple in a millennial age — but then the reinstated animal sacrifices (43:18–27) are hard to square with Hebrews' "once for all." (b) Symbolic/ecclesial: the perfected presence of God with his people, fulfilled in Christ and the church, climaxing in Revelation 21–22 (note: that city has no temple, "for the Lord God is its temple"). (c) Idealised: a visionary pattern of holiness, never meant as construction plans. Most across traditions read it Christologically: the measured temple and healing river find their fulfilment in Christ and the new creation, however one handles the details.

Commentary Shelf — The Major Voices on Ezekiel
Daniel I. Block
The Book of Ezekiel, 2 vols. (NICOT, 1997–98)
Evangelical · Standard Reference
The benchmark evangelical commentary — exhaustive on the Hebrew, the ancient Near Eastern background, and the theology. Block's organising insight: Ezekiel is relentlessly theocentric — every oracle exists so that God will be known and his holiness vindicated.
Strong on the recognition formula ("you will know that I am the LORD") as the book's theological engine, and on the glory's movement as its structural spine.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — The Exiles by the Kebar
A deported people, their temple about to be rubble, asking the question every refugee asks: is our God still with us, or did we leave him behind in the ruins? Ezekiel's answer is the throne on wheels — God has come to Babylon. The presence they thought was tied to a building has followed them into exile, and one day will fill a house made new. Their identity was not in the land or the temple but in the God who is "there."
Now — A Church After Loss
Ezekiel speaks to any believer or congregation that has watched something collapse — a ministry, a reputation, a season of vitality — and wonders whether the glory has quietly slipped out the door. The book refuses both denial and despair: it names what drove the presence away, and it stakes everything on the promise that the same God who left by the east gate will return by it. Real hope begins not by pretending the bones aren't dry, but by asking the Spirit to breathe.
Question to sit with: If the measure of a place is not its size or success but simply "the LORD is there," what in my life or church would change if that became the only question that mattered?
Controlling Idea
"Ezekiel watches the glory of God leave a temple that had filled itself with idols — and spends the rest of the book insisting it will return. The hope is not a restored building, a revived nation, or even resurrected bones, but a recovered Presence: the city's last name is 'The LORD Is There.'"
