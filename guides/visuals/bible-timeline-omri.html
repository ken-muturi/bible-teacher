Contemporaries
Judah Kings
Judah
Asa of Judah
King · ~910–869 BC
Overlapped Omri's entire reign. Sought alliance with Ben-Hadad I of Aram against Israel (1 Kgs 15:18–20). Known for religious reforms in Judah, yet stumbled in later years.
Judah
Jehoshaphat of Judah
King · ~872–848 BC
Allied with Ahab (Omri's son). His son Jehoram of Judah married Athaliah — bringing Omride blood directly into Judah's royal house. The alliance that nearly destroyed the Davidic line.
Prophets
Prophet
Elijah the Tishbite
~875–848 BC
Called during Ahab's reign — the direct inheritor of Omri's covenantal failures. Confronted Baal worship (Jezebel's import), declared drought, stood alone at Carmel (1 Kgs 17–18). The prophetic antidote to the Omride dynasty.
Prophet
Obadiah (the official)
fl. ~874–853 BC · 1 Kgs 18:3–4
Ahab's palace manager who feared God — hid 100 prophets from Jezebel. A quiet act of courage inside the Omride court. (Distinct from Obadiah the prophet whose book's dating is debated.)
Foreign Powers
Phoenicia
Ethbaal of Sidon
King of Sidon / Tyre
Omri's ally — sealed by the marriage of Ahab to Ethbaal's daughter Jezebel. Strategic alliance that opened Israel to Baal worship on an institutional scale. The price of geopolitical stability.
Aram-Syria
Ben-Hadad I of Aram
~880–841 BC
Rival of Omri and Ahab. Bribed by Asa of Judah to attack Israel (1 Kgs 15:18–20). Later made treaty with Ahab. The ongoing Syrian threat shaped Omride foreign policy.
Assyria
Shalmaneser III
~858–824 BC — growing shadow
Not yet a threat during Omri's lifetime — but fought Ahab at the Battle of Qarqar (853 BC). The Kurkh Monolith names "Ahab the Israelite." Assyria would call Israel "Bit Humri" (House of Omri) for a century.
Moab
Mesha, King of Moab
fl. ~853 BC · The Mesha Stele
Paid tribute to Omri and Ahab. Rebelled after Ahab's death. The Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone, ~830 BC) names Omri — one of the earliest extra-biblical attestations of an Israelite king. Moab's perspective on liberation from Israel.
Key Events (~885–835 BC)
Zimri assassinates Elah — and Omri marches~885 BC
Zimri kills Elah in Tirzah; reigns 7 days. The army immediately proclaims Omri king. Omri besieges Tirzah; Zimri burns the palace and dies in it.
1 Kings 16:9–20
Civil War — Omri vs. Tibni~885–880 BC
Five-year standoff between Omri's faction and Tibni son of Ginath. Tibni dies; Omri becomes sole king over Israel.
1 Kings 16:21–22
Omri buys the hill of Samaria — a new capital~880 BC
Purchased from Shemer for two talents of silver. A politically brilliant move — no tribal loyalties attached to Samaria. Built as a royal city from scratch, rivaling Jerusalem. It would stand 150+ years.
1 Kings 16:24
Omri humbles Moab; treaty with Phoenicia~880–874 BC
Forced tribute from Moab (recorded on the Mesha Stele: "Omri, king of Israel, oppressed Moab many days"). Arranged Ahab's marriage to Jezebel of Sidon — sealing the Phoenician alliance.
Mesha Stele; 1 Kings 16:31
Omri dies; Ahab succeeds~874 BC
Biblical assessment: "did more evil than all before him" (1 Kgs 16:25) — but this is a covenantal verdict on his Baal-friendly policies, not a military one. He was Israel's most powerful king.
1 Kings 16:28
Elijah's ministry — drought, Carmel, Naboth~874–853 BC
Ahab's reign brings the full consequence of Omri's Phoenician alliance: Jezebel's Baal worship goes institutional. Elijah declares drought; Mt. Carmel showdown; Naboth's vineyard seized (1 Kgs 21). The prophetic confrontation with the Omride legacy.
1 Kings 17–21
Battle of Qarqar — Ahab vs. Shalmaneser III~853 BC
Ahab joins the Syrian coalition against Assyria at Qarqar. The Kurkh Monolith records "Ahab the Israelite" with 2,000 chariots — the largest contingent. Ahab dies the same year at Ramoth-Gilead.
Kurkh Monolith; 1 Kings 22:34–37
Joram of Israel rules; Elisha's ministry begins~852–841 BC
The third-generation Omride. Elisha takes over Elijah's mantle. Moab rebels (2 Kgs 3 — corroborated by the Mesha Stele). The Omride dynasty begins to unravel.
2 Kings 1–9; 2 Kings 3
Jehu's Revolution — the Omride dynasty ends~841 BC
Jehu (anointed by Elisha's servant) kills Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah in one campaign. Jezebel thrown from the window; Ahab's 70 sons killed. Four generations of Omride power ends in a single afternoon.
2 Kings 9–10
Athaliah seizes Judah; Omride line in Jerusalem~841–835 BC
Omri's granddaughter Athaliah kills the royal family of Judah and rules as queen. The infant Joash is hidden in the temple by Jehosheba. After 6 years, Jehoiada the priest reveals Joash; Athaliah killed. The Omride line in Judah ends.
2 Kings 11:1–16

Extra-Biblical Evidence: The Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone, ~830 BC) explicitly names Omri — "Omri, king of Israel, oppressed Moab many days" — making him one of the most archaeologically confirmed Israelite monarchs. Assyrian records used "Bit Humri" (House of Omri) to refer to the northern kingdom of Israel for over a century after the Omride dynasty ended with Jehu's revolution in ~841 BC. The Kurkh Monolith names his son "Ahab the Israelite." Omri was invisible in world memory for centuries — then archaeology confirmed him as one of the most consequential kings of Iron Age Israel.
Then → Now
Then
Omri was politically brilliant but covenantally disastrous. He built Samaria (which stood 150 years), subdued Moab, and allied with Phoenicia — the ancient Near East took notice. But his real legacy was the Baal worship Jezebel brought in, and the Omride DNA he planted in Judah through Athaliah, which nearly wiped out the Davidic line entirely (2 Kgs 11:1). The Assyrians remembered his name; the prophets remembered his sin (Mic 6:16).
Now
Competence and covenant faithfulness are not the same thing. A leader can be effective, strategic, and historically significant — and still be building on sand. Omri's dynasty lasted 4 kings across 44 years. Jehu's revolution ended it in one afternoon. What outlasted the Omride dynasty was not their military power but the prophetic word that confronted it. The statutes of Omri (Mic 6:16) were still being invoked as a warning 150 years later.
