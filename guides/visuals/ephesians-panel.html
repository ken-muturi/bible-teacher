Ephesians
Book 49 of 66 · Paul's Prison Epistle · ~AD 60–62
Author: Paul (from prison — Rome or Caesarea)
Recipients: The church at Ephesus (possibly circular)
Key city: Ephesus — commercial hub, temple of Artemis
Companions: Colossians, Philemon, Philippians (Prison Epistles)
Genre: Doctrinal-Pastoral Letter → Circular
"The cosmic scope of salvation — all things in heaven and on earth united in Christ —
and the church as the community that makes it visible"
Controlling Verse — The Mystery in One Sentence
Ephesians 1:9–10 · The thesis of the letter
"He made known to us the mystery of his will according to his good pleasure, which he purposed in Christ, to be put into effect when the times reach their fulfilment — to bring unity to all things in heaven and on earth under Christ."
Ephesians 1:9–10 (NIV) — ἀνακεφαλαιώσασθαι τὰ πάντα ἐν τῷ Χριστῷ
The Letter's Structure — Doctrine → Therefore → Practice
Doctrine — What God Has Done
Chapters 1 – 3
"Seated with Christ in the heavenly realms"
1:1–2 · Greeting
Grace and peace from God our Father
The standard Pauline opening — but saturated with theological freight from the first word.
1:3–14 · The Berakah
Every Spiritual Blessing in Christ
One long sentence in Greek (vv.3–14). Election, adoption, redemption, forgiveness, the mystery of his will, the Spirit as deposit. The entire sweep of salvation compressed into a single doxology. All three persons of the Trinity present.
1:15–23 · First Prayer
The Spirit of Wisdom and Revelation
Paul prays that they would know what they have been given — hope, riches, incomparable power. Closes with Christ as head over all things for the church, which is his body, "the fullness of him who fills everything in every way."
2:1–10 · Death to Life
Dead in Sin → Made Alive by Grace
The sharpest statement of the human condition and the grace that reverses it. "By grace you have been saved, through faith — this is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God — not by works." But v.10: "created in Christ Jesus to do good works."
2:11–22 · One New Humanity
The Dividing Wall Demolished
Jews and Gentiles — "far" and "near" — brought into one body. The "hostility" (the Mosaic law as a dividing barrier) abolished in the flesh of Christ. One new humanity from two. A dwelling place for God by the Spirit.
3:1–13 · The Mystery Revealed
Gentiles Are Co-Heirs, Members Together
The "mystery hidden for ages" is now disclosed: Gentiles are co-heirs, members of the same body, sharers in the promise. Paul is the prisoner who carries this mystery. The church makes the wisdom of God known — even to "the rulers and authorities in heavenly realms" (v.10).
3:14–21 · Second Prayer
Rooted and Grounded in Love
Paul kneels — unusual. He prays for inner strengthening, for Christ to dwell in their hearts by faith, for them to grasp the four dimensions of Christ's love, to be filled to "all the fullness of God." Closes with the doxology: "immeasurably more than we ask or imagine."
Therefore
4:1
Practice — How We Are to Walk
Chapters 4 – 6
"Walking worthy of the calling"
4:1–16 · Walk Worthy
Unity and Diversity in the Body
The hinge verse: "I urge you to walk in a manner worthy of the calling." Seven unities (one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God). Then diversity: the ascended Christ gives gifts — apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors-teachers — to equip the body to build itself up in love.
4:17–32 · Old Self / New Self
Put Off / Be Renewed / Put On
The gentile way of life contrasted with the way of Christ: darkened understanding, hardened heart, sensuality, impurity. In contrast: put off the old self, be renewed in the spirit of your minds, put on the new self. Specific commands: speak truth, be angry and sin not, give, use words that build up. "Be kind and compassionate... forgiving each other, just as in Christ God forgave you."
5:1–21 · Walk in Love · Light · Wisdom
Three "Walk" Commands in One Chapter
Walk in love (5:2 — as Christ loved and gave himself). Walk in light (5:8 — you were darkness, now light). Walk carefully/wisely (5:15 — making the most of every opportunity). Culminates: "be filled with the Spirit" — the driving power of all three walks. The Spirit-filled life expressed in mutual submission (5:21 — the overlooked verse).
5:22–6:9 · Household Codes
Wives · Husbands · Children · Parents · Workers · Masters
Three pairs of relationships, each transformed by the gospel. Wives/husbands: the marriage relationship as the icon of Christ and the church (5:31–32 — "this is a profound mystery"). Children/parents: "honour your father and mother" — the first commandment with a promise. Workers/masters: serve as to the Lord, not to men — radical humanisation of the workplace.
6:10–20 · The Armour of God
Stand Against the Schemes of the Devil
The letter does not end with exhortations to niceness — it ends with warfare. "Our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against rulers, against authorities, against the powers of this dark world." Six pieces of armour. The repeated command: stand. The armour is defensive — holding the ground already won by Christ — except the sword of the Spirit (the Word) and prayer.
6:21–24 · Closing
Peace and Grace to All Who Love Christ
Tychicus will deliver the letter and tell them Paul's news. The blessing: "Peace to all who love our Lord Jesus Christ with an undying love." The word "undying" (incorruptibility) is the letter's last note — the eschatological permanence of grace.
The Mystery Thread — μυστήριον Running Through the Letter
μυστήριον (mystērion) — "mystery" appears 6 times. In Paul, mystery = formerly hidden, now revealed. Not a puzzle but a disclosure.
1:9
The mystery of his will — to unite all things in Christ. The thesis. The cosmic plan of the ages.
3:3–4
The mystery made known to Paul by revelation — Gentiles co-heirs. Hidden for generations, now disclosed.
3:9
The administration (oikonomia) of this mystery — hidden in God from before creation, now revealed through the church.
5:32
Marriage as mystery — "I am talking about Christ and the church." The household code's theological summit.
6:19
Pray that Paul may fearlessly make known the mystery of the gospel — the letter closes still in the grip of the mystery.

Two Great Prayers — Paul on His Knees for the Church
First Prayer · Ephesians 1:15–23
The Prayer of Knowing
① That they would receive the Spirit of wisdom and revelation in knowing God
② That the eyes of their hearts would be enlightened — to know the hope of his calling
③ To know the riches of his glorious inheritance in the saints
④ To know the incomparably great power at work in those who believe — the resurrection power
The prayer is entirely about knowing what you already have. The problem is not lack of blessing — it is lack of awareness of blessing already given.
Second Prayer · Ephesians 3:14–21
The Prayer of Being Filled
① That they would be strengthened with power through his Spirit in the inner being
② That Christ would dwell in your hearts through faith
③ That they would be rooted and grounded in love
④ To grasp the four dimensions of Christ's love — wide, long, high, deep
⑤ To be filled to all the fullness of God
The two prayers bracket the doctrine section. The first is for knowledge; the second for experience. Together they describe the full Christian life: knowing and being filled.
Five Key Words — The Vocabulary of Ephesians
πλήρωμα
plēroma
4× in letter
Fullness / completeness / that which fills
The controlling word. The church is "the fullness of him who fills everything in every way" (1:23). Paul prays they be filled to "all the fullness of God" (3:19). The body grows "to the measure of the fullness of Christ" (4:13). Fullness is both the origin (God's fullness in Christ) and the destination (the church arriving at that fullness).
μυστήριον
mystērion
6× in letter
Mystery / formerly hidden, now revealed
Not a puzzle but a disclosure. The mystery in Ephesians has two layers: (1) cosmic — all things united in Christ (1:9-10); (2) social — Jew and Gentile together in one body (3:3-6). Both were hidden from ages; both now disclosed through the church's existence. Marriage is also called mystery (5:32) — pointing to Christ and the church.
οἰκονομία
oikonomia
3× in letter
Stewardship / administration / plan
From oikos (house) + nomos (law/management). The "economy" of grace — God's management plan for history. "The administration of the mystery" (3:9). The whole of history is a managed plan with a goal: the unity of all things in Christ at "the fullness of times" (1:10). God is not reacting to history. He is administering it.
περιπατέω
peripateō
8× in letter
To walk / to conduct one's life
The key verb of the practice section (4-6). Six explicit "walk" commands shaping the second half of the letter. The word is Jewish — in Hebrew, halakah (the walk of life, how to live) is the practical application of Torah. Paul's "walk" commands are his halakah for the new-covenant community: walk worthy, walk in love, walk in light, walk wisely.
ἀνακεφαλαιόω
anakephalaiōsasthai
1:10 · Thesis
To sum up / recapitulate / unite under one head
Used only twice in the NT (here and Rom 13:9). From kephalē (head). To bring everything under one head — like the summary (recapitulation) of an argument. The whole of history is moving toward a recapitulation in Christ: all things in heaven and on earth gathered up and headed by him. This word contains the letter's entire theology in one verb.
The Full Armour of God — Ephesians 6:10–18
Six Pieces + Prayer · "Stand firm" (repeated 3× in 4 verses). The posture is not advance but holding the ground won by Christ.
6:14a
Belt of Truth
Truthfulness / integrity as the foundation of the Christian's stance. Nothing stands without it.
6:14b
Breastplate of Righteousness
Christ's righteousness credited — covering the vital organs. Not moral achievement but imputed standing.
6:15
Feet Fitted with Peace
Readiness that comes from the gospel of peace. The soldier is mobile because they stand on sure ground.
6:16
Shield of Faith
The large Roman scutum — can protect the whole body. Extinguishes "all the flaming arrows of the evil one."
6:17a
Helmet of Salvation
Protecting the mind — the assurance of salvation as armour against the mind-targeting attacks of the enemy.
6:17b–18
Sword of the Spirit + Prayer
The only offensive piece — the Word of God. Prayer is the atmosphere in which all the armour operates.
Context note: Clinton Arnold's research (Ephesians: Power and Magic) shows that Ephesus was the centre of magical arts and power religion in Asia Minor (Acts 19:18–19 — the book-burning). Paul's armour passage speaks directly into a culture saturated with the language of powers, spirits, and cosmic conflict. The armour is not metaphor for abstract spiritual disciplines — it is the equipment for engagement with real spiritual opposition in a city that took that opposition seriously.
Hardest Interpretive Moments in Ephesians
1. Predestination in 1:4–5, 11 — does Ephesians teach individual election to salvation?
Eph 1:4 — "chosen in him before the foundation of the world." The election language is real and should not be smoothed away. The primary debate: is the election corporate (God chose the church in Christ, and individuals participate by being in Christ through faith) or individual (God chose specific persons for salvation before time)? Reformed readers (Lloyd-Jones, O'Brien) take the individual reading; some evangelical readers prefer the corporate-in-Christ frame. Both acknowledge the sovereignty of grace; they differ on whether the election is of persons or of a body. The key anchor: the election is always "in him" — never apart from Christ.
2. "Wives, submit to your husbands as to the Lord" (5:22) — the household code debate
The most contested passage in the letter. Three things often missed: (a) 5:21 — "submit to one another out of reverence for Christ" — mutual submission precedes and frames the specific codes; (b) the husband's instruction (5:25–33) is the longer and more demanding: "love your wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" — a call to self-giving sacrifice, not patriarchal control; (c) the codes radically humanised existing structures by placing them under Christ and extending dignity to wives, children, and slaves in ways that were counter-cultural. The debate is between complementarian and egalitarian readings. Both agree the text requires husbandly self-sacrifice; they differ on whether a functional ordering persists in marriage.
3. "The church is his body, the fullness of him who fills everything in every way" (1:23) — what does fullness mean here?
One of the most debated verses in Ephesians. Three readings: (a) the church completes Christ — he is somehow incomplete without his body (the active sense of plēroma); (b) the church is filled by Christ — he pours his fullness into it (passive sense); (c) the church is the community in which Christ's all-pervading presence is expressed and displayed. Most commentators (O'Brien, Hoehner, Lincoln) prefer option (b) or (c). The point is not that Christ needs us to complete him, but that the church is the community that makes the fullness of Christ visible in the world.
4. "Filling up all the fullness of God" (3:19) — is this possible?
Paul prays that believers "may be filled to all the fullness of God" (εἰς πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα τοῦ θεοῦ). This is one of the most ambitious prayers in the NT. It does not mean becoming divine — but it does mean a participation in God's life so complete that there is no area of human existence left untransformed by his presence. The doxology that follows (3:20–21) acknowledges this exceeds anything we can ask or imagine — which is precisely why it is a prayer, not a self-improvement programme.
Cross-Bible Links
← OT Background
Isaiah 57:19 · "Peace to those far and near"
Paul quotes this in Eph 2:17 — "he came and preached peace to you who were far away and peace to those who were near." Gentiles are the "far off" whom Isaiah promised God would bring near. The Jew/Gentile wall of ch.2 is the fulfilment of Isaiah's vision.
← OT Background
Psalm 68:18 · The Ascending Warrior
Paul applies Ps 68 to the ascension in 4:8 — "when he ascended on high he led captives in his train and gave gifts to people." The warrior-king who ascends after victory distributes the spoils. Christ's ascension is the basis for the distribution of gifts to the church.
← OT Background
Genesis 2:24 · One Flesh
Paul quotes the foundational marriage verse in 5:31 and immediately says "this is a profound mystery — I am talking about Christ and the church." Marriage was always meant to point beyond itself. The one-flesh union of husband and wife is an icon of the union between Christ and his church.
≈ Parallel · Colossians
Colossians 1:15–20 · The Cosmic Christ
Colossians is Ephesians' closest companion letter — probably written at the same time from the same imprisonment. Col 1 parallels Eph 1's cosmic Christology. Colossians focuses on Christ as the head of the cosmos; Ephesians on the church as his body. The two letters are the two sides of the same coin.
≈ Parallel · Romans
Romans 8:28–39 · Predestined, Called, Justified, Glorified
Romans 8 and Ephesians 1 are the two great election passages. Romans 8 presents the golden chain from predestination to glorification. Ephesians 1 presents the same scope but from the doxological angle — as praise, not argument. Both insist: election is in Christ, not apart from him.
≈ Parallel · 1 Corinthians
1 Corinthians 12–14 · Gifts and the Body
1 Cor 12 and Eph 4 both treat the body metaphor and spiritual gifts. But there is a shift: 1 Cor 12 focuses on diversity within the body (any gift, each member matters); Eph 4 focuses on specific leadership gifts for equipping the body to maturity. The two passages complete each other.
NT → Fulfilment
Revelation 21:1–4 · All Things New
Eph 1:9–10 — all things united in Christ — finds its ultimate fulfilment in Revelation's new creation. "Behold, I am making all things new." The mystery disclosed in Ephesians is the plan that Revelation reveals as complete. The church is the firstfruits (Eph 1:12–14) of what Revelation 21 shows in full.
NT → Application
John 17:20–23 · That They May Be One
Jesus's High Priestly Prayer is the foundation of the unity Ephesians describes. "May they be brought to complete unity to let the world know that you sent me." The one new humanity of Eph 2 is the answer to the prayer of John 17. The broken wall is the answer to "may they be one."
NT → Theme
Galatians 3:26–29 · Neither Jew nor Greek
"There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." Galatians 3:28 is the thesis that Ephesians 2:11–22 unpacks at length. The abolition of the dividing wall is the social expression of the cosmic mystery.
Commentary Shelf
Martyn Lloyd-Jones
Exposition of Ephesians — 8 volumes (Banner of Truth, 1974–1982)
Reformed · Welsh Expository
The most comprehensive expository series on Ephesians in English — 232 sermons preached at Westminster Chapel across twelve years. Lloyd-Jones treats every verse with doctrinal depth and pastoral warmth. His volumes on chapters 1–3 are among the finest works on the doctrine of salvation in print.
On 1:3–14 (the berakah): Lloyd-Jones argues the entire doxology must be read as the foundation before any application is attempted. "You cannot walk worthy of the calling if you do not know what the calling is. The whole of chapters 1–3 must be understood before a single command in chapters 4–6 can be obeyed with power."
On 2:8–9: "The most important word in the Christian gospel is the word 'gift.' Grace means gift. You contribute nothing to your salvation — not even the faith, which is itself given. To add anything — any human effort, any merit — is to destroy the entire structure."

John Stott
God's New Society: The Message of Ephesians (BST, IVP, 1979)
Anglican Evangelical · Pastoral
Stott's volume in the BST series remains one of the most accessible full treatments of Ephesians. His organising theme is that Ephesians is fundamentally about the church — specifically the church as "God's new society," the community that embodies the reconciliation of Jew and Gentile and makes the wisdom of God visible to cosmic powers.
On 2:11–22 (the dividing wall): Stott argues this is the pastoral heart of Ephesians and the most politically explosive passage in the NT. "The church is the only community on earth where the most ancient, deep, and bitter divisions among human beings are transcended — not by political negotiation but by the cross." He challenges churches to ask whether they actually look like what Eph 2 describes.
On the household codes: Stott is complementarian but insists the husband's command (self-sacrificial love as Christ loved) is the more demanding half. "The headship Paul describes is not dominance — it is the servant leadership of the one who gave himself up."
ivpress.com — God's New Society: The Message of Ephesians [VERIFY URL]
Peter O'Brien
The Letter to the Ephesians (PNTC, Baker/Eerdmans, 1999)
Conservative Evangelical · Exegetical
The standard conservative evangelical technical commentary on Ephesians. O'Brien provides careful Greek exegesis with theological depth. On authorship: firmly Pauline, with detailed responses to the pseudonymous-authorship arguments. Essential for anyone preaching the letter.
On 1:23 (the fullness question): O'Brien argues for the passive reading — the church is the one being filled by Christ, not the one completing him. "The church's task is not to fill a gap in Christ but to receive and express his presence. The fullness flows from him to it, not the reverse."
On 6:10–18 (the armour): O'Brien reads the conflict as genuinely cosmic — Paul has in view the structures of evil that stand behind human sin and suffering. "The Christian is not fighting people but the invisible powers that manipulate and distort human life. The armour is for that engagement, not for interpersonal conflict."

Tim Keller
Redeemer Sermon Series on Ephesians · The Meaning of Marriage (2011, Dutton) — draws heavily on Eph 5
Reformed Pastoral · Preaching
Keller's sermon series on Ephesians at Redeemer Presbyterian is among his most theologically rich preaching. On 2:1–10 (dead to alive): "You were not sick. You were not struggling. You were not just a bit confused. You were dead. And the only thing that can change a corpse is not a programme — it is resurrection power." He uses the deadness language to address the common Christian tendency to treat salvation as a self-improvement project.
On 2:14 (the dividing wall): Keller argues this is the most radical sociological claim in the ancient world. Jews and Gentiles — with two thousand years of mutual hostility — are made one new person in Christ. "The church is not a collection of people who happen to agree with each other. It is a community of people who have nothing in common except that they have all been saved by the same cross."
On Eph 5 and marriage (from The Meaning of Marriage): "The gospel — a husband loving sacrificially, a wife responding in trust — is not a picture Paul uses to illustrate marriage. Marriage is the picture God designed from the beginning to illustrate the gospel." The order of logic matters: the gospel illuminates marriage, not the other way round.
penguinrandomhouse.com — The Meaning of Marriage [VERIFY URL]
Clinton Arnold
Ephesians: Power and Magic (SNTSMS, 1989) · Ephesians (ZECNT, Zondervan, 2010)
Evangelical · Cultural-Historical
Arnold's distinctive contribution is the Ephesian cultural background. He demonstrates from archaeological and literary sources that Ephesus was the centre of magical arts and power religion in the ancient world. The city's famous "Ephesian letters" (amulets with magical formulae) and the book-burning of Acts 19 provide the backdrop for understanding why Paul's power language and armour passage would have registered so sharply with original readers.
On 1:20–23 (Christ above every power and authority): Arnold shows that Paul's list of cosmic powers corresponds precisely to the hierarchy of spiritual beings that Ephesian magical texts describe. Paul's claim is pointed: Christ is seated above every one of them. The believers' fear of cosmic powers — a live anxiety in Ephesus — is addressed by the fact that they are "in him" who is above them all.
zondervan.com — Ephesians, ZECNT [VERIFY URL]
Harold Hoehner
Ephesians: An Exegetical Commentary (Baker Academic, 2002)
Conservative Evangelical · Technical
At 900+ pages, Hoehner's commentary is the most exhaustive conservative treatment of Ephesians in English. Essential for detailed word studies, syntactical analysis, and engagement with the scholarly literature. His treatment of the Greek sentence structure of 1:3–14 (one sentence in Greek) is definitive.
On "in Christ" (en Christō): Hoehner catalogues all 36 occurrences and argues that the phrase consistently functions as a locative — describing the sphere in which God's blessings exist and in which the believer now lives. "The believer does not receive blessings and then later enter Christ — they receive the blessings by virtue of already being located in him."

Theme Thread
The put off/put on structure (4:22–24) is a baptismal pattern — the Christian life is a continual disrobing of the old and clothing in the new. The gifts in 4:11 are not status titles — they are equipping tools: the goal is that the body "builds itself up in love" (4:16). The measure of maturity is not individual heroism but corporate Christlikeness — "the whole body... grows and builds itself up in love."

Key Passage Deep Notes — The Eight Texts Every Preacher Needs
Ephesians 1:3–14
The Berakah — Blessing God for Every Spiritual Blessing
"Praise be to the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in the heavenly realms with every spiritual blessing in Christ."
One sentence in Greek (vv.3–14). Structure: three stanzas each ending "to the praise of his glory/grace" — Father elects (vv.3–6), Son redeems (vv.7–12), Spirit seals (vv.13–14). The phrase "in the heavenly realms" (ἐν τοῖς ἐπουρανίοις) — the sphere above earthly conflict where the believer is already located. Tense: past (we have been blessed, chosen, predestined) — not future gifts we are waiting for.
Key word: ἐκλέγω (eklegō) — "chose us in him before the foundation of the world." The election is in Christ — not abstract, not arbitrary. To be in Christ is to be in the one who was chosen before creation. The sequence: chosen (v.4) → predestined for adoption (v.5) → redemption and forgiveness (v.7) → sealed with the Spirit (v.13). All accomplished; the Spirit's sealing is the present guarantee.
Preaching angle: What does it mean to already possess "every spiritual blessing"? The first prayer (1:15–23) is that they would KNOW what they have. The problem is not poverty — it is ignorance of riches. Preach the gap between what believers possess and what they experience, and call them to know.
Ephesians 2:1–10
Dead in Sins → Made Alive by Grace → Created for Good Works
"But because of his great love for us, God, who is rich in mercy, made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in transgressions — it is by grace you have been saved."
"Dead" (νεκρός) — not sick, not struggling, not confused. Corpse-dead. No spiritual capacity, no self-generated movement toward God. The grammar of v.1 is deliberately stark — "and you were dead" — before any verb of divine action intervenes. Then "But God" (v.4) — the two most important words in the passage. Three past-tense verbs: made alive, raised, seated (vv.5–6). All in the aorist — completed, done, fixed.
The sequence of vv.8–10 is often misread. v.8–9: not by works. v.10: created FOR good works, prepared in advance. The logical flow: grace produces salvation which produces good works. Good works are the result, never the cause. "The God who saved us without our works then prepared works for us to walk in." Salvation is entirely God's; the life that follows is entirely God's gift — walking in works he already prepared.
Preaching angle: "But God" (v.4) — two words that changed everything. Preach the contrast: the full weight of the "dead in sins" section, then the explosive reversal. The people who feel spiritually dead need to hear that deadness is not the last word — it is the exact condition where resurrection power works.
Ephesians 2:11–22
The Dividing Wall Demolished — One New Humanity
"For he himself is our peace, who has made the two groups one and has destroyed the barrier, the dividing wall of hostility."
The "dividing wall" (τὸ μεσότοιχον τοῦ φραγμοῦ) — possibly a reference to the literal stone barrier in the Jerusalem Temple beyond which Gentiles could not pass on pain of death. An inscription from this wall has been found: "Let no Gentile enter within the balustrade... Whoever is caught will be responsible for his subsequent death." Paul says this barrier — physical and theological — has been demolished in the body of Christ. Not modified. Abolished (v.15 — καταργήσας).
The result: not two groups in peaceful coexistence but "one new humanity" (ἕνα καινὸν ἄνθρωπον — one new person). The church is a new ethnic category — not Jew, not Gentile, but in Christ. The building metaphor (vv.19–22): joined together, growing into a holy temple. Each local church is a construction site for this cosmic building project.
Preaching angle: What walls still stand in our church? The text does not allow the dividing wall to exist between cultural groups, economic classes, or racial categories in the body of Christ. Preach both the cosmic fact (wall demolished at the cross) and the local application (is our church a demonstration of this?).
Ephesians 3:14–21
The Second Prayer — Rooted, Grounded, Filled to the Fullness of God
"I pray that you, being rooted and established in love, may have power... to grasp how wide and long and high and deep is the love of Christ — and to know this love that surpasses knowledge."
Paul kneels (v.14) — unusual; most prayer in the ancient world was standing. The posture signals urgency and depth. He asks for five things: strengthening in the inner person (v.16), Christ dwelling in the heart through faith (v.17a), rootedness in love (v.17b), comprehension of the four dimensions of Christ's love (v.18), filling to all the fullness of God (v.19). Note the paradox: "to know this love that surpasses knowledge" — the goal is beyond cognitive achievement, only possible through the Spirit's strengthening.
The four dimensions: wide (embracing all peoples, Jew and Gentile, the scope of ch.2–3), long (through all time — before creation to consummation), high (raised to God's right hand — the exaltation of 1:20), deep (the descent — incarnation, death, "descended into the lower parts of the earth" 4:9). Christ's love has already mapped the entire universe. There is no corner of human experience it has not entered.
Preaching angle: The doxology (3:20–21) — "immeasurably more than all we ask or imagine." The word ὑπερεκπερισσοῦ (hyperekperissou) appears only here in the NT — "exceeding abundantly above and beyond." Preach that the ceiling of God's ability is infinitely above the floor of our asking. Our problem is not that we ask too much — it is that we ask too little.
Ephesians 4:1–16
Walk Worthy — Unity, Gifts, the Mature Body
"I urge you to walk in a manner worthy of the calling to which you have been called, with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love."
The "therefore" of 4:1 carries all of chapters 1–3. The calling (κλῆσις) was established in eternity (1:4), revealed in grace (2:8), and made known as mystery (3:3). The walk must be worthy of all that. The four virtues of v.2 — humility, gentleness, patience, bearing with one another — are not the foundations of church unity; they are the maintenance of a unity already given ("the unity of the Spirit," v.3 — it exists; we are to keep it). The seven unities of vv.4–6 ground the appeal.
The gifts of 4:11 are not spiritual gifts in the 1 Cor 12 sense — they are gifted persons given to the church: apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors-teachers. Their function: "equip his people for works of service, so that the body of Christ may be built up." The goal: every member doing ministry, growing to "the whole measure of the fullness of Christ" (v.13). The target is corporate maturity, not individual spiritual achievement.
Preaching angle: The church grows "as each part does its work" (v.16). The health of the body depends on every member functioning, not on the performance of professional ministers. Preach that every believer is a minister — the five-fold gifts exist to release the whole church into ministry, not to replace it.
Ephesians 5:15–21
Be Filled with the Spirit — The Driving Power of the Whole Walk
"Do not get drunk on wine, which leads to debaclery. Instead, be filled with the Spirit, speaking to one another with psalms, hymns, and songs from the Spirit."
The command "be filled with the Spirit" (πληροῦσθε ἐν πνεύματι) is present passive imperative — "keep being filled," continuously, repeatedly, by the Spirit's agency (passive). The contrast with drunkenness is instructive: both involve being under the control of something beyond yourself. The drunk is controlled by wine; the Spirit-filled person is controlled by the Spirit. The evidence of Spirit-filling: speaking (psalms, hymns), singing, making music, giving thanks, submitting to one another.
The fruit of Spirit-filling (vv.19–21) flows directly into the household codes (5:22–6:9). The Spirit-filled life is not primarily mystical — it is expressed in relationships: how spouses treat each other, how children honour parents, how workers serve. The most Spirit-filled room in the letter is the household at peace.
Preaching angle: How do you get filled? Not by trying harder but by yielding — speaking, singing, giving thanks. The filling comes as you orient yourself toward God in worship and toward others in service. The command is to be filled, not to fill yourself. Surrender to the One who fills is the posture.
Ephesians 5:22–33
Marriage as Mystery — The Gospel on Display
"'For this reason a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two will become one flesh.' This is a profound mystery — but I am talking about Christ and the church."
Paul's logic runs backward from theology to practice, not forward: marriage does not illustrate the gospel; the gospel illuminates what marriage was always designed to be. Gen 2:24 was always pointing to Christ and the church. This is why marriage matters so much — and why its distortion grieves so deeply. The husband as head: kephalē (head) in Paul's usage carries both authority and responsibility; the husband's model is Christ who "gave himself up for her" (v.25). The most demanding instruction in the passage is to the husband.
The sanctification language (vv.26–27) is remarkable: Christ cleanses the church "by the washing with water through the word." The husband's role includes a sanctifying function — his leadership should make the wife more holy, not less. The goal: "to present her to himself as a radiant church, without stain or wrinkle." The measure of faithful headship is the holiness of the one led, not the obedience of the one following.
Preaching angle: Every marriage is preaching a sermon. The question is whether it is preaching the gospel or a counterfeit. Preach both the beauty of the design and the weight of the responsibility — especially for husbands, whose model is the self-emptying of the cross.
Ephesians 6:10–20
The Full Armour of God — Stand in the Victory Already Won
"Finally, be strong in the Lord and in his mighty power. Put on the full armour of God, so that you can take your stand against the devil's schemes."
"Be strong in the Lord" — the verb (ἐνδυναμοῦσθε) is passive: be strengthened. Not self-generated power but received power. The same power language as 1:19–20 (the resurrection power). The armour is God's own armour — Isaiah 59:17 shows God himself wearing breastplate and helmet of salvation. The believer puts on what God has already worn. The struggle is "not against flesh and blood" (v.12) — the primary conflict is invisible before it becomes visible. Principalities, powers, world-rulers, spiritual forces — Paul's list corresponds to the hierarchies of the Ephesian magical world.
"Stand" (στῆτε) is the dominant verb — repeated in vv.11, 13, 14. The posture is not advance but holding. The ground won by Christ at the cross is what the believer defends. The armour is entirely defensive except the sword (the Word of God). All six pieces protect; only one cuts. This means the primary Christian posture in spiritual warfare is not aggressive conquest but faithful maintenance of a position already won.
Preaching angle: You are not fighting for victory — you are fighting from victory. The armour is given (you put it on; you did not make it). The ground is won (you stand; you do not conquer). The battle is real (Paul is in prison when he writes this). The outcome is certain (Christ is already seated above every power, 1:20–21). Stand.

F. F. Bruce

Conservative Evangelical · NT Scholar
Bruce's NICNT volume treats Ephesians alongside its companion letters, allowing illuminating comparison throughout. He holds Pauline authorship while engaging seriously with the critical arguments. On 1:9–10 (anakephalaiōsasthai — to recapitulate/unite): Bruce argues this verb carries the full weight of Irenaeus's concept of recapitulation — Christ as the second Adam who sums up and restores what the first Adam scattered. Charles Hodge
A Commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians (1856; Banner of Truth reprint · Free at CCEL)
Old Princeton Reformed · Classic Exegetical
Hodge's 19th-century commentary remains one of the best technical Reformed treatments of Ephesians available, and it is free online (CCEL). His strength is in the doctrine of grace (chs.1–2) and in precise syntactical analysis. On Eph 2:8: "This passage asserts not merely that faith is the means of salvation, but that faith itself is the gift of God... the faith by which we embrace Christ is not something we produce out of our own strength — it is the fruit of divine operation in the soul. On Eph 1:4 (election): Hodge argues strongly for individual unconditional election — "chosen before the foundation of the world, not because God foresaw we would be holy, but that we should be holy. Then → Now Bridge
Then — The Ephesian Church
Ephesus was a city of competing powers — the temple of Artemis, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world; the magical arts of Acts 19; the imperial cult; the social stratification of slave and free, Jew and Gentile. Paul writes to a church embedded in this context. His pastoral concern: that they know the full scope of what they have been given in Christ — power that exceeds every competing power, a new humanity that transcends every social division, a cosmic identity that puts the Roman emperor's claims in their proper (tiny) place.
Now — Modern Context
The Ephesian situation is not distant. Contemporary Christians live in cultures with competing powers (ideological, political, technological, spiritual), competing identities, and competing accounts of what makes human community possible. Ephesians addresses all of it: your identity is "in Christ" before it is in any social, ethnic, or political category. The church is God's demonstration project — the community that makes visible the unity-of-all-things-in-Christ that history is moving toward. The armour passage speaks to the reality that the battle is cosmic before it is personal.
Question to sit with: Does the church I am part of look like what Ephesians 2:11–22 describes — a community where the most ancient human divisions are actually being overcome — or does it look like a gathering of people who simply agree with each other?
Controlling Idea · The Final Verdict
"God's plan — hidden for ages, disclosed in Christ, embodied in the church —
is to unite all things in heaven and on earth under one head.
The church is not the audience for that plan. It is the demonstration of it."
Ephesians 1:9–10 · 3:10–11 — "His intent was that now, through the church, the manifold wisdom of God should be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly realms."
