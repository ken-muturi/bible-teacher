Proverbs 30 — the words of Agur
Wisdom literature, c. 10th–6th c. BC. An unknown (possibly non-Israelite) sage opens by confessing he cannot reach God's knowledge, then trusts God's word, prays for "just enough," and reads the created and moral order with humble, watchful eyes.

"The Poor You Will Always Have" — a reflection on the human condition
A contemporary meditation anchored on Matthew 26:11. It reads human lack as entropy — the drift of a world left untended — and argues that the opposite of poverty is not wealth but intentionality.
PROVERBS 30
The Core Claim
Wisdom begins by admitting its limits. We cannot climb up to God's knowledge (30:2–4), so we live within our size — trusting his word, asking for enough, watching the world without pride.
How It Works
It observes. The numerical "three… four" sayings train the eye to notice the never-satisfied, the small-but-wise, the self-exalting. Wisdom is attention to the real order of things.
The Human Problem It Names
Insatiable appetite and pride. The leech cries "Give! Give!" and is never full (30:15–16); "haughty eyes" and the self-exalting fool run through the chapter and are silenced at the end (30:13, 32).
Its Answer
The only prayer in Proverbs: "neither poverty nor riches, but… my daily bread" (30:8). Not more — enough; the intentional middle that keeps you honest and dependent.
Key Verse
"Give me neither poverty nor riches, but give me only my daily bread."
Proverbs 30:8 (NIV) — the hinge of the chapter
Its Sharpest Image
"Four things on earth are small, yet they are extremely wise" (30:24). The ant stores its food in summer — wisdom is foresight and intention, not size, strength, or wealth.
Shared Diagnosis
BothRead the human condition honestly — no utopia, no despair; the drift is real and must be actively resisted.
BothLocate the problem not in a lack of resources but in a failure to act wisely within our limits.
BothEnd in humility — a prayer, a question — not in a boast or a formula.

THE REFLECTION
The Core Claim
The opposite of poverty is not wealth but intentionality. A fallen world drifts toward lack by default; it is reversed only when we deliberately pour energy in.
How It Works
It observes too — everyday life. We all know books, exercise, study, and good conversation change us… "yet something in us keeps negotiating with tomorrow."
The Human Problem It Names
Drift. "Time doesn't preserve things; it reveals what we've stopped tending to." A parked car decays with no force on it at all. "If nothing changes, nothing changes."
Its Answer
Intentional stewardship: "little resources + intention = building with little; abundance − intention = drifting in plenty." Each, in time, gets its report card.
Key Line
"Maybe the opposite of poverty is not wealth — but intentionality."
The reflection's central turn · anchored on Matthew 26:11
Its Sharpest Image
The parked car, untouched for two years, slowly becoming a wreck — "no force on it, yet it is changing." Decay needs no effort; only order costs intention.
Not More — Enough / Intention
Prov 30:8–9: neither poverty nor riches. ↔ Reflection: poverty's opposite isn't wealth but intentionality. Both refuse the lie that more is the answer.
The Never-Satisfied
Prov 30:15–16: the leech's "Give! Give!" — things never satisfied. ↔ Reflection: "something in us keeps negotiating with tomorrow." Appetite that never says "enough."
Foresight vs. Drift
Prov 30:24–25: the ant stores food in summer. ↔ Reflection: "if nothing changes, nothing changes." Both make foresight — acting early — the mark of wisdom.
The Humble Speaker
Prov 30:2: "I am too stupid to be a man." ↔ Reflection: "This isn't me talking at anybody. I'm in this conversation too." Neither preaches from above.
The Through-Line
Both say the human problem is not that we lack enough, but that we drift unless we live on purpose — and the cure is not more but attention within our limits: Agur prays for it before God; the reflection urges it against the clock.
