The Book in One Sentence
Offense is the bait Satan uses to trap believers: whether the hurt was real or only perceived, harbouring it breeds bitterness and unforgiveness that quietly imprison the soul — and the only way out is the deliberate, unconditional choice to forgive.

The Hinge of the Whole Book — σκάνδαλον
σκάνδαλον
skandalon — the Greek word behind "offense"
Bevere builds everything on one word study. Skandalon originally named the bait-stick or trigger of an animal trap — the moving stick to which the bait is fixed, that springs the snare when touched. From it we get the English "scandal." Jesus warns that "offenses (skandala) will surely come" (Luke 17:1), and that in the last days "many will be offended" (Matt 24:10). Bevere's striking claim: offense is literally bait — Satan baits the trap, and the moment we "take" it (take the bait), the snare closes. The image is vivid, memorable, and the etymology is broadly legitimate (lexicons gloss skandalon as "a trap, a cause of stumbling").
But watch the bridge. In the New Testament the skandalon word-group usually means "a cause of stumbling into sin or falling away from faith" — not the modern English sense of "taking offense" (hurt feelings, umbrage). Matt 24:10's "many will be offended" most naturally means "many will fall away / apostatise." Bevere quietly maps the modern emotional sense onto the biblical word. There is real overlap — harboured hurt genuinely can lead to falling away — but the equation is looser than the book implies. This single move is the source of most of the book's strengths and most of its exegetical weaknesses.
The Argument — How the Book Moves
1
Offense is a trap, and no one is exempt
Jesus said offenses will come (Luke 17:1). The question is not whether we'll be offended but what we do with it. The bait is set; the only safety is in how we respond.
2
Two kinds of offended people — same prison
(a) Those genuinely wronged, and (b) those who only believe they were wronged. Bevere argues both end up in the same trap of bitterness — and that being right about the offense doesn't make the captivity any less real.
3
The offended hide it well — often behind religion
Hurt curdles into self-justification, withdrawal, gossip, and a wounded identity. Many don't even know they're offended. The people who hurt us most are usually those closest — family, friends, church leaders.
4
Unforgiveness is the lock — and it's spiritually lethal
Using the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matt 18) and Jesus' words on forgiveness, Bevere presses that refusing to forgive hands us over to "the tormentors" and severs our own experience of God's forgiveness.
5
The way out: forgive, release, and (where possible) reconcile
Forgiveness is a decision, not a feeling, and is required unconditionally. Bevere distinguishes forgiveness (unilateral, always commanded) from reconciliation (mutual, requires the other's repentance) — a genuinely helpful distinction. He calls readers to confront, forgive, and walk free, often using his own ministry wounds as illustration.
Key Concepts Worth Keeping
"Taking the bait"
The central metaphor: offense only traps you if you pick it up. The offense will come; the choice to seize and nurse it is yours. Memorable and genuinely clarifying.
Right but still imprisoned
Even a fully justified grievance can become a cage. Being the wronged party doesn't exempt you from the spiritual damage of harboured bitterness. A hard but important truth.
Forgiveness ≠ reconciliation
Forgiveness is unilateral and always commanded; reconciliation needs two parties and repentance. This distinction (clearer in later editions) prevents the book from demanding victims re-enter unsafe relationships.
Hidden / unconscious offense
Many carry offense without naming it — it leaks out as coldness, criticism, or spiritual dryness. Naming the unnamed wound is the book's real pastoral gift.
Wounded by the closest
The deepest offenses come from family, friends, and leaders — precisely because trust was highest. Realistic about where church people actually get hurt.
Forgiveness as decision
Not a feeling to wait for but a choice to make, then live into. Liberating for those stuck waiting to "feel" forgiving — though it can underplay the slow work of healing.

What It Gets Right
Names a real, under-addressed, community-destroying sin — harboured offense — with unusual directness.
Refuses to let "I was right" excuse bitterness; this is a genuinely countercultural and freeing message.
The bait/trap metaphor is vivid and sticky; readers remember it and use it on themselves.
Strong, clear, unconditional call to forgive — grounded in Matt 18 and the gospel of having been forgiven.
The forgiveness-vs-reconciliation distinction protects against the worst misreadings.
Self-implicating: Bevere indicts himself with his own ministry failures, not just others.
Accessible to anyone; pairs well with small-group discussion and has freed many from long-held resentment.
Where to Read With Care
Builds heavily on an English-word bridge: "offense" (emotional) ≠ biblical skandalon (cause of stumbling/apostasy). Proof-texting follows.
Can shift the burden onto the victim — telling the genuinely wronged that their pain is "Satan's bait" may silence legitimate grievance.
Under-distinguishes sinful bitterness from righteous lament, holy anger, and the pursuit of justice.
Thin on confronting sin, protecting the vulnerable, and church discipline — forgiveness is not the same as tolerating ongoing abuse.
Forgiveness can sound like sheer willpower; light on the slow, grief-laden process of healing.
Theologically popular rather than rigorous — limited grounding in union with Christ, the cross, and the Spirit's work.
Repetitive and anecdote-driven; the same point is pressed many times.
The Central Exegetical Question
Does "offense" in the Bible mean hurt feelings?
This is the question on which the book stands or falls. Modern English "offense" covers a spectrum: at one end, being emotionally hurt or insulted; at the other, being caused to stumble into sin or abandon faith. The Greek skandalon / skandalizō lives at the second end. When Jesus says it would be better to have a millstone hung around your neck than to "cause one of these little ones to stumble (skandalisē)" (Matt 18:6), he is not talking about hurting their feelings — he means causing them to fall into sin or away from God.
Bevere's book mostly addresses the first sense — the wounded, resentful, "I can't believe they did that to me" experience — but draws its biblical authority from texts about the second. The two are related (nursed offense really can lead someone to fall away — which is exactly Hebrews 12:15's "root of bitterness"), so the book is not simply wrong. But the clean identification "offense = the bait of Satan, proven by every NT use of skandalon" is more than the texts will bear. A careful reader keeps the pastoral insight (bitterness is a trap) while loosening the word-study certainty.
A Necessary Pastoral Caution
When this book is used as a weapon
Because The Bait of Satan tells the offended person "your offense is the real problem — deal with your own heart," it can be, and has been, misused by unhealthy leaders to silence legitimate complaint. An abuser or a controlling church culture can hand a victim this book to reframe their entirely justified protest as a spiritual failing ("you've taken the bait of Satan"), deflecting all accountability onto the wounded party.
The book's own forgiveness-vs-reconciliation distinction is the corrective: forgiveness releases the offender from your judgment; it does not require restoring an unsafe relationship, dropping a just accusation, or pretending no wrong occurred. Teach it alongside the Bible's strong commands to confront sin (Matt 18:15–17), protect the vulnerable, expose darkness (Eph 5:11), and seek justice (Micah 6:8). Forgiveness and accountability are not opposites. Read with that balance, the book heals; read without it, it can wound the already-wounded.
Read Alongside / Instead
Total Forgiveness
R. T. Kendall
A deeper, more theologically careful treatment of the same terrain — what forgiveness is and isn't, and the inner work of releasing offenders. The best companion to Bevere.
Forgive: Why Should I and How Can I?
Timothy Keller
Grounds forgiveness in the gospel and the cross with more rigour, and handles the justice dimension Bevere under-treats. Strong on "forgiveness vs. excusing."
The Peacemaker
Ken Sande
The practical manual for actually resolving conflict biblically — confrontation, mediation, reconciliation. Fills exactly the gap Bevere leaves on confronting sin.
Unoffendable
Brant Hansen
A lighter, more contemporary take arguing Christians have no right to hold onto anger. Pairs well thematically, though it raises its own questions about righteous anger.
Who Should Read It — and How
Reach for it if…
You're carrying a grudge you keep replaying and justifying.
You sense coldness or bitterness in yourself but haven't named its source.
You lead a small group and want an accessible, convicting study on forgiveness.
You know you were wronged — and you're ready to stop letting it own you.
Be cautious if…
You're a victim of abuse and someone is using this book to silence you — get the forgiveness/justice balance first.
You want careful exegesis; treat the word-study claims as illustration, not proof.
You're in a controlling church being told your concerns are "offense" — read it with Matt 18:15–17 open beside it.
You need help confronting a wrong, not just releasing it — pair with Sande's The Peacemaker.
Final Verdict
The Bait of Satan is a genuinely valuable, convicting book on a real and corrosive problem the church rarely addresses head-on: the offense we nurse and the bitterness it breeds. Its central insight — that harboured offense imprisons us regardless of who was right — has set many free. Read it for its pastoral punch and its memorable trap metaphor; read it critically where it presses a word study past its limits, and read it carefully where it could be turned into a tool for silencing the genuinely wronged. Take the medicine; leave the over-claim. 7.5 / 10.
