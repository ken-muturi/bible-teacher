A Righteousness That Exceeds the Pharisees — Why "More" Meant "Different"
Deep Study
⊕ The Bereans · testing by the Word The Thorns · Luke 8:14
Anchor Text
Matthew 5:20 · The kingdom's righteousness
"For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven."
Matthew 5:20 (ESV)
The setting: Jesus is early in the Sermon on the Mount. He has just insisted He came not to abolish the Law but to fulfil it (5:17–19). Then comes this shock: the entrance-requirement for the kingdom is a righteousness that surpasses that of the scribes and Pharisees — the most scrupulous, disciplined, rule-keeping people His hearers knew. This was not the low bar; it was the impossibly high one. So how could Jesus demand more? The six "you have heard it said… but I say" statements that follow (5:21–48) unpack the answer: He is not asking for a bigger pile of the same religion, but for a righteousness of a wholly different kind — one that reaches the heart, and one that, unlike the Pharisees, moves toward sinners in mercy rather than away from them in disdain.
Two Kinds of Righteousness — Why "Exceed" Meant More Than a Higher Score
The Pharisees' Righteousness
Real, disciplined — but external
▪Measured by conduct — tithing, fasting, Sabbath-keeping, ritual washing. Impressive and sincere.
▪Stops at the action: don't murder, don't commit adultery. The hands are clean.
▪Aimed at being seen — "to be praised by others" (6:1–2, 5, 16).
▪Built on comparison: "God, I thank you that I am not like other men" (Luke 18:11).
▪Produces separation — clean outside, dead men's bones within (23:27).
The Kingdom's Righteousness
Greater in kind — of the heart
▪Measured by the heart — the root of anger, lust, contempt, not just the deed (5:22, 28).
▪Reaches the motive: not merely no-murder but no-hatred; not no-adultery but no-lust.
▪Aimed at the Father who sees in secret (6:4, 6, 18) — not the crowd.
▪Built on mercy received: "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" (Luke 18:13).
▪Produces mercy that draws near — even to enemies and sinners (5:44–48).
Jesus is not asking for the Pharisees' religion plus a little more effort. He is asking for a righteousness of a different order altogether — one that goes deeper than behaviour to the heart, and one that no amount of rule-keeping can manufacture. That is precisely why it can only be received, not achieved (5:3, 6).
"You have heard it said… but I say to you"
In 5:21–48 Jesus shows what the deeper righteousness looks like — every time, driving the command from the hand into the heart.
Heard: Do not murder.
But I say: do not even nurse anger or contempt.
Heard: Do not commit adultery.
But I say: do not lust in the heart.
Heard: Keep your oaths.
But I say: be so true that your bare yes means yes.
Heard: Eye for an eye.
But I say: do not repay; turn the other cheek.
Heard: Love your neighbour, hate your enemy.
But I say: love your enemies, pray for those who persecute you.
The aim: "You therefore must be perfect…
…as your heavenly Father is perfect" (5:48) — His kind of righteousness.
The Tell-Tale Sign — Two Tables
The Pharisees' Table
"Pharisee" = pārûsh, "the separated one"
Gathered with the religious, the clean, the qualified.
Tense with watching — who touched what, who broke which rule.
Comparison in the air: rank, honour, who sits where.
Sinners kept out — a tax collector or "sinner" would defile it.
"This man receives sinners and eats with them" — said as an accusation (Luke 15:2).
Jesus' Table
"Friend of tax collectors and sinners" (Matt 11:19)
Reclining with tax collectors and sinners in Matthew's house (Matt 9:10).
At ease with mercy — the sick are why the Physician came (9:12).
Joy in the air: heaven parties over one sinner who repents (Luke 15:7).
Sinners welcomed in — Zacchaeus, the woman who wept, Levi.
"I desire mercy, and not sacrifice… I came to call sinners" (Matt 9:13).
Here is the sharpest evidence that the two righteousnesses differ in kind. The Pharisees' holiness was separating — it pulled them away from sinners, and their gatherings grew tense with rule-watching and rank. Jesus' holiness was redeeming — it moved toward sinners in mercy without ever excusing sin. A righteousness that cannot sit with a sinner is not yet the righteousness of the kingdom; the King Himself is known as their friend.
Word Studies — The Vocabulary of the Greater Righteousness
περισσεύσῃ
perisseusē — "exceed / abound"
5:20
To overflow, abound, surpass
The verb pictures overflowing, not merely out-scoring. It can mean "more in quantity," but in context it signals a righteousness that so surpasses the Pharisees' that it must be different in nature — an abundance welling up from within, not stacked up from without.
δικαιοσύνη
dikaiosynē — "righteousness"
5:6, 20
Righteousness / right-standing
In Matthew, the whole-life conformity to God's will that marks a kingdom citizen. It is both something you hunger and thirst for and are filled with (5:6) — received as gift — and something lived out from the heart. Not a résumé you build, but a life you're given and grow into.
Φαρισαῖος
Pharisaios — from pārash
5:20
"The separated ones"
The name likely derives from the Hebrew root "to separate." Their zeal for holiness expressed itself as separation from the unclean and the sinner. The irony Jesus exposes: a holiness whose instinct is to withdraw from sinners has misread the God who "desires mercy."
ὑποκριτής
hypokritēs — "hypocrite"
6:2; 23:13
Play-actor, one wearing a mask
A stage-actor speaking behind a mask. Jesus' repeated charge (esp. Matt 23) is that the Pharisees' righteousness was performance — the outside staged for an audience while the inside stayed unchanged. The greater righteousness has no audience but the Father who sees in secret.
ἔλεος
eleos — "mercy"
9:13; 23:23
Mercy, compassion
Twice Jesus throws Hosea 6:6 at the Pharisees: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." Mercy is the "weightier matter" they neglected (23:23). The surpassing righteousness is not measured chiefly by scruple but by mercy — the very thing their separating holiness squeezed out.
ἐδικαιώθη
edikaiōthē — "was justified"
Luke 18:14
Declared righteous by God
In the parable, it is the tax collector — beating his breast, pleading mercy — who "went down to his house justified," not the Pharisee. The righteousness that exceeds the Pharisees begins where the Pharisee refused to stand: empty-handed, crying for grace. It is first received, then lived.
Across the Canon — Heart Over Externals, Mercy Over Sacrifice
Hosea 6:6 · Micah 6:8
"I Desire Mercy, Not Sacrifice"
The prophets already exposed a religion of ritual without heart. "What does the LORD require… but to do justice, to love mercy, to walk humbly?" Jesus' demand is not a new standard but the old prophetic one, finally reaching bottom — the heart God always wanted.
Deuteronomy 10:16 · 30:6
Circumcise the Heart
The Law itself pointed past the outward mark to an inward change: "Circumcise the foreskin of your heart." External conformity was never the goal; a transformed heart was — exactly the righteousness the Pharisees stopped short of.
Jeremiah 31:33 · Ezekiel 36:26
A New Heart, the Law Within
"I will put my law within them… write it on their hearts"; "I will give you a new heart." The greater righteousness is covenant-promise: God does by His Spirit what rule-keeping never could. It is grace-given before it is lived-out.
Isaiah 64:6 · Philippians 3:9
Not My Own Righteousness
"All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment." Paul — himself once "as to righteousness under the law, blameless" (a Pharisee!) — counts it loss, to gain "the righteousness from God that depends on faith." The exceeding righteousness is Christ's, credited and then formed in us.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. If we're saved by grace, why does Jesus set the bar even higher than the Pharisees?
Because the raised bar is the very thing that drives us to grace. The Sermon opens with "blessed are the poor in spirit" (5:3) and "those who hunger and thirst for righteousness" (5:6) — it is addressed to the spiritually bankrupt and the starving, not the self-sufficient. By demanding a righteousness that reaches the heart — no anger, no lust, love of enemies — Jesus makes it impossible to feel we've "arrived," and so shuts us up to receiving righteousness as a gift (Phil 3:9). The high standard is not the opposite of grace; it is grace's doorway. The Pharisee's error was thinking the bar was low enough to clear on his own.
2. Were the Pharisees just hypocrites? Isn't this caricature unfair to sincere, devout people?
Many Pharisees were genuinely earnest and disciplined — that is exactly what makes them the hardest case and the most sobering example. Jesus does not say their righteousness was fake or worthless; He says it was insufficient and of the wrong kind. The danger He exposes is not gross wickedness but respectable religion that manages the outside while the heart stays proud, comparing, and merciless. That danger threatens the most committed believer, not the obvious sinner. The point isn't "look down on the Pharisees"; it's "beware becoming one" — which is why the warning lands hardest on church people.
3. You've made much of "tense meetings, but no sinners." Is that really in the text?
The phrase compresses a pattern woven through the Gospels. The very name "Pharisee" means "separated one," and their holiness worked by separation from the unclean. Again and again they grumble that Jesus "receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2; also Matt 9:11; Luke 7:39; 19:7), and Jesus repeatedly diagnoses their gatherings as anxious about rank, appearance, and rule-boundaries (Luke 11:43; 14:7; Matt 23:5–7) while neglecting "mercy" (Matt 9:13; 23:23). So yes: a righteousness that keeps sinners at arm's length and turns fellowship into rule-watching is precisely the righteousness Jesus says must be exceeded. The surpassing righteousness is unafraid to sit with sinners because it draws its holiness from mercy received, not distance maintained.
4. Doesn't Jesus eating with sinners mean sin doesn't matter much?
The opposite. Jesus welcomes sinners because sin matters supremely — He came as the Physician precisely for the sick (9:12), to "call sinners" and heal them, not to leave them as they are. His table is not indifferent to sin; it is where sin is forgiven and lives are turned around (Zacchaeus gives back fourfold; the sinful woman is transformed by love). The Pharisee's separation and Jesus' welcome are two responses to the same seriousness about sin: one quarantines the sinner, the other rescues him. The greater righteousness holds together what we tend to split — uncompromising truth about sin and open-armed mercy to the sinner.
5. Practically, how do I pursue this without either laxity or a new Pharisaism?
Watch both cliffs. The first is laxity — using "it's the heart that counts" as an excuse to obey less; but Jesus intensifies the commands, He doesn't relax them. The second, subtler cliff is a new Pharisaism — turning "heart righteousness" or "grace" into a fresh badge to feel superior about, now looking down on the "legalists." The safeguards are three: (1) start every day at the tax collector's spot — "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" — so righteousness stays received, not achieved; (2) let mercy, not scruple, be your measure of progress — are you becoming kinder to sinners, or more separated from them?; and (3) test the fruit at the table — a heart truly being changed by grace grows more welcoming to the very people the old heart avoided.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — Two Kinds of Holy People
The Pharisees were the devout — disciplined, orthodox, tithing to the herb. Yet their holiness separated: their gatherings grew tense with rule-watching and rank, and no "sinner" was welcome at their table. Jesus, holier still, sat down with tax collectors and prostitutes — and was slandered as "a friend of sinners." His righteousness didn't withdraw from the unclean; it went to heal them, while never calling their sin clean.
Now — Which Righteousness Is Ours?
It is fatally easy to build a respectable, rule-keeping faith that manages the outside, quietly compares itself to others, and keeps "those people" at a distance — church as a tense room of the qualified. Jesus says that righteousness, however sincere, will never enter the kingdom. The greater righteousness starts at the tax collector's cry for mercy, works from the heart outward, and can be told by one unmistakable fruit: it makes us safer, warmer people for sinners to be near.
Question to sit with: Is my holiness the kind that separates me from sinners and makes my fellowship tense with comparison — or the kind that, having received mercy, can sit at the table with them the way my Lord did?
