Anchor Text — Balaam's Third Oracle
Numbers 24:5 · "Mah tovu" — the hired curse turns to blessing
מַה־טֹּבוּ אֹהָלֶיךָ יַעֲקֹב מִשְׁכְּנֹתֶיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל
"How beautiful are your tents, O Jacob, your dwelling places, O Israel! Like valleys that stretch afar, like gardens beside a river, like aloes that the LORD has planted, like cedar trees beside the waters."
Numbers 24:5–6 (ESV)
The setting: Israel, near the end of the wilderness years, is camped on the plains of Moab. Terrified, King Balak of Moab hires Balaam — a renowned pagan diviner from the Euphrates — to curse Israel so he can drive them off (Num 22). Three times Balak builds altars and pays for a curse; three times God overrules Balaam's mouth and only blessing comes out. This verse is the opening of the third and highest oracle. By now Balaam has stopped looking for omens (24:1); "the Spirit of God" comes upon him, and looking down on Israel's tribes encamped tribe by tribe, he sings the most tender line in the whole episode — words so beautiful that Israel would one day borrow them to greet God in worship. The man sent to condemn God's people ends up describing their glory.
The Great Reversal — Hired to Curse, Compelled to Bless
What Balak Paid For
The intended curse
▪"Come, curse this people for me… perhaps I shall be able to drive them out" (22:6, 11).
▪Altars built, bulls and rams offered — three times, at three vantage points (23:1, 14, 29).
▪Fees for divination in hand; the machinery of pagan cursing fully engaged (22:7).
▪Balak's fury rises as each attempt backfires: "I called you to curse… and behold, you have blessed" (24:10).
What God Delivered
The unstoppable blessing
▪"How can I curse whom God has not cursed?" (23:8) — the diviner is disarmed.
▪"God is not man, that he should lie… he has blessed, and I cannot revoke it" (23:19–20).
▪"How beautiful are your tents, O Jacob!" — tenderness from a hostile mouth (24:5).
▪"Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you" (24:9) — the curse rebounds on the curser.
This is Genesis 12:3 dramatised on a Moabite hilltop: "I will bless those who bless you, and him who dishonours you I will curse." Balak spends a fortune to reverse God's promise and cannot move it an inch. What God has blessed, no one can curse — not the most gifted sorcerer, not the highest bidder, not the enemy on the heights.
The Four Oracles — Blessing That Climbs
23:7–10
Oracle 1 · Set apart & countless
"How can I curse whom God has not cursed?"
Israel is "a people dwelling alone" — set apart — and as numberless as the dust of Jacob. Balaam cannot curse what God has not cursed. The promises to Abraham (a distinct nation, innumerable seed) are already secure.
23:18–24
Oracle 2 · Secure & unbreakable
"God is not man, that he should lie"
No blemish is charged against Jacob; "there is no enchantment against Jacob, no divination against Israel." The LORD their God is with them; He brought them out with strength like a wild ox. The blessing rests on God's unchanging word, not Israel's record.
24:3–9
Oracle 3 · Beautiful & flourishing
"How beautiful are your tents, O Jacob!"
Now the Spirit of God comes on Balaam and he drops divination altogether. He sees Israel's ordered camp as gardens beside a river, cedars beside the waters — watered, fruitful, kingly. Beauty, not defect, is what the God's-eye view reveals.
24:15–19
Oracle 4 · A King is coming
"A star shall come out of Jacob"
The blessing crests in prophecy: "a star shall come out of Jacob, and a sceptre shall rise out of Israel." A royal deliverer is coming — read by Jews and Christians alike as messianic, pointing beyond David to the King born under a star (Matt 2).
The View from the Heights
What Balaam Came to See — and What the Spirit Showed Him
Balak took Balaam to high places to look down on Israel and curse (22:41; 23:13, 28).
He came to see
A wilderness rabble — ex-slaves to be cursed and scattered; a threat crowding Moab's border, ripe for a hex.
→
The Spirit showed him
Tribes encamped in ordered ranks (cf. Num 2) — tents spread like well-watered gardens and river-fed cedars, a people planted by the LORD.
The same encampment looked like a threat to curse or a garden to admire, depending on whose eyes did the looking. Israel's beauty was not in their merit — the golden calf, the grumbling, and the coming disaster at Baal-Peor (ch. 25!) were all real. Their beauty was that God dwelt in their midst and had bound Himself to bless them. Grace, not performance, is what the view from the heights reveals.
Word Studies — The Vocabulary of Blessing
מַה־טֹּבוּ
mah-tovu — "how good/beautiful!"
24:5
An exclamation of delight
From tov, the "good" of Genesis 1 — goodness, beauty, and blessedness all at once. Not cool assessment but an outburst of wonder: how good, how lovely! The word that opens creation's praise now opens praise of God's people.
אֹהֶל
ohel — "tent"
24:5
Dwelling, household
The ordinary tent of a nomad family — and the same word used for the Tent of Meeting. Balaam praises the humble homes of a pilgrim people; their beauty is that at the centre of all those tents stands God's own Tent, His presence among them.
מִשְׁכָּן
mishkan — "dwelling place"
24:5
Dwelling — the Tabernacle word
"Your mishkenotecha" is the very term for the Tabernacle, God's dwelling, and the root of Shekinah, His dwelling glory. A deliberate echo: Israel's dwellings are beautiful because the God who dwells (shakan) has pitched His tent among them.
בָּרַךְ
barak — "to bless"
23:11,20; 24:1
To bless, empower, favour
The word that dominates the story, set against arar/qabab ("curse"). Balaam confesses, "I received a command to bless: he has blessed, and I cannot revoke it" (23:20). A blessing God speaks is irreversible by any human power.
נְאֻם
neʾum — "oracle / utterance"
24:3, 4, 15
A solemn divine declaration
"The oracle of Balaam… the oracle of the man whose eye is opened." The word normally introduces a prophet's "Thus says the LORD." Even a pagan diviner, when God takes his mouth, can only deliver God's declaration — nothing of his own.
כּוֹכָב
kokhav — "star"
24:17
Star — a royal, messianic sign
"A star shall come out of Jacob." In the ancient world a star heralded a king's birth. The blessing crests in a coming royal deliverer — read messianically in Judaism (and later invoked at Bar Kokhba, "son of the star") and fulfilled, for Christians, in the King sought by a star (Matt 2:2).
Across the Canon — The Blessing That Cannot Be Cursed
Genesis 12:3 · 27:29
"I Will Bless Those Who Bless You"
Balaam's climax (24:9) quotes the Abrahamic promise almost word for word. The whole episode is that promise proving itself: the nations' hostility recoils, and the curse falls on the curser, not the blessed.
Deuteronomy 23:4–5 · Neh 13:2
"The LORD Turned the Curse into a Blessing"
Israel is later taught to remember Balaam precisely this way: God "turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the LORD your God loved you." The Balaam story becomes a permanent testimony to covenant love.
Numbers 25 · 31:16
The Sting in the Tale
Unable to curse Israel, Balaam later advises Balak to seduce them into sin at Baal-Peor (31:16) — and thousands fall. What the enemy cannot do from outside by cursing, he attempts from inside by temptation.
2 Peter 2:15 · Jude 11 · Rev 2:14
"The Way of Balaam"
The New Testament makes Balaam a byword for the hireling prophet who loved "the wages of unrighteousness" and taught others to sin. The mouth that blessed and the heart that betrayed are held apart as a solemn warning.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Was Balaam a true prophet? How could God speak through a pagan diviner?
Balaam is a genuinely ambiguous figure — a renowned diviner-for-hire from the Euphrates who nonetheless really hears from Israel's God and, when the Spirit seizes him, speaks true prophecy. Scripture does not hold this up as normal; it holds it up as a marvel: God is so sovereign that He can commandeer even a hostile, mercenary mouth to bless His people. The point is not "pagan diviners are reliable" but "God's word is unstoppable — He can make the very man hired to curse pronounce the blessing." Later Scripture leaves no doubt about Balaam's true heart (see the next point).
2. If Balaam blessed Israel and even prophesied the Messiah, why is he condemned across the New Testament?
Because his gift and his heart ran in opposite directions. Prevented from cursing Israel directly, Balaam found another way to hurt them: he counselled Balak to lure them into idolatry and immorality at Baal-Peor (Num 31:16), and thousands died in the plague (Num 25). So the NT brands "the way of Balaam" (2 Pet 2:15), "Balaam's error" (Jude 11), and "the teaching of Balaam" (Rev 2:14) as the pattern of the religious professional who trades truth for money and leads God's people into sin. It is a sobering case study: a man can speak God's word accurately, even beautifully, and still be lost. Utterance is not the same as salvation.
3. How can Israel be called "beautiful," with "no iniquity in Jacob" (23:21), when they were rebellious — and about to sin grievously in the very next chapter?
This is the heart of the passage's grace. Numbers is brutally honest about Israel's failures — the calf, the spies, the grumbling, and Baal-Peor is literally the next scene. So the "beauty" and the "no iniquity charged against Jacob" are not a verdict on their performance; they are the God's-eye view of a covenant people He has chosen to bless and dwell among. It is imputed status, not achieved merit — the LORD looks on His people and, because of His own promise and presence, declares them lovely. Christians will hear an anticipation of justification: God sees His people robed in a righteousness that is His gift, not their record (cf. Rom 4; the same grace that saw beauty in a wilderness camp sees it in the church).
4. Is "a star shall come out of Jacob" (24:17) really about the Messiah?
The oracle's first horizon is royal and Davidic — a king who would subdue Moab and Israel's enemies (fulfilled in part by David). But its language of a rising star and sceptre carried a forward charge that both Jewish and Christian readers extended to the coming King. Ancient Judaism read it messianically (the rebel leader Simon was hailed as "Bar Kokhba," son of the star); the Dead Sea community cited it; and the Gospel of Matthew frames Jesus' birth around a star and Gentile seekers who come to worship the newborn King (Matt 2). Read canonically, Balaam's blessing crests in Christ — the ultimate reason God's people can never finally be cursed.
5. What does this passage actually promise me — is my life immune to all "curses"?
Not immunity from hardship — Israel still faced battles, discipline, and the real consequences of sin. What it promises is that no hostile power, human or spiritual, can reverse God's settled purpose to bless those who are His (cf. Rom 8:31–39, "if God is for us, who can be against us?… neither death nor life… shall separate us"). The story also flags where the real danger lies: not in the enemy's curse from outside (which fails), but in the enemy's seduction from within (Baal-Peor, which succeeds). So the comfort and the warning come together — rest in a blessing no one can revoke, and guard against the temptation that succeeds where cursing cannot.

Afterlife of the Text
The Curse That Became a Welcome
There is a final irony worth savouring. The words a hired sorcerer spoke while trying to curse Israel — "Mah tovu ohaleicha Ya'akov" — became, in Jewish tradition, the opening line of the prayer recited on entering the synagogue: "How lovely are your tents, O Jacob, your dwelling places, O Israel." To this day, worshippers cross the threshold of the house of God with Balaam's blessing on their lips. The enemy's intended curse was so thoroughly turned that it now greets God's people at the door of worship — a standing monument to the truth that what God blesses, no one can curse.
Then → Now Bridge
Then — A Blessing on the Heights
On a Moabite ridge, a king paid a fortune for a curse, and a famous seer strained three times to deliver it. Each time, God took his mouth and blessing came out — until the diviner was gazing down on a wilderness camp and calling it a garden. Israel never even knew the drama was happening; they were simply loved, and kept, and blessed, over their unwitting heads.
Now — Kept Over Our Heads
Much of the warfare over God's people happens where they never see it — on the heights, in accusations and schemes aimed to undo them. This passage says the outcome is already settled: the God who dwells among His people has bound Himself to bless them, and no accuser, however gifted or determined, can reverse His word. Our beauty before Him rests not on our record but on His presence and promise — secured, finally, in the Star that rose out of Jacob.
Question to sit with: Do I live as one whose blessing is settled by God's promise and God's presence — or as one still afraid that some enemy, failure, or curse could undo what God has spoken over me?
Controlling Idea
"Hired to curse and compelled to bless, Balaam proves that what God has blessed no power can curse — and that His people's beauty rests not on their record but on the God who dwells in their midst and the King who would rise, like a star, out of Jacob."
