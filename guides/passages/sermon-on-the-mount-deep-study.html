The Whole Discourse in One Frame
Matthew 5:1–2 & 7:28–29 · The frame around the whole Sermon
"Now when Jesus saw the crowds, he went up on a mountainside and sat down. His disciples came to him, and he began to teach them…"  →  "When Jesus had finished saying these things, the crowds were amazed at his teaching, because he taught as one who had authority, and not as their teachers of the law."
Matthew 5:1–2; 7:28–29 (NIV) — the opening and closing brackets of the discourse
What it is: The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) is the first and longest of the five great teaching discourses around which Matthew structures his Gospel — the manifesto, or charter, of the kingdom of heaven. Jesus goes up a mountain and sits (the posture of an authoritative teacher), deliberately echoing Moses at Sinai: here is a new and greater Moses giving the law of the new covenant, not on tablets but from a transformed heart. It is one sustained sermon with a clear arc — who kingdom people are (ch 5), how they live before their Father (ch 6), and how they must respond (ch 7) — bracketed by the crowds' arrival and their astonishment at an authority no rabbi had ever claimed.
The Grand Arc — One Sermon, Three Movements
Chapter 5
Character
Who the citizens of the kingdom are: the blessed (Beatitudes), salt & light, and a righteousness of the heart that surpasses the Pharisees' — the six antitheses driving the law inward.
→
Chapter 6
Heart
How they live before God: devotion for the Father's eyes not the crowd's (giving, praying, fasting), the Lord's Prayer, treasure in heaven, and freedom from anxiety — seek first the kingdom.
→
Chapter 7
Response
How they must respond: judge rightly, ask-seek-knock, the Golden Rule — then the two-ways finale that forces a verdict. Not hearing only, but doing: build on the rock.
Grace to obedience: it opens with blessing on the empty-handed (5:3) and ends with a summons to do what has been heard (7:24). Gift first — then the life the gift makes possible.
The Sermon at a Glance — Full Outline
5:1–2
Setting — Jesus ascends the mountain, sits, and teaches his disciples with the crowds listening.
5:3–12
The Beatitudes — Eight blessings on the unlikely: the poor in spirit, mourners, meek, hungry, merciful, pure, peacemakers, persecuted.
5:13–16
Salt & Light — Kingdom influence: stay distinct, don't be hidden, let works bring glory to the Father.
5:17–20
Christ & the Law — Not to abolish but fulfil; a righteousness that must surpass that of the scribes and Pharisees.
5:21–48
The Six Antitheses — "But I say to you": anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, enemies — the law driven to the heart.
6:1–18
Three Acts of Devotion — Giving, praying, fasting — for the Father who sees in secret, not for human applause. (Lord's Prayer, 6:9–13.)
6:19–24
Treasure, Eye, Master — Store treasure in heaven; a sound eye; you cannot serve God and money.
6:25–34
Do Not Worry — The Father feeds birds and clothes lilies; seek first the kingdom and trust him with tomorrow.
7:1–6
Judging — Remove your own plank first; then see clearly. Discern, but do not condemn.
7:7–11
Ask, Seek, Knock — Keep asking; the Father gives good gifts to his children.
7:12
The Golden Rule — Do to others as you would have them do to you — "this is the Law and the Prophets."
7:13–27
The Two-Ways Finale — Two gates, two trees, two claims, two builders — the demand to hear and do.
7:28–29
Response of the Crowds — Astonished, because he taught with authority — "and not as their scribes."
Threads That Bind the Whole Sermon
The Kingdom of Heaven
5:3, 10, 19–20 · 6:10, 33 · 7:21
The controlling reality. It opens and closes the Beatitudes, is prayed for ("your kingdom come"), sought first, and entered only by those who do the Father's will. Everything is life under God's reign.
"Your Father"
5:16, 45, 48 · 6:1–18, 26, 32 · 7:11, 21
Used ~17 times across the Sermon. God is not a distant lawgiver but the disciples' Father — who rewards in secret, feeds the birds, gives good gifts, and whose family likeness (love, mercy, perfection) they are to bear.
Righteousness (dikaiosynē)
5:6, 10, 20 · 6:1, 33
The Sermon's measure. Hungered for, suffered for, required to surpass the Pharisees', not to be paraded, and sought first with the kingdom. Both inward heart and outward doing — never one without the other.
The Two Audiences / Two Rewards
6:1–18 · cf. 5:12, 46 · 6:19–21
Every act is done before one of two audiences — people or the Father — and earns one of two rewards: shallow applause now, or treasure in heaven. The invisible motive decides the eternal outcome.
Inside vs Outside
5:21–48 · 6:1–18 · 7:15–23
The recurring exposure of the gap between surface and heart: murder/anger, adultery/lust, devotion/motive, sheep's clothing/inward wolves, "Lord, Lord"/doing the will. God reads the inside.
Grace, Then Obedience
5:3 → 7:24 · 7:7–11
The deep logic: it begins with blessing on the poor in spirit (pure gift) and ends demanding obedience (5:24, 7:24) — with "ask and it will be given" in between. Grace grounds the demand; the demand never grounds the grace.
The Great Brackets — How the Sermon Frames Itself
Two inclusios hold the discourse together
5:17
"I have not come to abolish the Law or the Prophets… but to fulfil them."
⟷ frames the body ⟷
7:12
"Do to others… for this sums up the Law and the Prophets."
5:3
"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."
⟷ opens & closes ⟷
7:21
"Not everyone who says 'Lord, Lord' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does…"
The phrase "the Law and the Prophets" (5:17; 7:12) brackets the entire ethical core: everything in between is what the fulfilled Law looks like, distilled into the Golden Rule. And the "kingdom of heaven" frames the whole from the first beatitude to the final warning — beginning as sheer gift to the empty-handed, ending as entrance reserved for those who actually do the Father's will. Read the brackets and you have the Sermon's argument in miniature: the Law, fulfilled in love, lived out from a kingdom given by grace.
Six Key Words Spanning the Sermon
βασιλεία
basileia tōn ouranōn
"kingdom of heaven"
The reign / rule of God
Matthew's reverent phrasing (avoiding "God"). Not mainly a place but God's active reign — already breaking in with Jesus, not yet complete. The Sermon is the constitution of life under that reign.
δικαιοσύνη
dikaiosynē
5:6,10,20 · 6:1,33
Righteousness / justice
The unifying ethical word — the right-ness that must exceed the Pharisees', be hungered for, and be sought first. Inseparably personal holiness and public justice; the character of the Father reproduced in his children.
πατήρ
patēr
~17× across 5–7
Father
The relational key. The ethic of the Sermon is not rule-keeping but family likeness: children who reflect a Father who loves enemies, sees in secret, and provides. Remove the Father and the Sermon becomes crushing law.
μακάριος
makarios
5:3–11 · the overture
Blessed / flourishing
The Sermon's first word about people — a pronouncement of God-given flourishing over the least likely. It sets everything in a key of grace before a single command is given: blessing precedes demand.
μισθός
misthos
5:12,46 · 6:1–18
Reward / wages
Runs through the "two audiences" teaching. Not payment earned but the natural harvest of where you invest: applause spent now, or the Father's reward stored in heaven. Motive determines which you receive.
ποιέω
poieō
7:12,21,24,26 · "do"
To do / make / practise
The verb of the finale. The Golden Rule is to be done; only the one who does the Father's will enters; the wise builder does Jesus' words. Hearing is assumed; doing is the dividing line.
How the Church Has Read the Sermon — Six Approaches
1. The Absolute Standard
much of the early church; monastic ideal
Take it plainly and literally as binding on all disciples now. Its rigor drove some toward monasticism or a "two-tier" ethic (counsels for the few, precepts for the many).
2. Law That Drives to Grace
Luther; much Reformation reading
Its impossible demands function to crush self-righteousness and expose our need — a mirror that sends us to Christ for mercy. Real ethic, but first a diagnosis.
3. Interim Ethic
Schweitzer & liberal scholarship
An emergency ethic for the short interval before an imminent end that (they held) never came. Widely rejected by evangelicals, but influential in critical study.
4. Kingdom Ethic for Now
Anabaptist; Bonhoeffer; Yoder
The concrete way of life for Jesus' community today — non-retaliation, enemy-love, and truthfulness meant to be actually practised, not spiritualised away.
5. Dispensational / Future
classic dispensationalism
Primarily the law of a future millennial kingdom, with principled application now. Preserves the plain sense but risks deferring its present claim.
6. Grace-Rooted Discipleship
Stott, Lloyd-Jones, Carson, Pennington
The dominant evangelical reading (adopted here): a description of the Spirit-shaped life of those already in the kingdom by grace — genuinely binding, genuinely gracious, pursued in dependence.
Commentary Shelf — The Sermon as a Whole
John Stott
The Message of the Sermon on the Mount (BST, 1978)
Evangelical · Pastoral
Reads the whole Sermon as the "Christian counter-culture" — a portrait of the kingdom community whose values, righteousness, devotion, ambitions, and relationships are all distinct from both the world and merely nominal religion.
Its unity: the disciple is called to be different — and the difference is essentially the character of the Father worked out in every sphere of life.
ivpress.com — The Message of the Sermon on the Mount [VERIFY URL]
D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones
Studies in the Sermon on the Mount (1959–60)
Reformed · Preaching
The classic devotional exposition: the Sermon is "a description of what every Christian is meant to be." Not entry conditions but the necessary character of the regenerate — and unliveable apart from the new birth and the Spirit.
Insists the order matters: the Beatitudes (what we are) precede the commands (what we do). You cannot reverse them without turning grace into law.
eerdmans.com — Studies in the Sermon on the Mount [VERIFY URL]
Jonathan T. Pennington
The Sermon on the Mount and Human Flourishing (2017)
Evangelical Scholarly
Reframes the Sermon as Jesus' answer to the ancient question of the good life (eudaimonia): true human flourishing (makarios) is found in whole-hearted, God-oriented "perfection" (teleios) — integrity of the whole self before God.
Argues "be perfect" (5:48) means whole/undivided, and that the whole Sermon presses toward this singleness of heart — a bridge between biblical faith and the wisdom tradition.
bakeracademic.com — The Sermon on the Mount and Human Flourishing [VERIFY URL]
Dietrich Bonhoeffer
The Cost of Discipleship (1937)
Lutheran · Discipleship
The most searching modern reading: the Sermon is the call of Christ to costly, concrete obedience. Against "cheap grace" that admires the words and evades them, Bonhoeffer insists they are given to be done — "only the doers believe."
Enemy-love and non-retaliation are not ideals but the visible extraordinariness (perisson) of the disciple, rooted in the cross.
fortresspress.com — The Cost of Discipleship [VERIFY URL]
D. A. Carson
Jesus' Sermon on the Mount (1978)
Conservative Evangelical
Guards against the twin errors of treating the Sermon as an unreachable ideal (so we ignore it) or as bare law (so we grow proud). It sets a standard that drives us to grace and then genuinely governs kingdom life.

Augustine of Hippo
Our Lord's Sermon on the Mount (c. 393)
Patristic
The first full commentary; coined the very title "Sermon on the Mount." Calls it "a perfect standard of the Christian life" and reads it as an ordered ascent — Beatitudes, petitions, and gifts of the Spirit climbing toward the vision of God.

The Big Questions About the Sermon as a Whole
1. Is the Sermon law or gospel — demand or grace?
Both, in the right order. It opens with gospel — blessing pronounced on the empty-handed poor in spirit (5:3), pure gift — and only then issues its towering demands. Its centre offers a Father who gives good gifts to those who ask (7:7–11). So it is neither bare law (which crushes) nor grace without content (which excuses). It is the shape of life for people already brought into the kingdom by grace: gospel that grounds a genuine, Spirit-enabled ethic. Preached as law alone it drives to despair; preached as grace alone it evaporates into sentiment. Held together, it is the charter of discipleship.
2. Can anyone actually live it — or is it an unreachable ideal?
Taken by willpower, no one can (that is partly the point — it strips self-righteousness bare). But it is not therefore an unreachable ideal to be admired and shelved. Jesus expected his community to do it (7:24), and the New Testament church took it as normative (James is the Sermon as a letter). The resolution is the first beatitude and 7:7: the life is received, not achieved — pursued in poverty of spirit, asked for, and worked in by the Spirit. We live it truly, if never yet perfectly, and grow toward the "completeness" (5:48) it describes.
3. Is it for individuals, the church, or society at large?
Primarily it is addressed to disciples (5:1–2) — the character of those who follow Jesus, lived first personally and in the church community. That is why applying, say, "turn the other cheek" directly as national foreign policy is contested (see the various historic approaches above). Yet its salt-and-light vision (5:13–16) means this distinct community is meant to influence the world, and its justice concern has public reach. Best read: a personal and ecclesial ethic with outward, society-shaping consequences — not a civil code, but not privately quietist either.
4. How does the Sermon relate to the rest of the New Testament, especially Paul?
Some pit "Jesus' ethics" against "Paul's grace," but the tension is overdrawn. The Sermon begins with grace (blessing to the poor in spirit) and Paul ends with ethics (Rom 12–15 reads like the Sermon applied — bless persecutors, don't retaliate, love fulfils the law). Both hold grace and obedience together in the same order: God's gift first, the transformed life second. James 1–5 is the clearest bridge, echoing the Sermon throughout. The whole New Testament sings the same tune — indicative before imperative, gift before demand.
5. Where is Jesus himself in the Sermon?
Everywhere — and this is easy to miss. He never says "thus says the LORD"; he says "but I say to you" (5:22ff) and stakes eternal destiny on "these words of mine" (7:24) and on whether he "knew" you (7:23). The crowds catch it: he teaches with an authority the scribes never had (7:29). So the Sermon is not first a program to implement but a person to reckon with. And read canonically, the one who commands this perfect righteousness is the one who came to be it and to give it — the Beatitudes are His own portrait before they are ours.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — A New Moses on a New Mountain
To a people who defined godliness by scrupulous external observance under scribal tradition, Jesus climbs a mountain like Moses and redraws everything from the inside. Blessing belongs to the empty-handed; righteousness is a matter of the heart; devotion is for the Father's eyes; and the whole thing rests on his authority — "but I say to you." The crowds go home astonished, having met not just a teacher but a claim.
Now — The World's Favourite Sermon
No text is more admired and less obeyed. Gandhi loved it; nations quote it; and most of us have quietly decided it can't really be meant. But it still measures the distance between managed behaviour and a changed heart, still calls the church to be a visible counter-culture, and still ends with a storm that tests every house. The Sermon will not let us stay admirers. It asks for builders.
Question to sit with: Across all three chapters, what is the one thing Jesus has clearly said that I have long admired — and never yet done?
