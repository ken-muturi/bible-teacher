The Text — James's Judgment
Acts 15:13–18 · The decisive speech
"When they finished, James spoke up. 'Brothers, listen to me. Simon has described to us how God first intervened to choose a people for his name from the Gentiles. The words of the prophets are in agreement with this, as it is written: "After this I will return and rebuild David's fallen tent. Its ruins I will rebuild, and I will restore it, that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, even all the Gentiles who bear my name, says the Lord, who does these things" — things known from long ago.'"
Acts 15:13–18 (NIV) — quoting Amos 9:11–12
The setting: Acts 15 records the first great doctrinal crisis of the church. Some taught that Gentile converts had to be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be saved (15:1, 5) — a question that struck at the heart of the gospel and threatened to split the young church in two. A council gathered in Jerusalem. After "much debate" (15:7), three testimonies were heard: Peter reported how God had given his Spirit to Gentiles (15:7–11); Paul and Barnabas recounted the signs God did among the nations (15:12); and then James — the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church — rose to give the decisive judgment. His method is the model: he listened to what God had done, and then tested it against what God had said. "The words of the prophets," he declared, "are in agreement with this, as it is written…" The most important decision the early church ever made was settled by opening the Scriptures.
James's Method — How the Decision Was Actually Made
Step 1 — Attend to God's acts
Listen to the testimony
James takes Peter's report seriously: God has visibly saved Gentiles and given them the Spirit. Experience is heard honestly — not dismissed, not made the final word.
→
Step 2 — Test it by Scripture
"The prophets agree… as it is written"
He does not settle it by the experience itself, by his own authority, or by a vote. He asks: does the written word confirm this? He turns to Amos 9 — and it does.
→
Step 3 — Reach a judgment
"Therefore it is my judgment…"
Grounded in the word, he renders a decision (15:19): don't burden the Gentiles — with a few pastoral requests for the sake of fellowship (15:20).
The order is everything: God's work was interpreted by Scripture, not Scripture bent to fit the experience. Experience raised the question; the written word answered it.
Three Testimonies — and Which One Settled It
Peter
Acts 15:7–11
"God, who knows the heart, showed that he accepted them by giving the Holy Spirit." Firsthand testimony to God's saving act among the Gentiles (Cornelius, ch.10). Salvation is "through the grace of our Lord Jesus," not the law.
Paul & Barnabas
Acts 15:12
They recount "the signs and wonders God had done among the Gentiles through them." Corroborating evidence of God's own initiative — the whole assembly falls silent to listen.
James — from Scripture
Acts 15:13–18
He weighs the testimonies and then anchors the verdict in the prophets: Amos foretold exactly this — a rebuilt house of David that gathers the nations. The Scripture is what makes the case decisive.
Structure of James's Speech
15:13–14
Movement 1 — The evidence weighed
"Listen… God has taken a people for his name"
James summarises Peter's report in weighty covenant language: God has "visited" the Gentiles to take from them "a people for his name" — words once reserved for Israel now applied to the nations. The fact is settled; the question is how to read it.
15:15
Movement 2 — The turn to the text
"The words of the prophets agree with this"
The hinge of the whole speech. Not "this feels right," or "God is doing a new thing beyond Scripture," but: the experience agrees with the written word. He appeals to "the prophets" (plural) — this is the settled testimony of Scripture, not a proof-text.
15:16–18
Movement 3 — The Scripture cited
Amos 9:11–12 — The Rebuilt Tent Gathers the Nations
James quotes Amos: God will rebuild David's fallen tent "that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, even all the Gentiles who bear my name." The inclusion of the nations is not an improvisation; it was "known from long ago." Scripture always planned this.
15:19–21
Movement 4 — The judgment
"It is my judgment that we should not burden them"
The conclusion drawn from the word: don't impose circumcision and the law on Gentile believers. Add only a few requests (abstain from idol-food, blood, strangled meat, sexual immorality) so Jew and Gentile can share table and fellowship without needless offence.
Word Studies — The Vocabulary of a Scripture-Rooted Decision
ἀκούσατέ μου
akousate mou — "listen to me"
15:13
A call to hear / heed
James opens not with "obey me" but "listen." His authority is exercised by directing attention — first to what God has done, then to what God has said. Leadership that roots decisions in Scripture invites the church to hear, not merely to submit.
συμφωνέω
symphōneō — "agree"
15:15
To sound together / harmonise
Literally "to symphony." The prophets' words harmonise with what God is doing. The test is not whether experience is loud or moving, but whether it sounds in tune with the written word. Scripture is the pitch everything else is tuned to.
καθὼς γέγραπται
kathōs gegraptai — "as it is written"
15:15
The perfect-tense "it stands written"
The standard NT formula for citing Scripture as final authority. The Greek perfect (gegraptai) means "it was written and still stands" — a permanent, settled word. To reach for this phrase is to end the argument, not extend it.
ἐπισκέπτομαι
episkeptomai — "visited / intervened"
15:14
God graciously visits to act
The word for God "visiting" his people to save (Luke 1:68; Ruth 1:6). James uses it of God visiting the Gentiles — a startling application. God has taken initiative; the council's task is only to recognise and align with what he has already done.
λαὸς
laos — "a people"
15:14
A people belonging to God
The covenant word for Israel as God's own laos. James says God has taken "a people for his name" from the Gentiles — the church is the covenant people expanded, exactly as Amos's rebuilt tent of David foretold. The identity is defined from Scripture.
κρίνω
krinō — "I judge / it is my judgment"
15:19
To decide / render a verdict
James's conclusion (15:19). It is a real, authoritative judgment — but it comes after and because of the Scripture cited. His verdict has weight precisely because it is not his opinion; it is the word applied. Decision follows the text, not the other way around.
The Scripture Behind the Decision
Amos 9:11–12
The Tent of David Rebuilt for the Nations
James's key text: God rebuilds the fallen booth of David so that "all the Gentiles who bear my name" may seek him. The rebuilt house of David is the church, and the inclusion of the nations was God's plan all along — the linchpin of the whole verdict. (See the companion study on Amos 9.)
Isaiah 45:22–23 · 49:6
A Light to the Nations
"Turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth"; the Servant made "a light for the Gentiles." The prophets James invokes as a chorus consistently foretell the nations streaming to Israel's God — Amos is representative, not isolated.
Genesis 12:3
All Nations Blessed in Abraham
The foundational promise: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed." Gentile inclusion is not a first-century novelty but the original goal of God's covenant — the "known from long ago" (15:18) that James underlines.
Jeremiah 12:16 · Zechariah 2:11
Nations Joined to the LORD
"Many nations shall join themselves to the LORD in that day and shall be my people." The prophetic expectation of Gentiles becoming God's people stands behind James's covenant word laos — the Scripture had prepared the church for exactly this moment.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. So what actually decided it — Scripture, the Spirit, experience, or the council's authority?
All four are present, and their relationship is the lesson. Experience (Peter, Paul) raised the evidence of what God was doing. The Spirit was genuinely at work — the letter even says "it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (15:28). The council exercised real authority. But the thing that made the case decisive was that Scripture "agreed with this" (15:15). Experience without the word would have been mere anecdote; the word confirmed and interpreted the experience. The Spirit did not lead around Scripture but through it. Rightly ordered: God acts, the Spirit works, Scripture confirms and governs, the church discerns and decides.
2. James quotes the Greek Amos, which differs from the Hebrew — does that weaken the argument?
The Hebrew of Amos 9:12 reads "that they may possess the remnant of Edom," while the Septuagint James follows reads "that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord" (a difference of vowel-pointing and one consonant: Edom/adam, "possess/seek"). Far from undercutting him, this shows careful use: James draws out the God-intended trajectory of the text toward the nations — which the very next words of Amos ("all the Gentiles who bear my name") already state plainly in either version. His argument rests not on one disputed clause but on the whole prophetic expectation that the nations would seek Israel's God. The point survives the textual variant intact.
3. Are the four "requirements" (15:20) still binding on Christians today?
Interpreters differ. The requests — abstain from food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals, and from blood — are best read not as a new mini-law for salvation (the whole point was that Gentiles are not under the law) but as pastoral guidance for table fellowship between Jewish and Gentile believers, sensitive to Jewish conscience (15:21). Sexual immorality remains universally binding as moral law; the food stipulations are widely understood as culturally-situated wisdom for that mixed-fellowship moment (cf. Paul's fuller treatment in 1 Cor 8–10; Rom 14). The abiding principle: freedom in the gospel, restrained in love for the sake of others.
4. Does Acts 15 support church councils and a teaching magisterium, or "Scripture alone"?
It actually supports both a high view of Scripture and genuine church authority — held in the right order. The council really did meet, debate, and issue a binding decision that the churches were to keep (15:22–29; 16:4). But its authority was ministerial, not autonomous: it decided by Scripture, not above it ("the prophets agree… as it is written"). This is the Protestant reading Calvin drew out: councils and church leaders have real authority precisely when — and because — they submit to and rightly apply the written word. A decision is binding not because a council said it, but because Scripture does.
5. Why does this matter for how we make decisions now?
Because the church constantly faces the same temptation the Judaizers embodied and the same temptation the "God is doing a new thing" crowd embodies — to let either tradition or experience become the final authority. Acts 15 models the alternative: take God's evident work seriously, listen to real testimony, debate honestly — and then bring it all to the Scriptures and let them govern. Churches, families, and individuals that decide this way build on rock; those that decide by the loudest voice, the newest experience, or bare pragmatism build on sand. The method is the message: root the decision in the word.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — A Church at a Crossroads
The stakes could not have been higher: get this wrong and the gospel of free grace is lost, or the church fractures into Jewish and Gentile halves. Into that crisis James brings no new revelation and pulls no rank as the Lord's brother. He listens to what God has done, opens the prophets, and lets Amos settle it. The first church council decided its hardest question by submitting everything — experience, debate, even apostolic testimony — to the written word of God.
Now — How We Decide
Every church, family, and believer still faces contested decisions, and still feels the pull to settle them by the strongest personality, the most moving experience, the prevailing culture, or plain pragmatism. Acts 15 hands us a better way — and a demanding one: take God's work seriously, hear one another honestly, and then bring it all under the Scriptures and let them govern. A decision rooted in the word can be owned by the whole people of God (15:22) and can stand.
Question to sit with: When I face my next hard decision, what will actually get the final word — my experience, my preferences, the loudest voice around me, or the Scriptures rightly opened?
