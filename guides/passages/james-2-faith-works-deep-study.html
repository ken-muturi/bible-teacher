James 2:14–26
Faith & Works — "Faith Without Works Is Dead"
Deep Study
⊕ James book overview James discussion guide
Anchor Text — The Passage That Launched a Debate
James 2:17, 26 · The bracketing verdict
"So also faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead." … "For as the body apart from the spirit is dead, so also faith apart from works is dead."
James 2:17, 26 (ESV)
The setting: James — the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church — writes to scattered Jewish believers a letter of intensely practical wisdom. Having just warned against showing favouritism to the rich (2:1–13), he turns to the most famous and most debated passage in the book: the relationship of faith and works. His target is not living, saving faith but a hollow substitute — a "faith" that is all profession and no practice, mere words with no life behind them. Against that, James argues bluntly that such faith "is dead," useless, and cannot save. The passage has been set against Paul's "justified by faith apart from works" (Rom 3:28) ever since — but read carefully, the two apostles are not enemies but allies, addressing opposite errors from opposite ends.
The Argument — Four Moves
2:14–17
1 · The Claim
"Can That Faith Save Him?"
A person "says" he has faith but has no works. James pictures sending a hungry, unclothed brother away with "be warmed and filled" — words without help. Such faith, "by itself," is dead (2:17).
2:18–19
2 · The Challenge
"Show Me Your Faith"
"Show me your faith apart from your works, and I will show you my faith by my works." Faith is invisible; works are how it becomes visible. Even the demons "believe" the creed — and shudder. Mere assent is not saving faith.
2:20–25
3 · The Evidence
Abraham and Rahab
Abraham's faith "was completed by his works" when he offered Isaac; Genesis 15:6 ("counted as righteousness") was fulfilled. Even Rahab the outsider was "justified by works" when she acted on her faith. Two witnesses: faith that acts.
2:26
4 · The Verdict
Body Without Spirit
"As the body apart from the spirit is dead, so faith apart from works is dead." Works are to faith what breath is to a body — not an add-on, but the sign that it is alive at all.
The Big Question — Does James Contradict Paul?
Same words, different battles — and no contradiction
James
Opponent: dead orthodoxy — people claiming faith with no fruit
"Faith": bare intellectual assent ("even the demons believe")
"Works": the fruit/evidence of living faith — deeds of love
"Justified": shown/proven righteous — vindicated as real
Point: faith that saves is never alone; it works
Paul
Opponent: legalism — trying to earn standing before God
"Faith": living trust that receives Christ
"Works": "works of the law" done to merit salvation
"Justified": declared righteous before God — the initial verdict
Point: we are saved by grace through faith, not by earning
Strikingly, both quote Genesis 15:6 — and both are right, because they answer opposite errors. Paul fights the person who thinks works earn salvation; James fights the person who thinks faith without works still saves. Their agreement is exact: we are justified by faith alone, but the faith that justifies is never alone. Paul himself says the only thing that counts is "faith working through love" (Gal 5:6) and that we are saved for "good works" (Eph 2:10); James never says works earn salvation, only that they prove it. Two apostles, one gospel, two front lines.
Luther felt the tension (he called James "an epistle of straw") — but the church has rightly kept James in the canon, because the danger of a dead, workless "faith" is as real as the danger of works-righteousness, and every generation needs both warnings.
Word Studies — The Terms in Dispute
πίστις
pistis — "faith"
2:14, 17, 22, 24
Faith / trust / the thing believed
The word covers a range — from living, trusting reliance to bare creedal assent. James's whole point turns on distinguishing them: the "faith" he attacks (2:14) is the kind demons have (2:19); the faith he commends is the kind that works (2:22).
ἔργα
erga — "works"
2:14–26
Works / deeds / actions
For James, deeds of practical love and obedience — feeding the hungry (2:15–16), Abraham's obedience, Rahab's action. Not Paul's "works of the law" done to earn merit, but the natural fruit of a faith that is truly alive.
δικαιόω
dikaioō — "justify"
2:21, 24, 25
To declare / show / prove righteous
The pivotal word. It can mean "declare righteous" (Paul's forensic sense) or "show/vindicate as righteous" (as in Luke 7:35, "wisdom is justified by her deeds"). James uses it in the latter sense: works demonstrate a righteousness faith has already received.
τελειόω
teleioō — "complete / perfect"
2:22
To bring to maturity / completion
"Faith was completed by his works." Works do not create Abraham's faith — he already believed (Gen 15:6, years earlier) — they bring it to full expression and maturity. Faith reaches its intended goal in action.
νεκρός
nekros — "dead"
2:17, 26
Dead / lifeless / a corpse
The verdict that brackets the passage. A workless faith isn't weak faith — it's a corpse, faith with no life in it. Like a body without breath, it has the outward form but none of the living reality.
ἀργή
argē — "useless / barren"
2:20
Idle, ineffective, unproductive
"Faith apart from works is useless" (2:20) — literally "workless / un-working" (a-ergos), a pointed pun: faith without erga is arg-, idle. It accomplishes nothing, saves no one, and helps no hungry brother.
Two Witnesses — Faith That Acted
Abraham
James 2:21–24 · Gen 15:6; 22
The father of the faithful — and the one Paul cites for justification by faith (Rom 4). James points to the later moment (offering Isaac, Gen 22) where the faith declared righteous in Genesis 15:6 was "completed" and shown genuine by obedience. Believed, then proven. Even "the friend of God" had a faith that worked.
Rahab
James 2:25 · Josh 2
A Gentile prostitute — the opposite of Abraham in every social way — set beside him as a second witness. Her faith in Israel's God was real because she acted: she received the spies and sent them out safely, risking her life. High and low, Jew and outsider: living faith always shows itself in deeds.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Does James contradict Paul on justification?
No — they fight opposite errors with the same vocabulary used in different senses. Paul opposes legalism (earning salvation by "works of the law") and uses "justify" to mean God's forensic declaration of righteousness received by faith. James opposes dead orthodoxy (a professed faith with no fruit) and uses "justify" to mean being shown/vindicated as genuinely righteous. Both quote Genesis 15:6; both are inspired and true. The synthesis, as Calvin put it: we are justified by faith alone, but the faith that justifies is never alone. Paul would say amen to James 2:17, and James to Ephesians 2:8–9.
2. So does James teach salvation by works?
No. James never says works earn or procure salvation. He says works are the necessary evidence that saving faith is present and alive — the fruit, not the root. His illustration is decisive: works are to faith what breath is to a body (2:26). Breath doesn't create life, but a body with no breath is a corpse. Likewise a "faith" that produces no love, no obedience, no care for the needy is not a weak faith but a dead one — and a dead faith cannot save because it was never the living thing that saves.
3. What kind of "faith" is James actually attacking?
Bare intellectual assent — believing the right facts about God without trusting or following him. His razor-sharp example is 2:19: "You believe that God is one; you do well. Even the demons believe — and shudder!" Demons have flawless theology and zero salvation. So agreeing with the creed, saying the words, having been raised in church — none of that is saving faith if it never bows the will or moves the hands. James is not lowering the bar on faith; he is exposing a counterfeit of it.
4. How can James say Abraham was "justified by works" (2:21) when Genesis and Paul say by faith?
By noticing which moment James points to. Abraham believed and was counted righteous in Genesis 15:6 (Paul's text). James points forward to Genesis 22 — the offering of Isaac, decades later — and says that act "completed" his faith and showed it to be real: "the Scripture was fulfilled" (2:23). So the faith declared righteous in ch.15 was demonstrated and vindicated as genuine in ch.22. "Justified by works" here means "shown to be righteous by the fruit of faith," not "made righteous by earning." The timeline itself rules out works-righteousness.
5. Was Luther right to call James "an epistle of straw"?
Luther's phrase reflected a pastoral worry that James could be misused against the gospel of grace he had fought to recover — and, read as if it taught works-salvation, it could. But the church was right to keep James canonical and Luther himself never removed it. Rightly understood, James does not threaten justification by faith; it guards it from cheapening into a bloodless profession. In fact Luther's own famous line — faith is "a living, busy, active, mighty thing" that cannot help doing good works — is James's exact point. The two belong together: Paul protects the root, James insists on the fruit.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — A Church Tempted to Coast
James wrote to believers who knew the right words and could recite the creed — "God is one" — but were showing favouritism to the rich and turning the poor brother away with empty blessings. He refuses to let them hide behind a profession of faith that changed nothing. "Show me," he says. Faith you can only talk is faith worth doubting.
Now — Believism Without Discipleship
We live where "I have faith" can mean little more than a stored belief and a past prayer, with a life indistinguishable from the world's. James is the needed corrective — not against grace, but against a counterfeit of faith that costs nothing and does nothing. He asks the uncomfortable question of every professing Christian: if your faith were put on trial, would there be any evidence to convict you?
Question to sit with: If someone examined only my actions this month — not my words about faith — what would they conclude I actually believe?
Controlling Idea
"We are justified by faith alone — but the faith that justifies is never alone. A faith that produces no love, no obedience, no care for the needy is not weak faith but a corpse; living faith always shows itself in works, as breath shows a body is alive."
James 2:17, 26 — "faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead… as the body apart from the spirit is dead."
