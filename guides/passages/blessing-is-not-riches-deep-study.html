Blessing Is Not the Same as Riches
Abraham's Wealth & the Call to Leave — Genesis 12:1–3 · 13:2
Deep Study
← Genesis 13 (Abram & Lot)
Two Verses to Hold Together
Genesis 12:1–2 & 13:2 · The call and the wealth
"Now the LORD said to Abram, 'Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you. And I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing.'"  …  "Now Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold."
Genesis 12:1–2; 13:2 (ESV)
The tension worth noticing: Abram is "very rich" (13:2) — Scripture states it plainly, and God really did prosper him. Yet the call that made him blessed (12:1–3) was a command to leave — his country, his relatives, his father's house — and to live for the rest of his life as a tent-dwelling stranger in a land he would never own (he had to buy a single field just to bury Sarah, ch. 23). His wealth came bundled with homelessness, strife (13:7), and danger (ch. 14). So the riches were real — but they were never the blessing. The blessing was God himself, his covenant, his promise, and being made a channel of blessing to all the nations (12:3; Gal 3:8). Blessing and riches overlapped in Abram's life, but they are not the same thing — and reading them as identical is the root error of every prosperity theology.
What God Actually Promised — vs. What We Assume "Blessed" Means
The blessing God promised
Genesis 12:1–3
A land — "that I will show you" (not yet owned; entered by faith)
A great nation — from a wife who was barren (12:2; 11:30)
A great name — honour from God, not self-made (contrast Babel, 11:4)
To be a blessing — a conduit of good to others, not a hoarder
All the families of the earth blessed — the mission that reaches to Christ (Gal 3:8)
God himself: "I am your shield, your very great reward" (15:1)
What we tend to assume
"Blessed" = comfortable & rich
More money, property, and possessions
Security, ease, and an upgraded standard of living
Status and reputation we build for ourselves
Blessing measured by what I receive and keep
Prosperity as the proof that God is pleased
God valued as the means to the good life
Not one line of God's promise says "and you will be rich." The wealth came (13:2), but it was a by-product God gave, never the substance of the blessing. To make riches the definition of blessing is to swap the covenant God for the comforts he sometimes provides — and to be left unable to explain the faithful who suffer (Heb 11:35–38).
Tracing It Through Abraham's Life
12:1–3
The Call — blessing begins with leaving
"Go… and you will be a blessing"
The blessing is defined entirely in terms of God's promise and mission — land, nation, name, being a blessing to all peoples. It begins with a costly command to leave home and security. Blessing here means being caught up into God's purpose, not upgraded in comfort.
12:4–9; 13:2
The Reality — rich, yet a wanderer
Very Rich — and Living in Tents
Abram becomes "very rich" (13:2). But he owns no land, moves from place to place, and pitches tents. The wealth is real yet it never settles into security; it even breeds strife (13:7) and makes him a target (ch. 14). Prosperity without a home of his own.
15:1
The Substance — what the blessing really is
"I Am Your Shield, Your Very Great Reward"
When God re-assures Abram, the reward on offer is not more livestock but God himself — "your shield, your very great reward." The heart of the blessing is a relationship: the covenant God who gives himself. Everything else is downstream of that.
17; 21
The Waiting — the real gift comes late
The Son, Not the Silver
The blessing Abram actually longed for — a son, the seed of promise — took twenty-five years and could not be bought with any of his gold. The riches he had; the promise he had to wait for in faith. What money could get, he already had; what mattered, only God could give.
23; Heb 11
The Verdict — a stranger to the end
Buying a Grave, Seeking a City
The only land the very rich Abram ever owned was a burial cave he purchased for Sarah (ch. 23). Hebrews 11 sums it up: he lived in tents as a stranger, "looking forward to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God." Blessed — and homeless by faith.
The Paradox — "Very Rich" and Yet…
Abram's wealth and Abram's poverty sat side by side
Very rich (13:2)
Livestock, silver, and gold in abundance
Able to arm 318 men of his own household (14:14)
Wealthy enough to refuse the king of Sodom's reward (14:22–23)
Yet a landless sojourner
Owned no land — lived his whole life in tents
Childless for decades; the promised son delayed 25 years
The only ground he ever bought was a grave (ch. 23)
The same man was "very rich" and had "no foothold" in the land of promise (Acts 7:5). His true wealth was never in the flocks; it was in the God who called him and the promise he clung to. That is why Hebrews can hold him up as the model of faith, not of prosperity: he "was looking forward to the city… whose builder is God" (Heb 11:10).
Blessing ≠ Riches — The Testimony of the Whole Bible
Matthew 5:3–12 · The Beatitudes
Jesus pronounces the "blessed" to be the poor in spirit, the mourners, the meek, the persecuted — the exact opposite of the world's picture of a blessed life. Blessing is defined by nearness to God's kingdom, not by comfort.
Matthew 8:20 · The Son of Man
"Foxes have holes… but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head." The most blessed life ever lived was materially poor and homeless. If blessing equalled riches, Jesus was not blessed.
Philippians 4:11–13 · Paul's secret
Paul learned to be content "in plenty and in want" — blessing is not the abundance but the sufficiency of Christ in either state. "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" is written from prison, about contentment.
Hebrews 11:13–16, 35–38 · The faithful
The heroes of faith "died… not having received the things promised," some destitute, afflicted, in caves. They sought "a better country." Faith's blessing is often visible only beyond this life.
Revelation 3:17 · Laodicea
"You say, 'I am rich… and need nothing,' not realising that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked." The church most confident of its riches was spiritually bankrupt. Wealth can mask poverty before God.
1 Timothy 6:6–10, 17–19 · True gain
"Godliness with contentment is great gain." Riches are not condemned but relocated: the rich are to be "rich in good works," storing up "treasure… for the future" — the love of money, not money, is the root of evil.
Word Studies — The Vocabulary of Blessing
בְּרָכָה
berakah — "blessing"
12:2
Blessing / a gift of favour & life
In Genesis, blessing is fundamentally relational and generative — God bestowing life, fruitfulness, and his own favour. It flows through the blessed to others ("you will be a blessing"). It is God's presence and purpose, not a bank balance.
לֶךְ־לְךָ
lek-lekha — "go!"
12:1
Go forth / go for yourself
The emphatic command that opens the whole Abraham story. The blessed life begins with leaving — country, kin, and father's house, the three securities of the ancient world. Blessing is inseparable from surrender and pilgrimage.
גֵּר
gēr — "sojourner"
Gen 23:4; cf. Heb 11:9
Resident alien / stranger
Abram's own self-description: "I am a sojourner and foreigner among you" (23:4). The father of the faithful lived his whole blessed life as a landless gēr. Faith is content to be a stranger here, because its home is elsewhere.
שָׂכָר
sakar — "reward"
15:1
Wage / reward / recompense
"I am… your very great sakar." God does not merely give a reward — he is the reward. The prize of the blessed life is God himself. Any theology that makes God the means to a different reward has inverted the promise.
מָגֵן
magen — "shield"
15:1
Shield / protector
Alongside "reward," God calls himself Abram's "shield." The blessing includes protection and security — but the security is a Person, not a portfolio. Abram's safety was God, not his 318 armed men or his gold.
μακάριος
makarios — "blessed"
Matt 5:3ff
Truly flourishing / to be congratulated
The NT word for "blessed" that Jesus pronounces over the poor in spirit and the persecuted. It names a God-given flourishing that has nothing to do with wealth — and is often found precisely where the world sees only lack.

Evangelical · Missional
Wright reads the Abrahamic blessing as fundamentally missional: "you will be a blessing… all peoples on earth will be blessed through you." The point of being blessed is to be a channel of blessing to the nations — the opposite of self-focused prosperity.
Blessing in Genesis is bound up with God's redemptive purpose for the whole world, ultimately fulfilled in Christ (Gal 3:8).

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. But wasn't Abram's wealth itself a sign of God's blessing?
In a sense, yes — God did prosper him, and Scripture doesn't hide it. But notice how the text handles it: the wealth is reported almost in passing (13:2), never listed among the covenant promises, and it comes bundled with real burdens (strife, war, homelessness). The blessing proper is spelled out in 12:1–3 and 15:1 — land, seed, name, mission, and God himself — and most of it Abram never possessed in this life. So wealth can accompany blessing without being it. The danger is inferring the reverse: that wealth proves blessing, which would make the poor cursed and the Laodiceans (Rev 3:17) commended — both plainly false.
2. Does "blessing ≠ riches" mean money is bad or that Christians should be poor?
No. Abram was rich, Job was restored, and 1 Timothy 6 assumes some believers will be wealthy and tells them how to steward it, not to divest. Wealth is a gift to be received with gratitude and used generously. The error is not having money but equating it with blessing — letting the bank balance become the measure of God's favour and the goal of the Christian life. Money is a good servant and a terrible god. The call is neither to worship it nor to despise it, but to hold it loosely as a tool for God's purposes.
3. What about the Old Testament promises of material prosperity (Deut 28, Proverbs)?
The Mosaic covenant did attach material blessing to national obedience, and Proverbs observes that wisdom and diligence generally tend toward flourishing. But even there, the deepest blessing is knowing and walking with God, and the wisdom literature itself complicates any simple equation (Job suffers though righteous; Ecclesiastes finds wealth "vanity"; Psalm 73 wrestles with the prospering wicked). The New Testament then decisively relocates blessing in Christ and his kingdom, with Jesus, Paul, and the martyrs as its models. The trajectory of Scripture moves away from measuring blessing by possessions.
4. So is the prosperity gospel simply false?
In its core claim — that faith reliably produces health and wealth, and that these are the mark of God's favour — yes, it fails the test of Scripture. It cannot account for the suffering of the most faithful (Heb 11:35–38), it contradicts Jesus' own poverty and the Beatitudes, and it turns God into a means to an end rather than the end himself. It mistakes the occasional by-product (wealth) for the substance (God, his covenant, his mission). That said, the answer is not a false spirituality that treats poverty as holy; it is to define blessing biblically — union with God in Christ — and let material things fall into their proper, secondary place.
5. Then how do I actually know if I'm blessed?
Not by your income, but by your relationship to God and his purposes. Are you in Christ, forgiven, indwelt by the Spirit? Are you being conformed to Jesus, bearing fruit, and being made a blessing to others (12:2)? Is God himself your "shield and very great reward" (15:1) — the treasure you would not trade for anything? A believer in a hospital bed or a refugee camp can be more blessed than a comfortable unbeliever, because blessing is measured by God's presence and favour, not by circumstances. The blessed life is the God-possessed life, wherever it is lived.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — A Rich Man in a Tent
Abram had silver, gold, and vast herds — and no address, no homeland, and for decades no heir. His neighbours would have called him wealthy; God called him blessed for a different reason entirely: he had been chosen, called, and given the promise, and he had the LORD himself as his shield and reward. The flocks were real, but they were never the point.
Now — Our Measure of "Blessed"
We casually call the comfortable, the successful, and the wealthy "blessed," and quietly wonder what we did wrong when money is tight. Genesis 12–13 resets the definition: blessing is being drawn into God's promise and possessing God himself, expressed in a life poured out as a blessing to others. Sometimes that comes with plenty; often it comes with a tent and a long wait. Either way, the blessing is God, not the goods.
Question to sit with: When I use the word "blessed," am I measuring God's favour by what I own and enjoy — or by whether I have God himself, and am becoming a blessing to others?
