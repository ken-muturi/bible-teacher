Passage — Luke 18:1–8 (NIV)
Key Text
Then Jesus told his disciples a parable to show them that they should always pray and not give up. He said: "In a certain town there was a judge who neither feared God nor cared what people thought. And there was a widow in that town who kept coming to him with the plea, 'Grant me justice against my adversary.'

"For some time he refused. But finally he said to himself, 'Even though I don't fear God or care what people think, yet because this widow keeps bothering me, I will see that she gets justice, so that she won't eventually come and attack me!'"

Then the Lord said, "Listen to what the unjust judge says. And will not God bring about justice for his chosen ones, who cry out to him day and night? Will he keep putting them off? I tell you, he will see that they get justice, and quickly. However, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?"
Luke 18:1–8 (NIV)
Placement in Luke: This parable sits in the travel narrative (Luke 9:51–19:44) — Jesus on the long road to Jerusalem. It follows immediately after the apocalyptic discourse on the coming of the Son of Man (17:20–37), which explains the closing question: "when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?" The parable is not just about prayer technique — it is about whether disciples will hold on in faith during the long, apparently God-silent stretch between Jesus's departure and his return.
Word Studies — Five Key Terms
ἐκκακεῖν
ekkakein
v.1 — "give up"
To grow weary / lose heart / give up
Literally: to become bad, to lose courage. Found also in 2 Cor 4:1, 16; Gal 6:9; Eph 3:13; 2 Thess 3:13 — always in contexts of endurance under pressure. The parable's stated purpose is to address this tendency: they should "always pray and not ekkakein." The enemy of persistent prayer is not rebellion but weariness.
ESV: "lose heart" · NIV: "give up" · NLT: "give up"
ἀναίδεια
anaideia
Luke 11:8 — companion text
Shameless boldness / persistent audacity
The key word in the companion parable (Friend at Midnight, Lk 11:8). From a (without) + aidōs (shame). Not impudence but the bold refusal to be put off by social embarrassment. The man knocks at midnight because he is not deterred by the awkwardness. This is what Jesus commends — not cleverness, not volume, but unbothered persistence.
ESV: "impudence" · NIV: "shameless audacity" · NLT: "boldness"
ἐκδίκησις
ekdikēsis
v.3, 7, 8
Justice / vindication / legal redress
The widow is not asking for revenge — she is asking for legal vindication that the system owes her. The word runs throughout the passage (vv.3, 7, 8). God will bring about ekdikēsis for his chosen ones. Prayer in this parable is not primarily petition for personal blessings but the cry of the oppressed for justice — "Grant me justice against my adversary."
ESV: "justice" · NIV: "justice" · KJV: "avenge"
πίστις
pistis
v.8 — closing question
Faith / faithfulness / trust
The parable closes with the most searching question in the passage: "when the Son of Man comes, will he find pistis on the earth?" The persistent widow is the image of what pistis looks like — not intellectual belief but active, refusing-to-quit trust. The eschatological frame shows that persistent prayer is not a technique but evidence of whether faith has survived the long wait.
ESV: "faith" · NIV: "faith" · NLT: "faith"
ὑπωπιάζῃ
hypōpiazē
v.5 — the judge's fear
Wear out / strike under the eye / beat black and blue
A vivid, almost comic word. Literally means "to give a black eye" — used in boxing contexts. The judge fears the widow will eventually "beat him up" (metaphorically) with her relentless coming. This earthy humour is deliberate: Jesus is painting a judge so worn down by a persistent woman that his only escape is to grant her justice. The a fortiori argument runs from this comic image to God's willing response.
ESV: "wear me out" · NIV: "wear me out" · NLT: "wear me out"
Structure — Four Movements
1
v.1 — The stated purpose
The Narrator's Frame: Pray and Don't Lose Heart
Luke unusually tells us upfront what the parable means before we hear it. This is not a riddle — it is medicine for a specific illness: the tendency to stop praying when nothing seems to be happening. The parable exists to treat that illness. The entire narrative is shaped by this pastoral aim.
2
vv.2–5 — The story
The Unjust Judge and the Persistent Widow
The judge is introduced with two negatives: he neither fears God nor respects people — he is accountable to no one. He is the worst possible judge. The widow has no power, no advocate, no leverage — only her repeated presence. She "kept coming" (imperfect tense — ongoing action). Eventually the judge grants justice not out of conscience but out of self-preservation: she will "wear him out." The comedy is intentional.
3
vv.6–8a — The argument
The A Fortiori Move: How Much More Will God Act?
Jesus draws the lesson with an explicit lesser-to-greater argument. If even an unjust, God-fearing-nothing judge eventually responds to persistence — how much more will a just, caring, covenant-keeping God respond to his own chosen ones who cry to him day and night? The answer is emphatic: "he will see that they get justice, and quickly." The word "quickly" (en tachei) points to the suddenness of the final vindication, not necessarily the speed of each answer.
4
v.8b — The closing question
The Eschatological Sting: Will He Find Faith?
The parable does not end with encouragement — it ends with a question that cuts the other way. The problem is not whether God will answer; God will act, and quickly. The problem is whether his people will still be praying when he comes. The widow is the image of what faith looks like in the waiting period. The question "will he find faith on the earth?" hangs over the whole passage as a challenge, not a comfort.
Companion Text — Luke 11:5–13 · The Friend at Midnight
The Same Lesson, a Different Angle
Luke 11 and Luke 18 are the two great persistent-prayer parables. Read together they are complete.
Luke 11:5–13
A man knocks at his neighbour's door at midnight asking for bread for an unexpected guest. The neighbour refuses — door locked, family in bed. But the man keeps knocking. The neighbour eventually gets up not because of friendship but because of the man's anaideia (shameless boldness). Jesus follows with "Ask... seek... knock" — all present imperatives meaning keep doing this. Climax: if you, being evil, give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him.
What Luke 11 adds
The object of prayer: Luke 11 ends with the Holy Spirit as the supreme gift — the parable is not about getting anything you want but about seeking God himself.

Anaideia: The word Luke 11:8 uses is not the widow's legal cry but personal boldness — shameless refusal to be embarrassed into silence.

Present imperatives: "Keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking" — the Greek grammar of persistence that Luke 18 illustrates with the widow's story.
"Ask and it will be given to you; seek and you will find; knock and the door will be opened to you." — Luke 11:9. The verbs are present imperatives. Not a one-time act. A posture.
Historical & OT Background — Where This Comes From
Genesis 32:22–32
Jacob Wrestling
The paradigm the tradition calls "prevailing prayer." Jacob wrestles through the night — receives a wound and a blessing — and refuses to release the man until he is blessed. "I will not let you go unless you bless me." He is named Israel: "you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed." The church has read this as a model of prayer that holds on through the dark, refuses to quit, and emerges both wounded and blessed.
"I will not let you go unless you bless me." — Gen 32:26
1 Kings 18:41–45
Elijah Prays Seven Times
After the confrontation on Carmel, Elijah prays for rain. He sends his servant to look toward the sea — seven times. On the seventh look: a cloud the size of a man's hand. Rain comes. James 5:17–18 holds this up as the normative model of persistent prayer, using the phrase "a human being like us" — this is not superhuman intercession but ordinary faith that refuses to stop praying until the answer appears.
"Go back seven times." — 1 Kgs 18:43
Daniel 10:2–13
21 Days of Prayer
Daniel prays and mourns for three weeks before the angel arrives, explaining the delay: the "prince of the Persian kingdom" resisted him for 21 days until Michael came to help. This passage became foundational for the idea that persistent prayer overcomes spiritual resistance in heavenly realms. Widely used in charismatic/intercessory traditions to explain unanswered prayer as spiritual warfare rather than divine silence.
"From the first day... your words were heard." — Dan 10:12
Exodus 32:9–14 · Numbers 14:11–20
Moses the Intercessor
Moses intercedes when God declares his intention to destroy Israel and start over. He does not pray once — he stands in the gap persistently, reasoning with God from his own character and covenant promises. "And the LORD relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people." OT intercession models prayer that takes hold of God's own promises and refuses to let go until he acts consistently with who he has declared himself to be.
"Remember your servants Abraham, Isaac and Israel..." — Exod 32:13
The Critical Tension — Persistence vs. Repetition
What the Parable Commends vs. What It Does Not Commend
What Jesus commends
Faith that does not quit — returning to God repeatedly over time
Relational boldness — the widow comes because she knows the judge owes her justice
Crying out "day and night" — prayer as a posture of life, not an occasional act
Not losing heart (ekkakein) under apparent silence
The shameless audacity of Luke 11 — unbothered persistence, not embarrassed silence
What the parable does not support
Mechanical repetition of the same words — Matthew 6:7 warns against this explicitly
The idea that God needs to be worn down or persuaded
Emotional escalation as a sign of valid prayer
Treating Acts 1:4 ("tarry") as a method for all prayer
The assumption that length of prayer increases the likelihood of answer
The resolution: The widow's persistence is relational and legal — she keeps coming because she knows the judge is obligated to give her justice and she will not let him off the hook. This is the model for Christian prayer: returning to God not to wear him down but because we know his character and his covenant, and we hold him to his own promises. The contrast is with pagans who "think they will be heard because of their many words" (Matt 6:7) — prayer as manipulation rather than trust.
Where the Practice "Pray Until Something Happens" Came From
Historical Context
The Holiness movement (19th c.) — Charles Finney's "new measures" revivals and the camp-meeting tradition embedded the idea that prayer is pressed through until a breakthrough comes. The doctrine of "entire sanctification" (a second definite work of grace) created the expectation of a specific, datable, felt experience that must be prayed into.

The Pentecostal "tarrying" tradition (1906 onward) — The Azusa Street Revival under William Seymour established "tarrying meetings" based on Acts 1:4: "Do not leave Jerusalem, but wait for the gift my Father promised." The 120 waited ten days until Pentecost. Pentecostal revivalists taught believers to pray and wait until they received the same experience — the baptism of the Holy Spirit evidenced by tongues.

The problem: Acts 1:4 is a specific historical instruction for a once-for-all event. Pentecost was not a template; it was the fulfilment of a promise. Treating it as a method for all believers to repeat misreads the genre of Acts. The biblical basis for persistent prayer is Luke 18 and 11, not Acts 1. Persistence in prayer is faith — not a technique for triggering an experience.

What is genuinely biblical: The practice of sustained, corporate, expectant prayer has roots in Acts 1:14 (the disciples devoted themselves to prayer together), Acts 12:5 (the church praying earnestly for Peter), and Daniel 10. Extended prayer in community with expectant faith is thoroughly biblical. The question is whether the goal is relationship and justice (Luke 18) or a particular felt experience (Pentecostal tarrying).
Teaching Illustrations
Everyday
The Parent Advocating for Their Child
A parent whose child has been wrongly treated by a school system keeps calling, keeps showing up at meetings, keeps writing letters — not because they think the headteacher needs to be worn down but because they know their child's case is just and they will not let the institution ignore it. They are not manipulating — they are refusing to allow injustice to go unaddressed. This is closer to what the widow is doing than the image of tearful, desperate repetition.
Historical / Literary
William Wilberforce — 20 Years in Parliament
Wilberforce introduced a bill to abolish the slave trade every year for nearly 20 years before it passed in 1807 — and lived to see slavery abolished entirely in 1833. He did not escalate his emotional state year by year; he simply kept returning, kept arguing, kept praying, with the same conviction that the cause was just and the outcome was inevitable. The widow of Luke 18, if she lived in 19th-century England, would have looked like this.
For a Non-Religious Audience
The Researcher Who Won't Stop
Every major scientific breakthrough has behind it someone who kept pursuing the same question when the field had moved on. Not because they were louder than everyone else, or worked themselves into emotional intensity — but because they were convinced the answer was there and refused to stop looking. Barbara McClintock worked on transposons for decades before the scientific community took her seriously. She received the Nobel Prize at 81. Persistence is not desperation — it is conviction that something is true.
Commentary Shelf
Darrell Bock
Luke 9:51–24:53 (BECNT, 1996)
Conservative Evangelical
On the eschatological frame: Bock emphasises that the closing question (v.8b) is the parable's sharpest point. The story is not primarily about getting answers to prayer — it is about whether disciples will maintain persistent faith during the long period between Jesus's ascension and return. Prayer is the evidence of living faith.
On hypōpiazē (v.5): Bock notes the boxing metaphor is deliberately humorous — Jesus uses comic exaggeration to make the a fortiori argument land. The unjust judge is a deliberately bad example to make the contrast with God as sharp as possible.

Joel Green
The Gospel of Luke (NICNT, 1997)
Evangelical Scholarly
Green stresses the social world of the parable: the widow was among the most vulnerable members of ancient society — no legal advocate, no male protector, no social capital. Her only asset is her persistent presence. Jesus chooses her precisely because of her powerlessness: if persistence works for someone with nothing, it is available to everyone.
On ekdikēsis: Green notes that the widow's cry is for justice, not personal favour — and God's response in v.7 mirrors this. The prayer Jesus commends in this parable is not primarily request for blessings but the cry of the oppressed for God to act with justice in a world that delays it.

N. T. Wright
Luke for Everyone (2001)
Pastoral / Anglican
Wright reads the parable in its immediate literary context — following ch. 17's apocalyptic discourse. The disciples have just been told that the Son of Man's coming will be sudden and unexpected. The parable is Jesus's answer to the implied question: what do we do in the meantime? You pray. You don't give up. You cry out for justice. You keep the faith that God will act.
On the closing question: "The issue is not whether God will act, but whether his people will still be praying — still be asking, still be trusting — when he does." Wright calls this one of the most challenging verses in the Gospels for the contemporary church.

Kenneth Bailey
Poet and Peasant / Through Peasant Eyes (1976/1980)
Middle Eastern Cultural Context
Bailey's contribution is irreplaceable for this parable. He shows that in the Middle Eastern honour-shame culture, the judge's fear of hypōpiazē ("giving him a black eye") was not physical but reputational — the widow would eventually come to him publicly, in front of witnesses, and humiliate him. The judge grants justice to protect his honour. This makes the a fortiori argument even sharper: God has no honour to protect by delay — he will act from pure covenant love.
On the widow: in Bailey's cultural reading, a widow repeatedly appearing before a public official would have been a remarkable act of social courage. She has already crossed every social boundary. This is what anaideia (shameless boldness) looks like embodied.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Does persistence change God's mind — or reveal our faith?
The parable's logic is a fortiori — from lesser to greater. God is not like the unjust judge; he is the opposite. The judge needed to be worn down because he didn't care. God already cares — "will he keep putting them off?" implies the answer is no. Persistence in prayer is not the mechanism that moves God from reluctance to willingness; it is the evidence that we believe God is just and will act. It changes us — keeping us oriented toward God — more than it changes God's intention.
2. Does "quickly" (v.8) mean God always answers fast?
En tachei ("quickly/speedily") in apocalyptic contexts usually refers to the suddenness of an event when it comes, not to how soon it comes. See Revelation 1:1 — "things that must soon take place" refers to events that will happen suddenly when triggered, not necessarily imminently. God's justice will come suddenly and decisively — but the parable itself acknowledges that the "day and night" period of crying out can be long. The answer to "quickly" is not "immediately" but "when he acts, it will be definitive."
3. Is the "tarrying" tradition a misreading of Acts 1?
The Pentecostal practice of praying until you receive the Holy Spirit (based on Acts 1:4) treats a specific historical instruction as a repeatable template. Acts 1:4 was spoken to specific disciples in a specific moment — "wait in Jerusalem for the Promise." Pentecost was the once-for-all fulfilment of Joel's prophecy (Acts 2:16). The biblical model for persistent prayer is Luke 18 and 11, not Acts 1. Extended, expectant, corporate prayer is thoroughly biblical; the idea that the Spirit must be "prayed down" in every generation replicates a unique historical event as a method.
4. Why does the parable end with a question about faith, not a promise about prayer?
This is the sharpest turn in the passage. Jesus shifts from assurance (God will bring justice, and quickly) to challenge (will he find faith?). The issue is not God's reliability — that is settled. The issue is human endurance. Prayer is the form faith takes when it cannot yet see what it is waiting for. The closing question suggests that the real threat to the praying community is not that God won't answer but that people will stop asking before he does. The parable is a call to persistence, not a guarantee of specific outcomes.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — Original Hearers
The disciples have just heard the apocalyptic discourse of ch. 17 — the Son of Man's coming, the days of Noah and Lot, the sudden catastrophic judgment. They would have been asking: when? how long? what do we do in the waiting? This parable is Jesus's direct pastoral answer: you pray. You do not give up. You cry for justice like a widow who has no one else to go to. You hold on to the covenant faithfulness of a God who is nothing like this unjust judge.
Now — Modern Context
Any believer or community in the long stretch between promise and fulfilment — praying for healing that hasn't come, for prodigals who haven't returned, for justice in situations that seem stalled, for revival that hasn't broken out. The temptation is ekkakein — not to rebel against God but simply to grow weary and quietly stop. The parable says: the problem is never whether God will act. The question is whether faith survives the wait. Keep coming. Keep crying out. Do not lose heart.
Question to sit with: Is my persistence in prayer coming from faith that believes God is good and will act — or from a technique that assumes God needs to be worn down?
Controlling Idea
"The problem is not whether God will bring justice. The problem is whether his people will still be praying — still trusting — when he does."
Luke 18:8b — "When the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?" The parable ends not with a promise but a question. Answer it with your life.
