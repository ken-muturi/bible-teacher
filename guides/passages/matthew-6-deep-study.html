Controlling Verse — The Climax of the Chapter
Matthew 6:33 · The single priority that reorders everything
"But seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well."
Matthew 6:33 (NIV) — the answer to piety-for-show, greed, and anxiety alike
Placement: Matthew 6 is the middle section of the Sermon on the Mount. Where chapter 5 defined kingdom character (Beatitudes) and the higher righteousness (the antitheses), chapter 6 turns to the God-ward orientation of the heart — the invisible motives underneath the visible life. Its unifying question is: who are you living for? The chapter treats three arenas — religious devotion (6:1–18), possessions (6:19–24), and anxiety (6:25–34) — and answers all three the same way: live before the Father who sees in secret, store treasure in heaven, and seek first his kingdom. At its centre stands the Lord's Prayer (6:9–13), the model of a heart rightly ordered toward God.
Structure — Four Movements
6:1–18
Movement 1 — Devotion
Piety Before the Father, Not the Crowd
Three acts — giving, praying, fasting — each with the same structure: don't perform for people's applause; do it in secret for the Father who sees. Reward comes from him, not from being noticed.
6:9–13
Centre — The Model Prayer
The Lord's Prayer
Set inside the teaching on prayer, the pattern for all praying: God's name, kingdom, and will first; then our bread, forgiveness, and deliverance. Followed by a pointed word on forgiving others (6:14–15).
6:19–24
Movement 2 — Treasure
Two Treasures, Two Eyes, Two Masters
Store treasure in heaven, not on earth, "for where your treasure is, there your heart will be." A healthy eye fills the body with light. And the verdict: "You cannot serve both God and money."
6:25–34
Movement 3 — Worry
Do Not Be Anxious; Seek First the Kingdom
Because the Father feeds the birds and clothes the lilies, anxiety over food and clothing is needless and pagan. Seek first the kingdom, and the Father will provide. "Do not worry about tomorrow."
Three Acts of Devotion — The Same Pattern Three Times (6:1–18)
6:2–4 · Giving
When you give…
Don't sound a trumpet as the hypocrites do. Give so quietly your left hand doesn't know what your right is doing. The Father who sees in secret will reward you.
6:5–15 · Praying
When you pray…
Don't pray to be seen, or heap up empty phrases like the pagans. Go into your room, shut the door, pray to your Father in secret — who already knows what you need.
6:16–18 · Fasting
When you fast…
Don't look gloomy to advertise your fasting. Wash your face, look normal, so that only your Father sees. He who sees in secret will reward you.
The repeated logic: each act assumes the disciple will do it ("when you give / pray / fast," not "if") — the issue is never whether, but for whom. The same refrain closes each: reward is either the shallow applause of people (received in full, now) or the Father's reward (from the One who sees in secret). Every act of devotion is a fork between two audiences.
The Lord's Prayer — A Heart Rightly Ordered (6:9–13)
First — God-ward: his name, reign, and will
"Hallowed be your name"
That God's name — his very self — be honoured as holy. Worship before request.
"Your kingdom come"
That God's reign break in — now in part, fully at the end. The great longing of the Sermon.
"Your will be done"
On earth as in heaven — surrender before supply. Prayer aligns us to God, not God to us.
Then — us-ward: bread, pardon, protection
"Give us… daily bread"
Dependence for today's needs (epiousios) — enough, not excess; asked one day at a time.
"Forgive us… as we forgive"
Pardon received and passed on. The one petition Jesus circles back to (6:14–15).
"Lead us not into temptation"
Protection from testing and "the evil one" — a plea of humility, not self-confidence.
The order is the teaching. Three God-ward petitions come before three human-ward ones: we approach as "Our Father," honour his name, long for his kingdom, and yield to his will — and only then bring our needs. Prayer is not primarily getting God to do our will; it is being re-ordered to his. Note too that it is "Our Father… give us… forgive us" — never a solo prayer, always prayed as family.
Word Studies — Six Key Terms
ὑποκριτής
hypokritēs
6:2, 5, 16 · "hypocrite"
Stage-actor / play-actor
Originally an actor speaking behind a mask. Jesus' repeated label for those who perform righteousness for an audience: their whole religious life is a role played for spectators. The reward — applause — is all they get.
πατήρ
patēr
throughout ch.6 · "Father"
Father
The controlling word of the chapter, used ~12 times. Every remedy — for hypocrisy, greed, and worry — is grounded in the fatherhood of God: a Father who sees in secret, knows what you need, and feeds the birds. Intimacy, not performance.
ἐπιούσιος
epiousios
6:11 · "daily"
Daily / for the coming day / needful
A famously rare word — virtually unknown outside this prayer. Likely "bread for the coming day" or "the bread we need." Whatever the nuance, it teaches day-at-a-time dependence, echoing the manna that could not be hoarded (Exod 16).
μαμωνᾶς
mamōnas
6:24 · "money/mammon"
Wealth personified as a rival master
An Aramaic term for wealth/property. Jesus personifies it as a would-be lord demanding service. The issue is not having money but being mastered by it — you cannot serve two lords with undivided loyalty.
μεριμνάω
merimnaō
6:25–34 · "be anxious"
To worry / be pulled apart with care
Repeated six times in this section. The root sense is a divided, distracted mind torn between concerns. Jesus does not forbid foresight or work — he forbids the corrosive anxiety that betrays distrust of the Father.
ζητέω
zēteō
6:33 · "seek"
To seek / pursue as a life-aim
Present imperative — a continual, governing pursuit. "Seek first" (prōton) is not first-in-sequence but first-in-priority: make the kingdom the organising centre, and the anxious "all these things" fall into their proper place.
Old Testament Roots
Exodus 16
Manna — Bread for the Day
Israel gathered manna one day at a time; hoarded manna bred worms. "Give us this day our daily bread" and "do not worry about tomorrow" both breathe the manna logic: trust the Father's provision daily rather than storing up against him.
Ezekiel 36:22–23
"Hallowed Be Your Name"
God acts to vindicate the holiness of his name profaned among the nations. The prayer's first petition — that God's name be treated as holy — takes up this deep OT concern for the honour of the Name above all else.
Psalm 37 · Proverbs 30:8
Do Not Fret; Give Me My Portion
"Do not fret because of evildoers" (Ps 37:1); "give me neither poverty nor riches, but only my daily bread" (Prov 30:8). The wisdom tradition of trusting provision and refusing anxious striving stands behind 6:25–34.
1 Kings 3:9–13
Seek First — and the Rest Is Added
Solomon asks not for wealth or long life but for wisdom to govern God's people — and God adds the rest. A living illustration of 6:33: seek the kingdom's priority first, and "all these things" follow.
Commentary Shelf
John Stott
The Message of the Sermon on the Mount (BST, 1978)
Evangelical · Pastoral
Stott frames ch.6 around the Christian's relationship to God as Father — the antidote to both religious hypocrisy and material anxiety. Devotion, money, and worry are all tests of whether we truly believe God is our Father.
On 6:1 vs 5:16: no contradiction — the difference is motive and direction. Let others see your good works so they glorify God (5:16); never parade them so they glorify you (6:1).

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Does 6:1 ("do not do your righteousness before others") contradict 5:16 ("let your light shine before others")?
No — the two verses target opposite motives. 5:16 wants good works to be visible so that people glorify the Father; 6:1 forbids parading them so that people admire you. Same visible act, opposite aim. The question is never whether others see, but whose glory you are seeking. Good works will be seen; the sin is doing them in order to be seen.
2. Is the Lord's Prayer a fixed formula to recite, or a model to follow?
Both, rightly held. Jesus says "pray like this" (a pattern) immediately after warning against "empty phrases" and vain repetition (6:7) — so it is first a model that shapes all our praying: worship, then kingdom, then needs. Yet the church has always also prayed the words themselves, and there is nothing wrong with that provided the heart is engaged. The danger Jesus names is not repetition but mindless repetition.
3. Is God's forgiveness of us conditional on our forgiving others (6:14–15)?
This is the sharpest verse in the chapter. Scripture is clear elsewhere that forgiveness is by grace through faith, not earned. The best reading: our forgiving others is the necessary evidence that we have truly received God's forgiveness — an unforgiving heart shows it has never grasped its own pardon (cf. the unforgiving servant, Matt 18). It is not that we buy God's mercy by forgiving, but that received mercy always overflows; a heart that refuses to forgive reveals it has not been melted by grace.
4. Does "do not worry" forbid planning, saving, and insurance?
No. The Greek merimnaō targets corrosive anxiety that betrays distrust, not responsible foresight. Scripture commends the ant's planning (Prov 6), Joseph's grain storage (Gen 41), and providing for one's household (1 Tim 5:8). Jesus himself assumes work ("your heavenly Father feeds them" — but birds still forage). The command forbids the anxious, faithless grasping that makes provision an idol; it does not forbid prudent stewardship done in trust.
5. "Seek first the kingdom, and all these things will be added" — is this a prosperity guarantee?
No. The "all these things" are explicitly the basics of 6:25–32 — food, drink, clothing — not wealth or success. And the promise is corporate and providential, not a personal transaction (faithful believers have gone hungry; Heb 11). The verse assures the disciple that a Father who feeds sparrows will not abandon his children to want as they pursue his kingdom — it is a call to reordered priorities under a caring Father, not a formula for guaranteed abundance.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — Devotion on Display
In a culture where public almsgiving, street-corner prayer, and disfigured fasting-faces earned real social honour, Jesus quietly detonates the whole economy of religious reputation. The Father in secret, not the watching crowd, is the audience that counts. And to peasants who lived hand-to-mouth, "do not worry" was not naïve advice but a call to trust the God who feeds the birds.
Now — Curated Piety & Chronic Anxiety
We have industrialised the very thing Jesus warned about — devotion performed for an audience, now with metrics. And we are perhaps the most anxious generation about money and the future ever to read these words. Matthew 6 still asks the two questions underneath it all: whose applause are you living for, and whom do you actually trust with tomorrow?
Question to sit with: If no one ever saw my giving, praying, or serving — and if I truly believed the Father would provide — what in my life would change?
Controlling Idea
"Piety, possessions, and worry are all symptoms of one question — who are you living for? The cure for all three is a Father who sees in secret, provides day by day, and is worth seeking first."
Matthew 6:33 — "Seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well."
