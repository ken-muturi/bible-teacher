Why We Praise & Worship God

Anchor text: Psalm 95:1–7 (NIV) · Deep study with C. S. Lewis and John Piper

One-line summary: Psalm 95 gives two calls to worship, each with a “because” — and the second half of the psalm warns what happens when worship goes quiet.

Anchor passage

“Come, let us bow down in worship, let us kneel before the Lord our Maker.” Psalm 95:6 · NIV

Placement: Psalm 95 opens Book IV of the Psalter, a run of psalms about the Lord as King. It is a call to worship used in a gathered community — “come,” “let us” — not a private mood.

Structure map — two calls, two reasons, one warning

Call 1 · vv. 1–2

Sing. Shout. Give thanks. Loud, joyful, bodily. Worship is invited to be noisy.

Reason 1 · vv. 3–5

“For” the great King. He is above all gods; depths, peaks, sea and land are his. Worship answers who he is.

Call 2 · v. 6

Bow. Kneel. The mood drops from shouting to stillness. Both belong.

Reason 2 · v. 7a

“For” he is our God. We are his flock. Worship answers what he has done and who we are to him.

Warning · vv. 7b–11

“Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.” The psalm turns to Meribah and Massah: Israel had seen wonders and still grumbled. Worship is tied to listening and obeying. [VERIFY: quote wording in NIV before teaching]

Word studies

Praise

hālal (הלל) → Hallelujah

“To boast, to celebrate, to speak well of out loud.” Hallelujah means “praise Yah” — a command to a crowd. Lost in English: the word is public and a little boastful, the way you boast about someone you admire.

Worship / bow down

shāḥâ (שחה) · proskynēo (προσκυνέω)

Hebrew: to bend low, prostrate. The Greek NT word is the standard equivalent, often a bowing or kneeling posture. In John 4:24 Jesus keeps the word and moves the focus to “spirit and truth.” Lost in English: “worship” can sound like a music slot; the Bible word is a posture of the whole person. [VERIFY: Greek etymology “kiss toward” is debated]

Thanks

tôdâ / yādâ (תודה / ידה)

Thanksgiving and public confession of who God is. Thanks is not private politeness; it is testimony (“I tell what he did”).

Glory

kābôd (כבוד)

From the idea of “weight.” To give God glory is to treat him as heavy, valuable, worth taking seriously. Lost in English: “glory” sounds like shine; the root says substance.

Historical and cultural context

Context

Ancient Israel lived among nations that praised many gods for rain, war and harvest. Psalm 95:3 makes a bold claim in that setting: the Lord is “great… above all gods.” Praise was also political: Israel’s King was not Pharaoh or a neighbour’s idol.

The psalm was used in gathered worship (a call “let us come,” “let us sing”) and is quoted at length in Hebrews 3–4, where the “today” warning is applied to the church. [VERIFY: Hebrews 3:7–4:11 use of Psalm 95; Hebrews 4:7 attributes it to David]

The “why” — five reasons Scripture gives

1 · He is worthy

Rev 4:11 — “You are worthy… to receive glory.” Praise is the right response to what is true.

2 · He made us

Psalm 95:6; 100:3. We worship the Maker because we are not self-made.

3 · He saved us

Exod 15 (the song at the sea); Eph 1:3–14. Praise is what rescued people do.

4 · He is good to be with

Ps 16:11 — “fullness of joy” in his presence; Ps 73:25–26. Worship is where delight is allowed.

5 · Worship forms us

Ps 115:8 — we become like what we worship. Praise reorders what we love.

And a limit

Praise without listening is hollow: Ps 95:7b–8; Isa 29:13; Amos 5:23–24.

Why does God command praise? Is it for his good or ours?
