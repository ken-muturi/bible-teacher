Matthew 13:44–46
The Hidden Treasure & the Pearl of Great Price — Who is the "man"?
Deep Study
⊕ Simple one-page version
Passage — Matthew 13:44–46 (NIV)
The Two Twin Parables
"The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field. When a man found it, he hid it again, and then in his joy went and sold all he had and bought that field.

"Again, the kingdom of heaven is like a merchant looking for fine pearls. When he found one of great value, he went away and sold everything he had and bought it."
Matthew 13:44–46 (NIV)
Placement in Matthew: These are the fifth and sixth of the seven kingdom parables in Matthew 13 — the third of Matthew's five great discourses. They form a matched pair at the centre of the chapter, set between the Mustard Seed/Leaven (the kingdom's growth) and the Dragnet (the kingdom's final sorting). Crucially, just six verses earlier (13:38) Jesus interprets the Parable of the Weeds and states plainly: "the field is the world." That single line is the strongest interpretive control on the question "who is the man?" — because if the field is the world, the man who buys the whole field to obtain the treasure hidden in it looks far more like the world's Redeemer than like an individual seeker buying his own salvation.
The Pair — Same Shape, One Key Difference
Why Jesus Tells It Twice
Twin parables in Matthew reinforce a single point from two angles. The doubling is emphasis, not repetition.
Treasure (v.44) — Found by Surprise
A labourer, probably a hired hand ploughing a field he does not own, strikes buried treasure by accident. In a land repeatedly overrun by armies, burying valuables was common (cf. the buried talent, Matt 25:25). He has not been looking. The discovery is sheer grace — it falls on him.
Pearl (vv.45–46) — Found by Searching
A wholesale merchant (emporos) is a professional, deliberately seeking fine pearls. Pearls were among the most prized treasures of the ancient world — Pliny ranked them above all gems. This man knows value when he sees it, and the supreme pearl ends his search.
The shared core in both: a discovery of supreme worth → the selling of everything → the buying of the prize, all driven by joy. The difference (accident vs. search) is exactly what lets the pair be read two ways — and is why the "who is the man?" question is genuinely open.
Word Studies — Five Key Terms
ἀγοράζω
agorazō
v.44, 46 — "bought"
To buy / purchase / redeem
The hinge word of the whole debate. From agora (marketplace). The NT repeatedly uses it for Christ purchasing his people: "you were bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:20; 7:23), "Christ redeemed us" (Gal 3:13), and the redeemed are those "purchased from the earth" (Rev 5:9; 14:3–4). The fact that the man buys tilts the parable toward Christ — and away from the believer, who cannot buy salvation.
ESV/NIV: "bought" · KJV: "buyeth"
ἀγρός
agros
v.44 — "field"
Field / open country / the world
The same word Jesus defines in 13:38: "the field (agros) is the world (kosmos)." This is decisive context. If the field is the world, the man buys the entire world to secure the treasure hidden in it — which fits Christ redeeming the world to win his people, far better than a believer buying a field he wants for himself.
ESV/NIV: "field" · 13:38 "the field is the world"
θησαυρός
thēsauros
v.44 — "treasure"
Treasure / stored-up wealth
In the OT, Israel is God's segullah — his "treasured possession" (Exod 19:5; Deut 7:6; Mal 3:17). The image of God's people as his hidden treasure has deep roots. Read this way, the treasure is the church/the elect, hidden in the field of the world, which Christ comes to recover. The same root gives "treasure in heaven" (Matt 6:21).
ESV/NIV: "treasure" · LXX Exod 19:5 segullah
μαργαρίτης
margaritēs
vv.45–46 — "pearl"
Pearl — singular, supreme
A pearl is unique among treasures: it is organic, formed slowly inside a living oyster around a wound or irritant, and cannot be cut or improved — only found whole. Many of the Fathers saw the church born from Christ's pierced side as the one pearl, costly and singular. Note: it is one pearl, not many — a single, unified, beloved people.
ESV/NIV: "pearl" · cf. Rev 21:21 pearly gates
χαρά
chara
v.44 — "in his joy"
Joy / gladness
The motive of the sale is joy, not grim sacrifice. He sells "in his joy." This single word echoes Hebrews 12:2 — "for the joy set before him he endured the cross." On the Christ reading, the joy that drove Calvary is the joy of this man buying the field. On the believer reading, it is the gladness that makes radical discipleship feel like gain, not loss (cf. Phil 3:7–8).
ESV/NIV: "in his joy" · cf. Heb 12:2
The Core Question — Who is the "Man"?
Two Readings of the Buyer
Reading A — The Man is the Believer
The kingdom is worth everything you have
Intuitive and common in devotional preaching: when you truly see the kingdom's worth, you gladly give up all to gain it.
Fits Paul "counting all things loss" for Christ (Phil 3:7–8) and the call to forsake all (Luke 14:33).
Honours real human response — repentance, discipleship, wholehearted commitment.
Problem 1: You cannot buy the kingdom — it is free (Isa 55:1; Rom 6:23; Eph 2:8–9). A purchase sits awkwardly with grace.
Problem 2: "None seeks for God" (Rom 3:11) — yet this reading makes the human the worthy seeker.
Reading B — The Man is Christ
Christ gave all to buy his people
The treasure / pearl = the church, the redeemed; the buyer = Christ, who "sold all he had" (Phil 2:7) to buy them.
Anchored in 13:38: "the field is the world" — he buys the whole world to secure the treasure in it.
"For the joy set before him" (Heb 12:2) maps exactly onto the man who buys "in his joy."
The price is his blood (Acts 20:28; 1 Pet 1:18–19; Rev 5:9) — agorazō is the redemption verb.
Keeps grace intact: he pays; we are the treasure he counted worth everything.
The judgment of this study: Reading B (Christ as buyer) is the more defensible, for three reasons — (1) Matthew 13:38 already tells us the field is the world; (2) the gospel does not let anyone purchase the kingdom; (3) "sold all… for joy" maps cleanly onto Philippians 2 and Hebrews 12:2. But Reading A is not false — the kingdom truly is worth everything, and that call is genuinely taught in Scripture; it is simply better drawn from Luke 14:33 or Philippians 3 than from the buyer in these two parables. The best sermon holds both: the kingdom is so valuable a person would give everything for it — and the deeper wonder is that Christ already did, and you were the treasure he bought.
Structure — Three Beats (Identical in Both)
1
The Discovery
Something of Supreme Worth is Found
Treasure in a field; the one perfect pearl. In both, the value of the find is so far beyond everything else that comparison ends. On the Christ reading: the Son sees his people and counts them worth everything. On the believer reading: the soul finally sees what the kingdom is.
2
The Cost
He Sells ALL That He Has
No half-measures, no negotiation — total divestment. "Sold all he had" / "sold everything." On the Christ reading this is Philippians 2:7, the self-emptying to the point of the cross. On the believer reading it is Luke 14:33, the cost of discipleship. The word "all" is absolute either way.
3
The Purchase — driven by joy
He Buys It
The transaction completes "in his joy." The cost is real but never grudging — it is gladly paid because the prize is worth infinitely more. This joy is the emotional engine of both parables and the strongest tie to Hebrews 12:2.
Biblical Background — God's "Treasured Possession"
Exodus 19:5 · Deut 7:6 · 26:18
Israel as God's Segullah
God repeatedly calls Israel his segullah — "treasured possession," the personal treasure a king keeps for himself above all his holdings. This is the deep OT root for reading the treasure as God's people, not as an abstract "kingdom." The treasure in the field already has a biblical identity: it is the covenant people.
"You will be my treasured possession out of all nations." — Exod 19:5
Malachi 3:17
"They Will Be Mine… My Treasured Possession"
In the very last book of the OT, God promises that on the day he acts, the faithful "will be mine… my treasured possession (segullah)." The hope that God will one day gather and claim his hidden treasure stands directly behind the parable of a man who buys a field to recover the treasure in it.
"They will be my treasured possession… I will spare them." — Mal 3:17
1 Peter 2:9 · Titus 2:14
The Church as the Purchased People
The NT applies the same language to the church: "a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9), "a people that are his very own" whom Christ "gave himself to redeem" (Titus 2:14). The treasure motif lands on the redeemed community — bought at cost, treasured by God.
"…a people for his own possession." — 1 Pet 2:9
Isaiah 53:11 · Hebrews 12:2
The Joy That Drove the Cost
"Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied" (Isa 53:11); "for the joy set before him he endured the cross" (Heb 12:2). The buyer's joy in v.44 is not incidental — it matches the scriptural portrait of a Redeemer who paid the ultimate price gladly, for the sake of those he was winning.
"For the joy set before him he endured the cross." — Heb 12:2
Commentary Shelf — Both Readings Represented
R. T. France
The Gospel of Matthew (NICNT, 2007)
Evangelical ScholarlyReading A
France reads the man as the disciple and resists allegorising every detail. The point of the pair is the overwhelming worth of the kingdom and the wholehearted, glad response it demands — the selling of all is not a transaction earning salvation but a picture of total reorientation around the kingdom's supreme value.
On the accident-vs-search contrast: France notes the pair deliberately covers both the person who stumbles on the kingdom unawares and the lifelong seeker — so that no one is excluded by how they came to it.

D. A. Carson
Matthew (Expositor's Bible Commentary, 1984)
Conservative EvangelicalReading A
Carson argues the buyer is the person who grasps the kingdom's worth; the "selling all" expresses the radical, joyful renunciation discipleship requires. He cautions strongly against the Christ-buys-us allegory on the grounds that it makes the kingdom something Christ purchases rather than something he brings — and risks confusing the parable's plain thrust.
On joy: Carson stresses that the renunciation is glad, not reluctant — "the man is so happy he can hardly believe his good fortune." The kingdom is gain, not deprivation.

Charles Spurgeon
Sermons / The Treasure Hid in the Field (1890s)
Reformed Baptist · PreachingReading B
Spurgeon preaches the man as Christ: "Who is the merchantman? It is the Lord Jesus… He sold all that He had to buy us." The treasure and the pearl are the church, and the price is Calvary. He delights that we were valued so highly that the Son emptied heaven to obtain us.
He also preached the believer reading on other occasions — for Spurgeon both are true, but the Christ reading is the deeper and more humbling, because it makes us the prize rather than the purchaser.

John Nolland
The Gospel of Matthew (NIGTC, 2005)
Critical / ExegeticalReading A
Nolland anchors the pair in the response of the hearer: the kingdom so eclipses all competing values that letting go of everything else becomes the only rational, joyful choice. He treats the two parables as a unit emphasising the surpassing worth that reorders a life.
He observes the parables say nothing about earning — the "buying" is a vivid picture of cost and commitment within the story world, not a doctrine of purchased salvation; pressing the economics too hard distorts the genre.

Tim Keller
King's Cross / Preaching the Parables
Reformed · PastoralBoth — held together
Keller's signature move: read it first as Reading A, then turn it. Yes, Jesus is the treasure worth selling all to gain — but the gospel twist is that "Jesus is the one who sold everything he had to get you. You were the treasure he saw and the pearl he bought with his blood." Grace, not effort, is the engine.
He warns that if you preach only Reading A, you produce anxious strivers; the relief comes when the hearer realises they are the bought treasure, not the buyer who must somehow afford the kingdom.

Augustine & the Fathers
Patristic readings (Tertullian, Augustine, Gregory)
PatristicReading B
A strong early stream read Christ as the merchant and the church as the one pearl, often noting the pearl's formation within a wounded, living shell as an image of the church born from Christ's pierced side. The "selling of all" was read as the incarnation and the cross.
Caution: the Fathers freely allegorised, and not every detail they pressed (the precise identity of field, treasure, pearl) is warranted by the text. Their instinct that the buyer is Christ, however, aligns with 13:38 and the agorazō word-field.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Doesn't "the field is the world" (13:38) settle it for the Christ reading?
It is the single strongest argument for Reading B, and it comes from Jesus' own lips in the same chapter. If the field is the world, the buyer purchases the whole world to obtain the treasure hidden in it — which fits Christ redeeming the world to secure his people far better than an individual buying a field he personally wants. The honest counter: Jesus interprets the weeds parable in 13:38, not necessarily these two; parables can reuse imagery differently. But the burden of proof sits on anyone who wants "field" to mean something different just a few verses later.
2. If we cannot buy salvation, how can the believer be the buyer?
This is Reading A's deepest problem. Scripture is emphatic that salvation is free and unpurchasable (Isa 55:1; Rom 6:23; Eph 2:8–9). Defenders of Reading A answer that the "buying" is not a price paid to God but a picture within the story of total cost and commitment — the same way Jesus elsewhere demands we "count the cost" (Luke 14:28–33). The "selling of all" is surrender, not payment. That is coherent — but it requires reading the purchase non-literally, which is precisely the move that makes Reading B (where the buyer really does pay a real price) feel more natural to the imagery.
3. Does the accident/search difference between the two parables matter?
Yes, and it is often missed. The treasure is found by a man not looking for it (grace breaking in unbidden); the pearl is found by a lifelong professional seeker. On Reading A this beautifully covers both conversion stories — the surprised and the searching. On Reading B the nuance shifts: Christ "stumbles" on no one, so the pair is better read as two angles on the same redeeming act rather than two types of buyer. The difference is real either way, and a good sermon names it rather than flattening the two parables into one.
4. Is it cheating to preach both readings at once?
No — provided you order them rightly. Preach Reading A as the surface call (the kingdom is worth everything), then turn to Reading B as the deeper ground (you could never afford it, but Christ already paid — and you were the treasure). This is Keller's pattern, and it guards against two errors: legalism (if only Reading A) and passivity (if only Reading B). The text's own joy-language and agorazō support the move. What you should not do is claim the text unambiguously teaches only one; the "man" is genuinely contested, and saying so is more honest than false certainty.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — Original Hearers
A Galilean crowd who knew that treasure really was buried in fields during invasions, and that a single great pearl could cost a fortune. They heard a story about a once-in-a-lifetime find worth liquidating everything to secure. Against the backdrop of Israel as God's "treasured possession," the more theologically alert would have caught the echo: God has always been the one who treasures and gathers a people.
Now — Modern Context
We instinctively make ourselves the hero of every parable — the seeker, the buyer, the one who gets it right. These twin parables resist that. Read at their deepest, they put us in the unfamiliar position of being the prize, not the purchaser — the treasure Christ saw, the pearl he bought at the cost of everything. That reframes both our worth (we are treasured) and our security (the price is already paid).
Question to sit with: When I read these parables, do I see myself as the one who must somehow afford the kingdom — or as the treasure Christ already gave everything to buy?
Controlling Idea
"The kingdom is so valuable that a person would give everything for it — and the deeper wonder is that Christ already did, and you were the treasure he bought."
Matthew 13:44 — "in his joy he went and sold all he had and bought that field." The most defensible reading: the man is Christ; the treasure is you.
