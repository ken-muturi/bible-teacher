2 Samuel 12:1–14

2 Samuel  ·  Prophetic Confrontation  ·  NIV
Nathan's parable — the trap that makes David judge himself before he knows he is the accused
The Turn
v.1–4 — Nathan tells of a rich man who takes a poor man's beloved ewe lamb to feed a guest, rather than one from his own flock.

v.5–6 — David burns with anger: "The man who did this must die! He must pay back four times the amount."

v.7 — "Then Nathan said to David: 'You are the man!'"
2 Samuel 12:1–14 (NIV)  ·  Follows chapter 11: adultery with Bathsheba, Uriah's engineered death, the cover-up
Sent
šālaḥ (Hebrew)
God sent Nathan — a formal prophetic commission. Nathan is not acting on personal courage; he is dispatched. The same verb used of Moses, Elijah, Isaiah.
Ewe lamb
kibśâ (Hebrew)
A diminutive form — small, tender. The poor man raised it "like a daughter." The word choice is designed to make David feel the attachment before he knows who he is judging.
Structure — Three Beats
1
vv.1–4 — The Trap Set
The Parable
Rich man takes the poor man's beloved lamb. Nathan builds maximum emotional attachment before the verdict lands.
2
vv.5–7a — The Trap Closes
"You are the man"
David pronounces his own sentence. Nathan's reversal is a single line — no argument, no defence. The parable has done its work.
3
vv.7b–14 — The Indictment
God's Charge + Consequence
God rehearses his gifts to David. David had everything — and took the one thing that wasn't his. Four consequences declared.
Teaching Illustrations
Rhetoric — The Indirect Accusation
A direct accusation of a king could mean death for the accuser. Nathan does something more dangerous to David: he gets David to convict himself first. The verdict is already out of David's mouth before he knows who the defendant is. Self-judgment lands deeper than external judgment — David can't dismiss it as Nathan's opinion.
Everyday — The Blind Spot
A manager who demands honesty from his team while quietly padding his expense report. A parent who lectures a child on fairness while playing favourites. The passage names the gap between moral clarity about others and moral blindness about ourselves — not as hypocrisy but as the ordinary condition Nathan is exploiting.
Historical Context

Royal impunity: Ancient Near Eastern kings had near-absolute power over life, property, and women. David's actions in ch.11 were not unusual for the political world around him — but they were being measured against a different standard. The covenant God, not the surrounding kings, set the terms.

Nathan's risk: Prophets who displeased kings were killed (cf. 1 Kings 18:4). The parable form is not just rhetoric — it is survival strategy. Nathan needs David to be inside the story before he can name him in it. [VERIFY: See Walter Brueggemann, First and Second Samuel, Interpretation Commentary]

What the Text Is Not Saying

Not that David loses his salvation — God's response (v.13) is immediate: "The LORD has taken away your sin." Forgiveness and consequence coexist.

Not that the consequences are punishment in the retributive sense — they are the structural outworking of what David set in motion. Violence in the household mirrors the violence David used on Uriah.

Then — Original Audience
For Israel, this passage established that the covenant king was not above the covenant law. God's choice of David did not grant immunity. Nathan's access to the king — and the king's accountability — were markers of Israel's distinction from the surrounding nations.
Now — Your Audience
The passage confronts the gap between moral clarity and self-knowledge — how readily we see injustice in others and how slowly we see it in ourselves. Nathan's method is also worth sitting with: truth reaches people most effectively when they have already agreed with it before they know it applies to them.
The Controlling Idea
