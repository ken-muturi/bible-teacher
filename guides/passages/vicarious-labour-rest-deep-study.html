Deep Study · Thematic
For the Sake of the Child
Vicarious Labour — pain carried for another — and the rest that it purchases
Deep Study John 16:21 · Gal 4:19 · Isa 53 · Matt 11:28 Rest · Comfort · Nursing
"A woman giving birth to a child has pain because her time has come; but when her baby is born she forgets the anguish because of her joy that a child is born into the world."
John 16:21 (NIV)
The Asymmetry — What Makes This Different from Ordinary Suffering
The mother carries every bit of the cost. The child carries none of it.
What belongs to the mother
The contractions — all of them
The tearing, the exhaustion, the risk
The hours of labour before the birth
The anguish that is later forgotten
The cost of the child's existence
What belongs to the child
Life — received, not earned
The world they are born into
The arms that receive them
The nourishment that follows
A life that cost them nothing
This is not suffering with a silver lining. This is suffering whose entire purpose exists in someone else.
The mother does not labour to produce something for herself. She labours so that another can live.
Three Acts — The Full Arc of the Metaphor
Act I
Labour
John 16:21a · Gal 4:19 · Isa 53:4–6 · Rom 8:26 · Matt 27:45–46
The Agony Carried for Another
Someone is in pain. But the pain does not belong to them in the ordinary sense — it is pain taken on for someone else's sake. Jesus on the cross bears what the disciples, and the world, could not bear for themselves. Paul in Galatians 4 is "again in the pains of childbirth" for the Galatians — his anguish is entirely for them. The Spirit "groans" on our behalf in Romans 8:26 with words we cannot form ourselves. In each case: the labourer carries pain that belongs to, or is for, another.
Act II
Birth
John 16:21b · Isa 53:10–11 · John 19:30 · Gal 4:19b
The Child Is Born — "It Is Finished"
The labour ends. Not in defeat but in delivery. Isaiah 53:11 — "after he has suffered, he will see the light of life and be satisfied." Jesus on the cross: "It is finished" (τετέλεσται — tetelestai, perfect tense: completed, accomplished, permanently done). The child is born into the world. The Servant sees the fruit of his travail. Paul's labour finds its goal when "Christ is formed in you." The birth is an event — actual, datable, irreversible.
Act III
Rest
Matt 11:28–30 · Isa 66:12–13 · Isa 49:15 · Heb 4:14–16 · John 16:22
The Child Rests in the Arms That Laboured
After the birth, the mother holds the child. The same body that bore the labour now becomes the place of comfort, warmth, and rest. In Isaiah 66, God says: "I will extend peace to her like a river... As a mother comforts her child, so I will comfort you." In Matthew 11, Jesus — who has himself borne the cost — invites the exhausted to come to him and rest. The one who laboured for others is also the one who gives rest to others. The comfort given is the comfort of the one who paid the price of the child's life.
Who Is Labouring For Whom — Three Dimensions
The Cross
Jesus labours for the world
Isaiah 53 · John 16:21 · Hebrews 12:2
The cross is vicarious birth-labour at its most absolute. Jesus bears pain that entirely belongs to others — "he was pierced for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities" (Isa 53:5). His suffering produces nothing for himself. What it produces is the life of all who are born from it. Hebrews 12:2 — "for the joy set before him he endured the cross." The joy was not his own relief. The joy was the children who would live because of his labour.
"By his wounds we are healed." — Isaiah 53:5
The Minister
Paul labours for the Galatians
Galatians 4:19 · Col 1:24 · Rom 9:1–3
Galatians 4:19 is the most explicit use of birth-pain language for vicarious ministry: "My dear children, for whom I am again in the pains of childbirth until Christ is formed in you." Paul places himself in the position of the labouring mother — his anguish has no benefit for himself. Its sole purpose is that the Galatians arrive at full Christ-formed life. In Romans 9:1–3 the same logic reaches its extreme: "I could wish that I myself were cursed and cut off from Christ — for the sake of my people."
"I am again in the pains of childbirth until Christ is formed in you." — Galatians 4:19
The Spirit
The Spirit labours for us
Romans 8:26–27
Romans 8:26 — "the Spirit intercedes for us with groans (στεναγμοῖς — stenagmois) that words cannot express." The same groaning/labouring word that runs through Romans 8 (creation groaning in birth-pains, v.22; we groan inwardly, v.23) is now applied to the Spirit himself. Even now, in the midst of our weakness and incomprehension, Someone is in labour on our behalf — carrying the anguish of our prayers when we cannot form the words ourselves. We are never alone in the labour.
"The Spirit intercedes for us with groans that words cannot express." — Romans 8:26
Word Studies — Four Key Terms
עָמָל
amal (Hebrew)
Isaiah 53:11
Travail / toil / anguish
KJV translates: "the travail of his soul." The word carries both physical and emotional labour — exhausting, costly, purposeful work. The LXX uses ponos (painful toil). Isaiah 53:11 says the Servant will "see the fruit (or light) of his travail and be satisfied." The amal is entirely for others; the satisfaction is seeing them live.
What is lost: modern translations ("suffering," "anguish") lose the labour-and-birth resonance. The KJV's "travail" preserves the link to the birth-pains tradition that runs through both Testaments.
ὠδίνω
ōdinō
Galatians 4:19
To be in labour / to travail in birth
The verbal form of ōdin (birth pang). Paul uses it of himself — he is "ōdinō-ing" for the Galatians. This is extraordinary: a man describing his ministry anguish as birth-labour, placing himself in the position of the labouring mother. The word elsewhere describes literal birth-pain (Rev 12:2) and cosmic suffering (Rom 8:22). Paul claims the same category for pastoral intercession.
What is lost: "in the pains of childbirth" (NIV) is good but makes it sound like a simile. In Greek it is direct — Paul is in labour, not like a woman in labour. He has taken the mother's place.
ἀναπαύω
anapauō
Matthew 11:28
To give rest / to refresh / to cause to cease from labour
"I will give you rest" — the verb Jesus uses in Matthew 11:28 is anapauō: to cause to stop labouring, to provide relief from toil, to refresh. The word appears in the LXX for the Sabbath rest (Exod 20:11 — God "rested"). The rest Jesus offers is not inactivity but the cessation of striving under a burden you were never meant to carry alone — because someone else has already carried the heaviest part.
What is lost: "I will give you rest" sounds passive. The Greek is active and causal — Jesus will make you rest, will produce the rest in you. It is a gift, not a state you achieve by trying to relax.
παράκλησις
paraklēsis
2 Cor 1:3–7
Comfort / consolation / coming-alongside
From para (beside) + kaleō (to call) — literally "a calling-alongside." Not sympathy from a distance but presence in the same space. The same root as Paraclete (Jn 14:16 — the Holy Spirit as the one called alongside). 2 Corinthians 1:3–7 uses it ten times in five verses: God comforts us in our suffering so that we can comfort others with the same comfort. Comfort received is comfort given.
What is lost: "comfort" in English is mild. Paraklēsis is active, personal, and embodied — someone comes to where you are and stands with you. It is not an emotion but a presence.
How Vicarious Labour Brings Rest — Six Movements
The rest the child receives is not rest that the child earned. It is rest purchased by the mother's labour.
Matthew 11:28–30
Come to the One Who Has Already Laboured
"Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest." Jesus does not say "try harder" or "endure better." He says "come to me." The one who invites rest is the one who has already borne the burden that produced the new world. His yoke is easy — not because following him requires nothing, but because he has already borne the weight of what our standing before God requires. We are invited to rest in a completed work, not to replicate it.
"Come to me... and I will give you rest." — Matt 11:28
Isaiah 53:5
His Wounds, Our Healing
The Servant's suffering does not produce his own healing — it produces ours. "By his wounds we are healed." The wound belongs to the Servant. The healing belongs to us. This is the asymmetry in its most concentrated form. The comfort available to us is not comfort that we earned through suffering alongside him — it is comfort that flows directly from his having suffered in our place. The child rests because the mother laboured, not because the child also laboured.
"By his wounds we are healed." — Isaiah 53:5
Isaiah 66:12–13
The Comforting Mother After the Birth
After the birth of the new Jerusalem, God says: "As a mother comforts her child, so I will comfort you; and you will be comforted in Jerusalem." The one who bore the labour of bringing the new creation to birth is also the one who then holds and nurses the child. The comfort is not from a stranger — it is from the one who paid the cost of the child's existence. That is what makes it wholly safe. The arms that hold you are the arms that bore the birth-pains for you.
"As a mother comforts her child, so I will comfort you." — Isa 66:13
Hebrews 4:14–16
The Sympathetic High Priest
We can approach the throne of grace with confidence because our high priest "is not unable to empathise with our weaknesses" — he has been in the place of suffering, of temptation, of the full weight of human agony. He has carried what we carry, and more. Because he has laboured through it, we can approach him not with shame but with confidence — "so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need." The one who laboured for us is also the one we run to when we struggle.
"Let us then approach the throne of grace with confidence." — Heb 4:16
John 16:22
Joy That Cannot Be Taken — The Security of the Born Child
"No one will take away your joy." The born child does not need to perform to maintain its existence — it simply lives, in the life purchased by the mother's labour. The joy Jesus promises is permanent not because circumstances will be good but because the resurrection that produced it cannot be undone. The child's life is secure. Nothing can un-birth what has been born. This is what "no one will take it from you" means — not that nothing bad will happen, but that nothing can revoke the birth.
"No one will take away your joy." — John 16:22
2 Corinthians 1:3–7
Comfort Received Becomes Comfort Given
The birth-pain structure does not end with the child resting. It continues: "the God of all comfort, who comforts us in all our troubles, so that we can comfort those in any trouble with the comfort we ourselves receive from God." The child who was carried by another's labour eventually enters the same pattern — comforting others with the comfort they have received. Vicarious love reproduces itself. The birth-pains and the rest are both given to be passed on.
"We can comfort those in any trouble with the comfort we ourselves receive." — 2 Cor 1:4
The Nursing Mother — The Image Behind the Image
Isaiah 49:15 — "Can a mother forget?"
God uses the nursing mother as the image of his most indestructible commitment: "Can a mother forget the baby at her breast and have no compassion on the child she has borne? Though she may forget, I will not forget you." The nursing image follows the birth-labour: the same body that bore the pain of birth now holds the child and feeds it. The bond formed in labour is the bond that sustains in nursing.
"Though she may forget, I will not forget you. See, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands." — Isaiah 49:15–16
The Comfort of the Breast
The nursing child does not strive. It does not earn the milk. It simply receives from the one who bore the cost of its existence. This is the posture that vicarious labour makes possible — the child at rest at the breast of the one who laboured for it. Isaiah 66:11 uses this image for the restored Jerusalem: "For you will nurse and be satisfied at her comforting breasts; you will drink deeply and delight in her overflowing abundance." The rest is not earned. It is drunk.
"You will nurse and be satisfied at her comforting breasts; you will drink deeply and delight in her overflowing abundance." — Isaiah 66:11
Commentary Shelf — Scholars and Preachers
Tim Keller
Jesus the King (2011) · The Reason for God (2008) · Walking with God through Pain and Suffering (2013)
Reformed Pastoral
Keller on vicarious suffering and rest: "Christianity is the only religion that says God himself has entered fully into suffering — not to observe it from above but to absorb it from within. The rest Jesus offers in Matthew 11 is not a spiritual technique. It is the rest that flows from resting in a completed work. You stop striving because Someone Else has done the striving that counted."
On Galatians 4:19: Keller notes that Paul's birth-pains language for ministry is "one of the most emotionally candid things Paul ever wrote." It shows that genuine pastoral care is not professional service but vicarious identification — bearing the spiritual formation of others in your own body. "You know you are really praying for someone when it costs you something."
On the comfort that follows: "The comfort God gives is not the comfort of someone who understands your pain from the outside. It is the comfort of someone who has been inside it — further inside it than you have — and come out the other side with the scars to prove it."

Martin Luther
Lectures on Galatians (1535) · The Freedom of a Christian (1520)
Lutheran · Reformation
Luther on the "happy exchange" (fröhlicher Wechsel): "Christ takes our sin, our guilt, our labour — and gives us his righteousness, his standing, his rest. Like a rich bridegroom who takes on the debts of his poor bride, and in exchange gives her all his wealth." This is the vicarious structure in its most concentrated form. The bride (the church) did not labour for what she receives. The groom laboured. The exchange is entirely asymmetric — which is what makes the rest so absolute.
On Galatians 4:19 specifically: Luther says Paul shows that a minister is not a dispenser of information but a bearer of persons — "he carries the congregation in his body as a mother carries a child." This is not metaphor for Luther; it is the literal description of what faithful pastoral labour looks like. The minister's suffering has a direction: the formation of Christ in the hearers.

Charles Spurgeon
Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit · "The Weeping Prophet" · "Come Unto Me" sermons
Victorian Baptist · Expository
Spurgeon on Matthew 11:28: "He does not say 'Endure.' He says 'Come.' He does not say 'Keep striving until the weariness passes.' He says 'Come to me and I will give you rest.' The rest is a gift — given by the One who bore the labour that your standing before God required. You have not been summoned to repeat that labour. You have been summoned to receive its fruit."
On the nursing mother image: Spurgeon preached that Isaiah 49:15 represents "the most absolute pledge in Scripture." He pointed out that God does not say "I am like a mother who remembers" — he says "even if a mother forgets, I will not forget you." The comparison is set up only to be surpassed. The labour of the cross has engraved us on the palms of his hands — the nail-marks are the engraving. We are written there in scars.

Fleming Rutledge
The Crucifixion: Understanding the Death of Jesus Christ (2015)
Anglican · Systematic-Pastoral
Rutledge argues that the heart of the atonement is precisely the vicarious structure — "substitution" is not a cold legal transaction but the most intimate act of love imaginable: someone taking the place of another so the other does not have to go there. Her chapter on "The Descent into Hell" uses the birth-pains logic: the deepest suffering was entered by Christ so we would not have to enter it alone or unredeemed. "He went all the way down so that there is no depth into which you can fall where he has not already been."
On comfort: Rutledge insists that the comfort the gospel offers is not cheap reassurance but "the comfort of the last word." Because Christ has gone through death and come out the other side, death is not the last word over anyone he has laboured for. The rest available to the believer is eschatological rest — not the absence of suffering but the certainty that suffering is not the end.

John Calvin
Commentary on Galatians (1548) · Institutes of the Christian Religion (1559)
Reformed · Genevan
On Galatians 4:19 — Calvin says Paul's labour image shows that "the minister of the gospel is not merely a teacher but a midwife — he does not simply transmit information but labours for the new birth of hearers in Christ." He notes Paul says "until Christ is formed in you" — the labour has a goal and a terminus. It is not endless anguish but directional suffering aimed at a specific outcome: the Galatians arriving at full Christlikeness.
On rest and assurance: Calvin grounds Christian rest not in experience but in the objective completed work of Christ. "The troubled conscience finds no rest in anything it can do or feel. It finds rest only in the alien righteousness of Christ — a righteousness that does not belong to it, that it received entirely from outside itself, that it could not produce by any amount of labour." The rest is precisely alien — it comes from another's labour, not our own.

Tish Harrison Warren
Prayer in the Night (2021)
Anglican · Contemporary Pastoral
Warren's book, written through a season of grief and loss (including a miscarriage), engages the vicarious-suffering theme through the Compline prayer "Be present, O merciful God, and protect us through the hours of this night." She argues that the great gift of the Christian tradition in suffering is not explanations but company — the presence of the one who has been where you are and further. "We are not comforted by reasons. We are comforted by presence — the presence of the one who went through the darkest night ahead of us and left the door open."
On the nursing-mother image: Warren draws on Isaiah 49 in her chapter on grief — "what the nursing image gives us is not a God who fixes things from above but a God who holds us from below. The comfort is physical, immediate, embodied. The child does not need to understand the nourishment. It simply receives."

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Does vicarious suffering mean suffering is always for someone else — that self-directed suffering has no meaning?
No — the birth-pains metaphor in John 16:21–22 and Matthew 24:7–8 also addresses suffering that is in some sense one's own (the disciples' own grief, the church's own tribulation). The vicarious dimension is a specific and profound subset of how the metaphor functions — not its only register. Jesus's cross is uniquely and exhaustively vicarious; Paul's labour is vicariously shaped but not identical to the atonement; the Spirit's groaning is for us; and ordinary human suffering can also have vicarious fruit without being primarily defined by it. The three registers work together: suffering can be purposeful for oneself (endurance), purposeful for others (vicarious), and both simultaneously.
2. Doesn't Colossians 1:24 ("filling up Christ's afflictions") suggest the atonement was incomplete?
This is the hardest verse in this cluster. Paul cannot mean that Christ's atoning work was insufficient — that is ruled out by Hebrews 1:3, 9:12, 10:10–14 ("once for all"). The most defensible reading is that "Christ's afflictions" in Colossians 1:24 are not the atoning sufferings of the cross but the ongoing sufferings of the body of Christ in the world — the continuation of the ministry of the crucified Servant through those who carry his name. Paul fills up what is "lacking" in the sense that the full harvest of that one completed birth-labour is still being gathered in, through the ongoing vicarious ministry of those who preach and suffer for the sake of the church.
3. Is the "rest" of Matthew 11:28–30 passive — does it mean doing nothing?
Jesus says "take my yoke upon you" in the same breath as "I will give you rest." A yoke is a working implement. The rest is not the absence of effort but the transformation of its character. Yoked to Jesus, the burden is shared — "my yoke is easy and my burden is light" because he is pulling alongside. The striving that produces the rest has already been done at the cross. What remains is the lighter, collaborative, Spirit-enabled work of those who labour from a position of security rather than toward one. The child at rest in the mother's arms is not inert — it feeds, it grows, it eventually walks. But it does so from a foundation of received life, not earned existence.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — Original Hearers
The disciples in the Upper Room were hours from watching Jesus enter his agony — alone, in darkness, carrying something they could not carry with him. John 16:21 gives them a frame for understanding it afterward: what they watched him go through was labour — not for himself but for them. The birth he was labouring toward was their new life. Every time they later looked at a nursing mother they were meant to understand what the cross was doing.

For the Galatians, receiving Paul's letter: "I am in birth-pains for you" — the apostle who founded them and is now geographically absent is carrying them in his body. They were not simply recipients of teaching; they were being born.
Now — Modern Context
For anyone who has struggled to receive grace — who feels they must earn their standing, perform their salvation, re-justify their place in God's family: the birth-labour image cuts through this at the root. You did not labour to be born. You were born through Another's agony. Your standing is not the fruit of your own striving. It is the fruit of his. The rest available to you is not something you achieve — it is something you return to, again and again, by remembering whose labour produced your life.

For those who intercede for others — parents, pastors, friends, intercessors — Galatians 4:19 names what you are doing. Your sleepless nights, your unanswered prayers, your sustained grief over another person's spiritual state: this is birth-labour. It is the shape of love when love has a long way to go.
Question to sit with: Are you trying to earn a life that has already been purchased for you — or are you learning to rest in the arms of the One whose labour brought you into existence?
