Deep Study
⊕ Matthew 5 discussion guide
Controlling Verses — The Hinge of the Chapter
Matthew 5:17, 20 · Fulfilment & the higher righteousness
"Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfil them… For I tell you that unless your righteousness surpasses that of the Pharisees and the teachers of the law, you will certainly not enter the kingdom of heaven."
Matthew 5:17, 20 (NIV) — the interpretive key to the whole Sermon
Placement: Matthew 5 opens the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7), the first of the five great teaching discourses around which Matthew structures his Gospel. Jesus goes up on a mountain and sits to teach — Matthew is deliberately painting him as a new and greater Moses, delivering the charter of the kingdom from a new Sinai. The audience is the disciples, with the crowds listening in (5:1; 7:28). Chapter 5 moves in four movements: the Beatitudes (the character of kingdom people), salt & light (their influence), Jesus and the Law (5:17–20, the thesis), and six antitheses that show what "surpassing righteousness" actually looks like — reaching past the action to the heart.
Structure — Four Movements
5:3–12
Movement 1 — Character
The Beatitudes
Eight (or nine) pronouncements of blessing on the least likely people — the poor in spirit, mourners, the meek, the persecuted. Not entry requirements to earn, but a portrait of the citizen of the kingdom and the gifts that are theirs.
5:13–16
Movement 2 — Influence
Salt of the Earth, Light of the World
Kingdom character is not private. Two metaphors: salt (preserving, flavouring — useless if it loses its distinctiveness) and light (a city on a hill, a lamp on a stand — pointless if hidden). The goal: good works that make people glorify the Father.
5:17–20
Movement 3 — Thesis
Christ and the Law: Fulfil, Not Abolish
Jesus affirms the whole Law and Prophets and says he came to fulfil them. The hinge (v.20): a righteousness that must surpass the scrupulous outward observance of the Pharisees — a righteousness of the heart, not merely the hands.
5:21–48
Movement 4 — Demonstration
The Six Antitheses: "But I Say to You"
Six case studies of the higher righteousness — anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, enemies. In each, Jesus drives the command inward and forward, exposing the heart behind the act and calling for a love that mirrors the Father (v.48).
The Beatitudes — Eight Portraits of the Blessed (5:3–10)
5:3
The poor in spirit
theirs is the kingdom of heaven
Spiritual bankruptcy — those who know they have nothing to bring. The kingdom opens to empty hands, not full ones. The gateway beatitude.
5:4
Those who mourn
they will be comforted
Grief over sin and a broken world — not stoic detachment. Echoes Isaiah 61's comfort for those who mourn in Zion.
5:5
The meek
they will inherit the earth
Strength under control, not weakness. A direct quote of Psalm 37:11 — the opposite of the world's grasping self-assertion.
5:6
Those who hunger & thirst for righteousness
they will be filled
A craving for right-ness — personal holiness and public justice. The only appetite Jesus promises will be fully satisfied.
5:7
The merciful
they will be shown mercy
Those who forgive and give freely. The measure they use is measured back — mercy given flows from mercy received.
5:8
The pure in heart
they will see God
Single-minded, undivided devotion — not sinless perfection but an unmixed heart. The great promise: the vision of God himself.
5:9
The peacemakers
they will be called children of God
Not peace-keepers who avoid conflict, but peace-makers who create it at cost — doing the family work of God the reconciler.
5:10–12
The persecuted for righteousness
theirs is the kingdom of heaven
The list ends where it began — the kingdom. Suffering for Jesus is not proof of failure but of belonging, and it puts them in the line of the prophets.
Read the shape: the first and eighth beatitudes share the same promise — "theirs is the kingdom of heaven" — bracketing the whole list (an inclusio). Everything between is what belonging to that kingdom looks like. The verbs are almost all future passive ("they will be comforted / filled / shown mercy") — God is the one who acts. These are not achievements that earn blessing; they are a description of the people God is blessing, with the gifts he will surely give.
Salt & Light — Kingdom Influence (5:13–16)
Salt of the Earth
Salt preserved, purified, and flavoured — indispensable and distinct. Its whole value is in not being like what surrounds it. The warning: salt that "loses its saltiness" is good for nothing. A disciple who blends indistinguishably into the world has forfeited the one thing that made them useful.
Light of the World
A city on a hill cannot be hidden; a lamp is lit to be set on a stand. Light exists to be seen. The warning is the mirror image of salt: not loss of distinctiveness but concealment. And the aim is pointed carefully — not "that they may see you" but "that they may… glorify your Father."
The Six Antitheses — "You Have Heard… But I Say" (5:21–48)
Murder
21–26
You have heard
"Do not murder"; the killer is liable to judgment.
But I say
Anger and contempt ("Raca," "You fool") already put you under judgment. Be reconciled before you offer your gift.
Adultery
27–30
You have heard
"Do not commit adultery."
But I say
Lustful looking is adultery of the heart. Deal ruthlessly with what causes you to stumble — "tear it out," better to lose a part than the whole.
Divorce
31–32
You have heard
"Give her a certificate of divorce" (a legal loophole culture leaned on).
But I say
Divorce is not a casual paperwork matter; it exposes people to adultery. Marriage is to be protected, not conveniently dissolved.
Oaths
33–37
You have heard
"Do not break your oath; keep your vows to the Lord."
But I say
Do not swear at all. Let your "Yes" be yes and your "No," no. Truthfulness so total it needs no oath to prop it up.
Retaliation
38–42
You have heard
"Eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (the lex talionis).
But I say
Do not resist an evil person with revenge. Turn the other cheek, go the second mile, give freely — refuse the cycle of retaliation.
Enemies
43–48
You have heard
"Love your neighbour and hate your enemy" (the second half was folk addition, not Torah).
But I say
Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you — so that you reflect the Father, who sends sun and rain on all. "Be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect."
Word Studies — Six Key Terms
μακάριος
makarios
5:3–11 · "blessed"
Blessed / flourishing / truly well-off
Not primarily "happy" (a feeling) but a state of God-given flourishing — to be congratulated, in the deepest sense. Jesus pronounces this over people the world pities. It is a declaration of objective favour, not a mood.
πληρόω
plēroō
5:17 · "fulfil"
To fulfil / fill up / bring to completion
The key verb of v.17. Jesus does not abolish (kataluō) the Law but brings it to its intended goal — completing its meaning, embodying its aim, and revealing the heart it always pointed toward. The Law finds its destination in him.
δικαιοσύνη
dikaiosynē
5:6, 10, 20 · "righteousness"
Righteousness / justice / right-ness
The thread running through the chapter: hungered for (v.6), suffered for (v.10), and required to surpass the Pharisees' (v.20). Both inward conformity to God's character and outward doing of justice — never one without the other.
πραΰς
praüs
5:5 · "meek"
Meek / gentle / power under control
Not spinelessness. Used of a war-horse broken to the bridle — strength harnessed, not absent. Jesus applies it to himself ("gentle and humble in heart," 11:29). The meek inherit precisely what the aggressive grab for.
τέλειος
teleios
5:48 · "perfect"
Perfect / complete / mature / whole
Less "flawless" than "brought to its intended end." In context (loving enemies), it means a love that is complete — extending even to enemies, as the Father's does. A goal that both humbles and pulls us forward, not a claim we can tick off.
ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω
egō de legō
5:22, 28, 32… · "but I say"
"But I myself say to you"
The stunning refrain of the antitheses. No prophet said "but I say"; they said "thus says the LORD." Jesus sets his own word alongside — indeed above — the received reading of Scripture, an implicit claim to divine authority the crowds noticed (7:29).
Old Testament Roots — What Jesus Is Drawing On
Exodus 19–20
Sinai — the Mountain of the Law
Moses ascends the mountain to receive the Law; Jesus ascends a mountain to give its true meaning. Matthew casts him as the new and greater Moses — but where Moses relayed God's words, Jesus speaks as their author: "But I say to you."
Psalm 1 · Psalm 37:11
The "Blessed" Man & the Meek
The Beatitudes stand in the wisdom tradition of "blessed is the one who…" (Ps 1:1). The third beatitude quotes Psalm 37:11 almost verbatim — "the meek shall inherit the land" — reframing the Psalm's patient trust as kingdom hope.
Isaiah 61:1–3
Good News to the Poor, Comfort to Mourners
The opening beatitudes echo Isaiah's Servant, anointed to bring good news to the poor and comfort to those who mourn — the very text Jesus reads in Luke 4. The Beatitudes announce that the promised age of comfort has arrived in him.
Leviticus 19:18 · 24:20
Love of Neighbour & the Talion
"Eye for eye" (Lev 24:20) was a limit on vengeance for the courts, not a licence for personal revenge; "love your neighbour" (Lev 19:18) is Torah, but "hate your enemy" was never in it. Jesus strips away the distortions and extends the command to its limit.
Commentary Shelf
John Stott
The Message of the Sermon on the Mount (BST, 1978)
Evangelical · Pastoral
Stott's controlling theme: the Sermon describes the Christian counter-culture — a community whose values run directly against the surrounding world. The Beatitudes and antitheses are not impossible ideals but the visible distinctiveness of kingdom people.
On 5:20: the "greater righteousness" is not more rules but a deeper, inward reality — righteousness of the heart, not merely conduct — that only God can produce.

D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones
Studies in the Sermon on the Mount (1959–60)
Reformed · Preaching
Lloyd-Jones insists the Sermon is a portrait of what every Christian is meant to be — not a set of entry conditions but the necessary evidence of regeneration. The Beatitudes describe character in a logical order, beginning with poverty of spirit as the foundation of all the rest.
Emphatic that you cannot live this by willpower; the Sermon drives you first to the cross for a new heart, and only then can it be obeyed.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer
The Cost of Discipleship (1937)
Lutheran · Discipleship
Bonhoeffer reads the Sermon as the call of "costly grace" — the summons to concrete obedience that follows Christ. Against "cheap grace" that spiritualises the commands away, he insists "only he who believes is obedient, and only he who is obedient believes."
On loving enemies: this is precisely where the disciple's life becomes visibly extraordinary (perisson) — the "more" that distinguishes the follower of Jesus from the world.

Evangelical Scholarly
France stresses the "greater righteousness" (5:20) as the heading over the whole of 5:21–48: the antitheses are not new laws but illustrations of the radical, heart-level obedience the kingdom calls for, exceeding mere external conformity.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Are the Beatitudes entry requirements — things I must achieve to be blessed?
No. The verbs are future passive ("they will be comforted, filled, shown mercy") — God is the actor. And they open with "poor in spirit," the confession of spiritual bankruptcy. You do not qualify for the kingdom by becoming meek and merciful; Jesus pronounces blessing on people who already belong to the kingdom and describes the life the Spirit produces in them. Gift precedes demand. Read as a ladder of self-improvement, the Beatitudes crush; read as a description of grace, they comfort and then shape.
2. In the antitheses, is Jesus contradicting or correcting Moses?
Neither abolishing nor contradicting — he says so plainly (v.17). Note the wording: "you have heard that it was said," not "God said." Jesus is opposing the received oral interpretation — the scribal tradition that reduced God's law to manageable external rules ("don't murder," "hate your enemy") — and restoring its full, heart-deep intent. He is not raising the Law's true standard; he is exposing how far the popular reading had lowered it. He fulfils the Law by revealing what it always meant.
3. "Be perfect" (5:48) — is sinless perfection actually commanded?
Teleios means "complete / brought to its goal," not flawless in the modern sense. In context it caps the command to love enemies: as the Father's love is complete — reaching even to the ungrateful — so should ours be. Luke's parallel reads "be merciful as your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36), confirming the sense. It is a real call to Godlike, all-embracing love that we pursue by grace and never fully arrive at in this life — a horizon, not a checkbox.
4. Does "turn the other cheek" (5:38–42) forbid all justice and self-defence?
The target is personal retaliation and revenge, not the God-given role of courts and government to restrain evil (cf. Rom 13:1–4). "Eye for eye" was itself a judicial limit on vengeance; Jesus goes further, forbidding the disciple to answer personal insult and injury with retaliation. It is a call to absorb wrong rather than escalate it — the way of the cross — not a blanket abolition of justice or a command to enable ongoing abuse. Wisdom is required to apply it without weaponising it against victims.
5. Can anyone actually live this? Is the Sermon realistic?
Not by willpower — and that may be part of the point. The Sermon sets a standard so high (anger is murder, lust is adultery, love your enemies, be perfect) that it strips away all self-righteousness and drives the hearer to the first beatitude: "blessed are the poor in spirit." It functions like a mirror, showing our need, and like a map, showing the Spirit-shaped life now possible in Christ. Divorced from grace it is despair; rooted in grace it is the genuine, if unfinished, shape of discipleship.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — On the Mountainside
To a crowd under Roman occupation, taught by scribes who had turned the Law into a checklist of externals, Jesus redraws the whole picture. Blessing belongs not to the powerful and pious but to the poor in spirit and the persecuted. Righteousness is not surface compliance but a transformed heart. And he teaches it all with an authority — "but I say to you" — that no rabbi had ever claimed.
Now — In the Pew and the Street
We still domesticate the Sermon — admiring it, quoting it, and quietly assuming it can't really be meant. But it still measures the gap between managed behaviour and a changed heart, still calls the church to be salt that stays distinct and light that isn't hidden, still asks whether we love the people hardest to love. And it still drives us, when we're honest, to the empty-handed poverty of spirit where the kingdom is given, not earned.
Question to sit with: Where am I settling for the outward act while the heart behind it goes unchanged — and what would it mean to bring that to Jesus rather than manage it myself?
Controlling Idea
"The kingdom belongs to the empty-handed, and its righteousness is not the polished surface of behaviour but a transformed heart — a standard so high it drives us to grace, and so real it reshapes the life grace gives."
Matthew 5:20 — "unless your righteousness surpasses that of the Pharisees… you will certainly not enter the kingdom of heaven."
