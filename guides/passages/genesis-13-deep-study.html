Genesis 13
Abram & Lot Separate — Faith That Lets Go vs. Sight That Grabs
Deep Study
Anchor Text — The Two Choices
Genesis 13:10–11, 14–15 · Lot lifts his eyes; God lifts Abram's
"Lot lifted up his eyes and saw that the plain of the Jordan was well watered everywhere like the garden of the LORD… So Lot chose for himself all the plain of the Jordan… and Lot journeyed east. Thus they separated from each other… The LORD said to Abram, after Lot had separated from him, 'Lift up your eyes and look from the place where you are… for all the land that you see I will give to you and your offspring forever.'"
Genesis 13:10–11, 14–15 (ESV) — the same phrase, two opposite directions
The setting: Genesis 13 follows Abram's lapse in Egypt (12:10–20), where fear drove him to lie about Sarai. Now he returns — "very rich in livestock, in silver and in gold" — and, tellingly, goes back to the altar he had first built at Bethel and "called on the name of the LORD" (13:4). The prosperity that God has given becomes the very thing that strains the family: the land cannot support both Abram's and Lot's herds, and strife breaks out. The chapter turns on a single decision — who gets first choice of the land — and on the way two men make it. Lot chooses by sight, grabbing the best-looking ground and drifting toward Sodom. Abram, though the elder and the greater, lets go, trusting God with the outcome — and it is precisely to the one who relinquished his rights that God re-confirms and expands the promise of the whole land.
Structure — Five Movements
13:1–4
Movement 1 — Return
Back to the Altar
Abram comes up from Egypt, wealthy, and journeys by stages back to Bethel — to "the place where he had made an altar at the first," and calls on the name of the LORD. After the Egypt detour, he returns to worship. Recovery begins at the altar.
13:5–7
Movement 2 — Strife
The Land Cannot Hold Them
Both men are so rich that the land can't sustain them together; their herdsmen quarrel. The narrator adds a pointed aside: "the Canaanites and the Perizzites were dwelling in the land" — the watching world sees the family of promise squabbling.
13:8–9
Movement 3 — Magnanimity
Abram Yields the Choice
"Let there be no strife… for we are kinsmen." Though Abram is the elder and holds the promise, he gives Lot first pick: "If you take the left hand, I will go right; if right, I will go left." He surrenders his rights for the sake of peace.
13:10–13
Movement 4 — Choosing by Sight
Lot Chooses the Plain
Lot "lifted up his eyes" and chose the well-watered Jordan Valley — "like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt." He takes the best and journeys east, pitching his tent "toward Sodom." The narrator warns: "the men of Sodom were wicked, great sinners against the LORD."
13:14–18
Movement 5 — The Promise Renewed
God Lifts Abram's Eyes
Only after Lot separates does God speak: "Lift up your eyes… all the land you see I will give you and your offspring forever," offspring "as the dust of the earth." "Arise, walk through the land." Abram moves to Hebron, to the oaks of Mamre, and builds an altar.
Two Men, Two Ways
Abram
Faith — receives by promise
Yields his right to choose, though he is the elder and greater
Seeks peace: "let there be no strife… we are kinsmen"
Trusts God with the outcome rather than grasping
God tells him to lift his eyes — and gives him the whole land
Builds altars; worships; "calls on the name of the LORD"
Lot
Sight — grabs by appearance
Takes first pick without hesitation or deference
Chooses by what looks best: "well watered… like Eden, like Egypt"
Journeys east and pitches his tent "toward Sodom"
Moves toward a place the narrator flags as wicked
No altar, no worship recorded — only a calculation of advantage
The irony runs deep: the one who let go of the choice received everything by promise; the one who seized the best chose, unknowingly, the road to ruin. Faith could afford to be generous because it trusted the God who gives; sight had to grab, because it trusted only what it could see.
The Key Motif — "Lift Up the Eyes" (nasa einayim)
The same Hebrew phrase, used of both men — in opposite directions
Genesis 13:10 · Lot
Lot lifted up his own eyes and chose for himself — his gaze fixed on what was fertile, prosperous, advantageous. He looked to take.
Genesis 13:14 · Abram
God tells Abram to lift up his eyes and receive a gift — the whole land, in every direction, forever. He looked to be given.
The narrator deliberately repeats the phrase. Lot lifts his eyes to seize the best portion for himself; God invites Abram to lift his eyes to receive a far greater portion as gift. What Lot grasped by sight was a fraction of what Abram was given by promise — and Lot's "best" turned out to sit next to Sodom. The lesson is not that seeing is wrong, but who directs the gaze and whether we grab or receive.
The Drift Toward Sodom — One Small Step at a Time
Genesis 13:12
Pitched tent toward Sodom
Not in it yet — just facing it, drawn by the view. Proximity chosen for advantage.
Genesis 14:12
Living in Sodom
By the next chapter he "was dwelling in Sodom" — captured when the city is raided. The tent moved inside.
Genesis 19:1
Sitting in the gate of Sodom
A civic leader of the city — fully embedded, on the eve of its destruction, barely escaping with his life.
The move toward Sodom is never a single leap — it is toward, then in, then of. The choice made "by the eyes" in Genesis 13 sets a trajectory that Genesis 19 completes.
Word Studies — Six Key Terms
נָשָׂא עֵינַיִם
nasa einayim — "lift up the eyes"
13:10, 14
To raise the eyes / look intently
The hinge phrase of the chapter, used of Lot (v.10, to grab) and of Abram at God's command (v.14, to receive). The same act of looking leads to opposite ends depending on whose direction it follows and whether it seizes or trusts.
רִיב
rîb — "strife / quarrel"
13:7, 8
Contention, dispute, lawsuit
The word for a legal or personal dispute. Prosperity, not poverty, triggered it — abundance strained the relationship. Abram's response ("let there be no rîb… we are kinsmen") makes the relationship worth more than the rights at stake.
פָּרַד
pārad — "to separate"
13:9, 11, 14
To divide, part company
Repeated three times. The separation is real and permanent. Notably, God speaks the renewed promise to Abram only "after Lot had separated" (v.14) — the letting-go clears the way for the gift.
כִּכָּר
kikkār — "the plain / valley"
13:10–12
A round / circle (of the Jordan)
The lush "circle" of the Jordan that Lot chose — described as "like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt." Two loaded comparisons: it looked like Eden, but it was also like Egypt, the place Abram had just left in disgrace.
עָפָר
ʿāphār — "dust"
13:16
Dust of the earth
God promises offspring "as the dust of the earth" — innumerable, and tied to the very land Abram is walking. The one who gave up a patch of ground is promised descendants as countless as its dust. Sight measures acres; faith is given the dust.
מִזְבֵּחַ
mizbēaḥ — "altar"
13:4, 18
Altar / place of sacrifice
The chapter is bracketed by altars — Abram returns to one at Bethel (v.4) and builds another at Hebron (v.18). Worship frames his whole story; Lot's story has none. The man of faith is marked by altars; the man of sight, by real estate.
Across the Canon — Where This Story Points
Genesis 12:1–3, 7
The Promise First Given
The land-and-offspring promise of ch.12 is what makes Abram's generosity possible: he can afford to give Lot first choice because the land is already promised to him by God, not won by grabbing. Faith rests the future on God's word, not on the best acreage.
Genesis 19:1–29
Where Lot's Choice Ends
The city Lot pitched toward is destroyed by fire; he escapes with almost nothing, his wife lost looking back. The narrator's warning in 13:13 pays off tragically. The "best" ground by sight became the site of judgment.
2 Peter 2:7–8
"Righteous Lot," Vexed
The NT calls Lot "righteous," tormented daily by Sodom's lawlessness — a caution against reading him as simply wicked. He was a believer who made a sight-driven choice and spent years grieved by its consequences. Compromise can trap even the righteous.
Hebrews 11:8–10 · Psalm 16:5–6
Looking for the City With Foundations
Abram lived in tents, "looking forward to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God." His eyes were set beyond the visible land. "The LORD is my chosen portion… the lines have fallen for me in pleasant places" (Ps 16) — faith's contentment with God as the portion.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Did Lot sin in choosing the plain, or was it just unwise?
The text never says "Lot sinned" at the moment of choosing — he took a fair offer. But the narrator surrounds the choice with warning signals: he "lifted up his own eyes" and "chose for himself," he moved "toward Sodom," and immediately we're told "the men of Sodom were wicked." The critique is less about a single sinful act than about a way of deciding: by appearance and self-interest, with no reference to God, no altar, no seeking the LORD. That method set a trajectory (13:12 → 14:12 → 19:1) that ended in disaster. Unwise and self-serving rather than flagrantly sinful — but the two are not far apart when God is left out of the looking.
2. Was Abram's generosity naïve — giving away the best land?
Not naïve, but faith-grounded. Abram could yield the choice precisely because he believed the whole land was already promised to him by God (12:7). He wasn't gambling; he was trusting. Faith made him free from the need to compete — he didn't have to secure his future by grabbing, because his future was secured by God's word. And the outcome vindicates him: God gives him "all the land you can see," far more than the patch Lot seized. Generosity rooted in God's promise is not loss; it is freedom.
3. Is it wrong to choose the "well-watered" option — to seek prosperity?
The point is not that fertile land is bad or that prudence is sin. Abram was himself wealthy, and God's promise includes land and blessing. The issue is the basis and direction of the choice: Lot chose purely by what looked best to the eye, for himself, with no regard for the spiritual environment ("toward Sodom") or for God. The same phrase — "lift up the eyes" — is holy when God directs it (v.14) and perilous when self-interest does (v.10). Prosperity sought as a gift received from God differs entirely from prosperity grabbed by sight regardless of cost.
4. Why does God renew the promise only after Lot leaves (v.14)?
The timing is deliberate and pastoral. Abram had just surrendered his right to choose, seemingly settling for whatever Lot left — and it is exactly then, "after Lot had separated," that God says "lift up your eyes… all this is yours." The letting-go did not cost Abram the promise; it cleared the stage for God to confirm it more expansively than ever. It models a repeated biblical pattern: those who release their grip on their own advantage for God's sake find God meeting them with more than they gave up (cf. Matt 19:29; Mark 10:29–30). God honours the open hand.
5. How does "toward Sodom" become a warning for us?
Lot never chose Sodom's sin — he chose its real estate, and pitched his tent "toward" it for the view and the pasture. But proximity chosen for advantage becomes residence, and residence becomes identity: toward (13:12), in (14:12), of — sitting in the gate (19:1). The drift is gradual and each step seems small and reasonable. The warning is not against living near sinners (we're called to be salt and light) but against choosing our environment purely by material advantage while ignoring its spiritual pull — and then being surprised when the pull wins.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — Two Herdsmen and a Land
Two wealthy relatives, one strip of land too small for both, and a choice to make. One trusted the promise of God enough to hand the decision away; the other trusted his own eyes and took the greenest ground — never guessing that its horizon held Sodom. The chapter quietly measures a life not by what it grabbed but by whether it worshipped and trusted.
Now — Our Own Choices by Sight
We still choose jobs, houses, relationships, and opportunities by "lifting up our eyes" to what looks best, most watered, most advantageous — often with no reference to God and little thought to the spiritual pull of the place we're moving toward. Genesis 13 asks two questions of every such choice: Am I grabbing or receiving? And what am I pitching my tent toward?
Question to sit with: In my next big decision, will I choose by sight — grabbing what looks best for me — or by faith, holding my rights loosely and trusting God with the outcome?
Controlling Idea
"Faith can afford to let go, because it trusts the God who gives; sight has to grab, because it trusts only what it can see. Abram surrendered the choice and was given the whole land; Lot seized the best and pitched his tent toward Sodom."
