Pull the Thorns
Luke 8:14 — The Thorny Soil in the Parable of the Sower
Deep Study
⊕ Faith Rooted in Scripture
The Verse in Focus
Luke 8:14 · with the parable (8:5–8) and its meaning (8:11, 15)
"And as for what fell among the thorns, they are those who hear, but as they go on their way they are choked by the cares and riches and pleasures of life, and their fruit does not mature."… "The seed is the word of God."… "As for that in the good soil, they are those who, hearing the word, hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patience."
Luke 8:14, 11, 15 (ESV)
The setting: Jesus tells the Parable of the Sower to great crowds (8:4), then explains it privately to his disciples. One seed — "the word of God" (8:11) — falls on four kinds of ground, and the soils are four kinds of hearers. The path, the rock, and the thorns each fail in a different way; only the good soil bears fruit. Our focus is the third soil, the thorns (8:14), because it is the most deceptively dangerous. Here the word is genuinely received and even begins to grow — this is no hardened path or shallow rock. But three competing growths — cares, riches, and pleasures — are allowed to grow up alongside it, and slowly strangle it, so that the plant lives but never ripens to mature fruit. The call the image presses is unmistakable: pull the thorns — clear the ground of what is choking the word — before your fruitfulness is quietly suffocated.
The Four Soils — Four Ways the Word Meets a Heart
v.12 · Path
Snatched away
The word never penetrates; the devil takes it away before it can be believed. Rejection.
v.13 · Rock
No root
Received with joy, but shallow. In the time of testing it withers and falls away. Collapse.
v.14 · Thorns ←
Choked
Received and growing — then strangled by cares, riches, pleasures. Lives, but never ripens. Suffocation.
v.15 · Good soil
Fruitful
An honest, good heart holds the word fast and bears fruit with patience. Harvest.
The thorny soil is the most sobering because it looks most like success — the word is heard, welcomed, growing. Nothing dramatic goes wrong; the plant is simply crowded to death by things that are not, in themselves, evil.
How the Choking Happens — Slowly, "As They Go on Their Way"
The word is not attacked head-on — it is out-competed
1 · Received
The word is genuinely heard and takes root. Real growth begins.
2 · Company
Thorns grow up with it (Mark 4:7) — cares, wealth, desires, given room in the same soil.
3 · Crowded
The thorns compete for light, water, room. Nothing is renounced; the word is simply squeezed.
4 · Choked
Strangled (sympnigō). The plant survives but the fruit "does not mature" (telesphoreō).
Luke's phrase is haunting: they are choked "as they go on their way" — not in a moment of crisis but over the ordinary course of daily life. There is no single decision to abandon the word; it dies of neglect while attention goes elsewhere. That is why thorns must be actively pulled: left alone, they always win. Fruitfulness is not the absence of thorns but the ongoing work of clearing them.
The Three Thorns — Named So We Can Pull Them (8:14)
μέριμναι
merimnai
The Cares of Life
Anxiety, worry, the endless pressure of responsibilities, money troubles, health, family, the future. Not luxury but the weight of ordinary life that fills the mind and crowds out the word. The same word Jesus uses in "do not be anxious" (Matt 6:25).
Pull it: "cast all your anxieties on him" (1 Pet 5:7); pray it out (Phil 4:6); seek first the kingdom and trust the Father who feeds the birds (Matt 6:33).
πλοῦτος
ploutos
The Riches of Life
Wealth and the pursuit of it — Mark adds "the deceitfulness of riches" (4:19). Money promises security and satisfaction it cannot give, and quietly reorders a life around getting and keeping until there is no room for the word.
Pull it: hold money loosely and give it away (1 Tim 6:17–19); refuse to serve two masters (Luke 16:13); store treasure in heaven where it can't be choked (Matt 6:19–21).
ἡδοναί
hēdonai
The Pleasures of Life
Comforts, entertainments, appetites, the endless small gratifications that fill our time and affections. Not sins so much as good things loved too much — pleasures given the hours and the heart-space the word needed.
Pull it: deny yourself and take up the cross daily (Luke 9:23); make no provision for the flesh's desires (Rom 13:14); find a deeper pleasure in Christ (Ps 16:11).
Notice what the three thorns have in common: none of them is obviously wicked. Cares, money, and pleasure are ordinary parts of a normal human life — that is precisely what makes them lethal. The path is defeated by the devil and the rock by persecution; but the thorns are just life itself, allowed to grow unchecked. The most common way to lose your fruitfulness is not scandal or apostasy but a slow crowding-out by good things given too much room. Weeds don't need to be planted; they only need to be left alone.
Word Studies — The Language of Choking & Fruit
ἄκανθαι
akanthai — "thorns"
8:7, 14
Thorns, thistles, brambles
The same word-world as Eden's curse: "thorns and thistles it shall bring forth" (Gen 3:18). Thorns are the native growth of fallen ground — they need no cultivation. Left to itself, the human heart grows them by default.
συμπνίγω
sympnigō — "choke"
8:14 (cf. 8:42)
To choke, strangle, crush together
A violent word — to throttle or press the life out. Luke uses the same verb of the crowds "pressing in" on Jesus (8:42). The word is not gently outgrown; it is suffocated. The thorns are killers, however domestic they look.
μέριμνα
merimna — "care / anxiety"
8:14
Worry, a mind pulled apart by cares
The first thorn. The same word Jesus repeats six times in "do not be anxious" (Matt 6:25–34). Care becomes a thorn when it displaces trust — when the mind is so full of the burdens of life that the word cannot get its attention.
ἡδονή
hēdonē — "pleasure"
8:14
Pleasure, gratification (root of "hedonism")
The third thorn. Elsewhere in the NT it names sinful indulgence (Jas 4:1–3; Titus 3:3), but here it is the ordinary "pleasures of life" — good gifts that become weeds when they are loved more than the Giver and consume the heart's affection.
τελεσφορέω
telesphoreō — "bring to maturity"
8:14
To ripen / bring fruit to completion
The tragic verb: the thorny soil's "fruit does not telesphoreō" — does not ripen to maturity. It is not that there is no life, but that the life never comes to anything. A choked plant, an unfinished harvest, a faith that never matures into fruit.
ὑπομονή
hypomonē — "patience"
8:15
Patient endurance, steadfastness
The good soil bears fruit "with hypomonē." Fruit takes time and steadfast holding-fast — the opposite of the distracted drift that lets the thorns win. Fruitfulness is a long obedience: keep the word central, keep pulling the weeds, and endure.
Across Scripture — Thorns, Weeds & a Cleared Field
Genesis 3:17–18
Thorns — the Mark of the Curse
"Cursed is the ground… thorns and thistles it shall bring forth." Thorns are the emblem of the fall — what fallen ground grows on its own. The thorny heart isn't unusual; it's the default that grace must clear.
Matthew 6:19–34
Two of the Thorns, Named Again
Jesus' teaching on treasure (riches) and worry (cares) is the Sermon-on-the-Mount version of two thorns — and the antidote: "seek first the kingdom." The Sower diagnoses; the Sermon prescribes.
Luke 12:15–21 · 21:34
The Rich Fool & the Weighed-Down Heart
The man whose barns crowded out his soul; and the warning that hearts get "weighed down… with the cares of this life." Luke keeps returning to the thorns of wealth and worry.
Hebrews 6:7–8 · 12:1
Land That Bears Thorns · Lay It Aside
Ground that yields only thorns is "worthless… near to being cursed." And the positive command: "lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely" — the athlete's version of pulling the thorns.

Interpretive Pressure Points
1. Is the thorny-soil person saved, or were they never truly converted?
The parable does not resolve this cleanly, and it may not mean to. Its purpose is diagnostic, not to sort out the eternal status of borderline cases. What it insists on is that only fruit-bearing hearing is saving hearing (cf. 8:21, "my mother and brothers are those who hear the word and do it"). A life whose word is permanently choked shows no evidence of true reception — "by their fruit you will know them." Rather than using this to speculate about others, the honest response is self-examination: is the word bearing fruit in me, or being choked? The parable is a mirror before it is a map of categories.
2. Are riches and pleasures sinful in themselves, then?
No — and this is the crucial point. Money, comfort, work, family, and legitimate pleasures are God's good gifts (1 Tim 4:4; 6:17). They become thorns not by existing but by being given room to dominate the heart and crowd out the word. The problem is not the presence of these things but the proportion — when the cares of life get more of your mind than God's word, when wealth reorders your priorities, when pleasures consume the time and affection the word needed. The soil doesn't fail because thorns exist; it fails because the thorns are never pulled.
3. Is worry a sin?
Jesus treats anxiety as something to be repented of and released, not merely a personality trait — "do not be anxious" is a command (Matt 6:25), and here worry is a thorn that kills fruit. Yet Scripture also speaks of a legitimate concern and care (Paul's "daily pressure… anxiety for all the churches," 2 Cor 11:28; "casting your care," 1 Pet 5:7 assumes we have cares). The line is between godly concern that drives us to God in prayer and trust, and corrosive anxiety that displaces God and chokes the word. The thorn is the second kind — worry that has become a rival to faith.
4. How do I "pull the thorns" without becoming ascetic or legalistic?
Pulling thorns is not renouncing all money, comfort, and responsibility — that would be a different error (Col 2:20–23 warns against self-made asceticism that has "no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh"). It is keeping the word central and everything else subordinate: giving God's word the prime time and attention, holding possessions loosely and giving generously, saying no to pleasures that are crowding out prayer and obedience, and repeatedly casting cares on God. The goal is not a bare field but a cleared one — the word given room to grow to maturity. And it is ongoing: weeds regrow, so weeding is a lifelong discipline, done in grace, not a one-time purge done in guilt.
5. Why does the same word bear fruit in one heart and choke in another?
The seed is identical and good in every case; the difference is the soil — the condition of the heart. The good soil is "honest and good" and "holds fast" (8:15), and it bears fruit "with patience." The parable therefore lays responsibility on the hearer to become good ground: to receive the word honestly, to hold it, to guard it, and to keep the competing growths cut back. Ultimately God himself must break up the fallow ground (Hos 10:12; Ezek 36:26 — the new heart), so this is not raw self-effort; but the means he uses include our vigilant, prayerful weeding. Grace makes the ground good; grace-empowered attentiveness keeps it clear.

Then → Now Bridge
Then — A Farmer's Field
Everyone in Jesus' audience knew that thorns grow whether you plant them or not, faster than the crop, and that a field left unweeded yields nothing. The image needed no explanation: good seed, real growth, and a harvest lost to neglect. The danger they'd all seen in the soil, Jesus turns into a picture of the human heart.
Now — The Peril of the Comfortable
We are perhaps the most thorn-rich generation ever — endless cares amplified by our screens, wealth and its pursuit assumed as the goal of life, and pleasures on demand at every idle moment. Few of us will fall away by persecution; far more will simply let the word be crowded out by a busy, prosperous, entertained life. The thorns don't need our help — only our permission.
Question to sit with: If I named the one thorn most choking the word in me right now — a care, a pursuit of money, or a pleasure — what would it be, and what is one concrete way I could pull it this week?
